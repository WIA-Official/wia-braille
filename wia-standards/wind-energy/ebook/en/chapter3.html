CHAPTER 3

Wind Resource Assessment

Measuring and Forecasting the Power of Wind

Introduction to Wind Resource Assessment

Wind resource assessment is the foundation of successful wind energy project development. Before investing millions or billions of dollars in a wind farm, developers must thoroughly understand the wind characteristics at a potential site. This process combines meteorological science, statistical analysis, and sophisticated modeling to predict long-term wind patterns and estimate energy production with high confidence.

A comprehensive wind resource assessment answers critical questions: How much wind is available at the site? How does wind speed and direction vary throughout the day and across seasons? What is the wind shear profile—how wind speed changes with height? What are the turbulence characteristics? These answers directly determine the economic viability of a wind energy project.

Understanding Wind Fundamentals

What Causes Wind?

Wind is fundamentally caused by differential solar heating of the Earth's surface. When sunlight strikes the Earth, different surfaces absorb heat at different rates. Land heats and cools more quickly than water, dark surfaces absorb more energy than light surfaces, and equatorial regions receive more direct sunlight than polar regions. These temperature differences create pressure gradients in the atmosphere, and air flows from high-pressure to low-pressure regions—creating wind.

At the global scale, prevailing wind patterns are shaped by the Earth's rotation (the Coriolis effect), the distribution of land and ocean, and large-scale atmospheric circulation patterns. The trade winds, westerlies, and polar easterlies represent these global circulation patterns. Understanding these large-scale patterns helps identify promising regions for wind energy development.

The Atmospheric Boundary Layer

Wind turbines operate within the atmospheric boundary layer—the lowest portion of the atmosphere directly influenced by the Earth's surface. This layer typically extends from the surface to heights of 1-2 kilometers and exhibits complex behavior due to surface friction, thermal effects, and topographic influences.

Surface friction causes wind speed to increase with height above the ground, a phenomenon called wind shear. The rate of increase depends on surface roughness—rough terrain with forests and buildings causes stronger shear than smooth surfaces like water or flat plains. Understanding wind shear is critical for wind turbine design and energy production estimates, as rotor-swept areas span significant vertical heights where wind speeds may vary considerably.

Wind Measurement Techniques

Meteorological Towers

Traditional wind resource assessment relies on meteorological towers (met towers or met masts) equipped with anemometers and wind vanes at multiple heights. These towers, typically 50-120 meters tall, provide direct measurements of wind speed and direction over extended periods (usually 1-3 years minimum).

Modern met towers incorporate sophisticated instrumentation including cup anemometers for wind speed measurement, wind vanes for direction, temperature and pressure sensors for air density determination, and sometimes additional sensors for solar radiation, precipitation, and icing detection. Data is recorded at high frequency (typically 10-minute averages) to capture both mean conditions and turbulence characteristics.

Key Measurement Parameters

  • Wind Speed: Measured in meters per second (m/s) or kilometers per hour (km/h)
  • Wind Direction: Compass bearing from which wind originates (degrees)
  • Air Temperature: Affects air density and thus available wind power
  • Atmospheric Pressure: Also influences air density calculations
  • Turbulence Intensity: Standard deviation of wind speed divided by mean wind speed
  • Wind Shear: Rate of change of wind speed with height

Remote Sensing: LiDAR and SODAR

Remote sensing technologies have revolutionized wind resource assessment, offering alternatives or supplements to traditional met towers. Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) and Sound Detection and Ranging (SODAR) systems can measure wind characteristics at multiple heights without requiring tall physical structures.

LiDAR systems emit laser pulses into the atmosphere and analyze the backscattered light from aerosol particles moving with the wind. By measuring the Doppler shift of the returned signal, LiDAR calculates wind speed at various distances from the device. Modern wind energy LiDAR can measure wind profiles from ground level to 300 meters or higher with excellent accuracy.

SODAR operates on similar principles but uses acoustic waves instead of light. SODAR systems are typically less expensive than LiDAR but may have reduced performance in noisy environments or extreme weather conditions. Both technologies offer significant advantages: no need for tall towers, ability to measure at multiple heights simultaneously, mobility for rapid deployment, and reduced permitting requirements compared to traditional met masts.

Satellite-Based Assessment

Satellite remote sensing provides valuable data for preliminary wind resource screening, especially for offshore sites. Scatterometers on polar-orbiting satellites measure ocean surface roughness, from which near-surface wind speeds can be inferred. While satellite data cannot replace ground-based measurements for final project assessment, it effectively identifies promising regions for more detailed investigation.

Data Analysis and Statistics

Wind Speed Distributions

Measured wind data must be statistically analyzed to characterize the wind resource and support energy production estimates. The Weibull distribution is most commonly used to describe wind speed frequency distributions. This two-parameter distribution (defined by scale parameter A and shape parameter k) effectively captures the characteristics of wind speed at most locations.

The Weibull distribution allows analysts to calculate important metrics from limited data: mean wind speed, most probable wind speed, wind speed at which maximum energy occurs, and the percentage of time wind speeds fall within specific ranges. These statistics directly inform turbine selection and energy production modeling.

Wind Rose and Directional Analysis

Wind direction significantly impacts wind farm design and energy production. Wind rose diagrams provide visual representations of wind frequency and speed distributions by direction. A well-designed wind rose reveals prevailing wind directions, identifies sectors with highest energy potential, and helps optimize turbine layout to minimize wake losses.

Directional analysis also informs micro-siting decisions. Turbines should ideally be positioned to maximize exposure to prevailing winds while avoiding terrain features that cause flow separation or extreme turbulence. Detailed directional analysis may reveal that certain sectors should be avoided even if they have strong winds, due to excessive turbulence that would reduce turbine lifespan or availability.

Temporal Variability

Wind resources vary on multiple timescales: diurnal (daily), seasonal, and inter-annual. Understanding these patterns is essential for grid integration planning and energy production forecasting.

Diurnal variations often show distinct patterns: coastal sites may experience stronger afternoon winds as land heats up, while inland sites might have stronger nighttime winds. Seasonal patterns vary by location—some regions have stronger winter winds, others peak in spring or summer. These patterns affect the value of wind energy, as high-demand periods may or may not align with high-production periods.

Inter-annual variability presents one of the greatest uncertainties in wind resource assessment. Even with multi-year measurement campaigns, the specific years measured may not represent true long-term average conditions. Measure-correlate-predict (MCP) techniques address this by correlating on-site measurements with long-term reference data from nearby weather stations or reanalysis datasets, adjusting short-term site measurements to estimated long-term average conditions.

Wind Flow Modeling

Numerical Weather Prediction Models

Global and regional numerical weather prediction (NWP) models simulate atmospheric conditions using fundamental physical equations. Reanalysis datasets, which assimilate historical weather observations into consistent NWP simulations, provide valuable long-term wind resource information. Modern reanalysis datasets like ERA5 offer 40+ years of hourly wind data globally at horizontal resolutions of 25-30 km.

While too coarse for site-specific assessment, these datasets help establish long-term average conditions, identify inter-annual variability, and support MCP analyses. They're particularly valuable for offshore sites where historical observations are sparse.

Mesoscale Modeling

Mesoscale atmospheric models simulate regional wind patterns at higher resolutions (1-10 km) than global models. The Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model is widely used in wind energy for downscaling coarser data to region-specific wind resource maps. These models incorporate terrain effects, land-use patterns, and local climatology to produce detailed wind resource estimates.

Mesoscale modeling is particularly valuable in complex terrain where simple extrapolation from distant measurement sites would be unreliable. However, even mesoscale models have limitations in capturing very local effects, necessitating further microscale modeling for final turbine siting.

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)

For the final stage of wind resource assessment—determining wind conditions at specific turbine locations—computational fluid dynamics provides detailed modeling at scales of meters to kilometers. CFD solves the Navier-Stokes equations governing fluid flow, simulating how wind interacts with terrain features, forests, buildings, and other obstacles.

Modern wind energy CFD combines Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) approaches for steady-state flow patterns with Large Eddy Simulation (LES) for detailed turbulence characterization. These simulations inform turbine micro-siting, wake modeling, and turbulence assessment, ensuring turbines are positioned to maximize energy capture while minimizing loads and wake interference.

Energy Production Estimation

The ultimate goal of wind resource assessment is estimating long-term energy production. This process combines wind resource characterization, turbine performance curves, wake losses, availability assumptions, and environmental corrections to produce an Annual Energy Production (AEP) estimate with associated uncertainty bounds.

Power Curve Application

Each wind turbine model has a certified power curve showing electrical output as a function of wind speed at hub height under standard conditions (15°C temperature, 101.325 kPa pressure). The power curve typically shows zero output below cut-in wind speed (usually 3-4 m/s), increasing power from cut-in to rated wind speed (where maximum power is reached), constant power from rated to cut-out wind speed, and zero power above cut-out speed (typically 25 m/s) where the turbine shuts down for protection.

Applying the power curve to site wind speed distributions yields gross energy production estimates. However, numerous corrections are necessary for realistic AEP predictions.

Losses and Adjustments

Wake losses occur when upstream turbines extract energy from the wind, reducing wind speeds and increasing turbulence for downstream turbines. Wake effects depend on turbine spacing, layout, wind direction frequencies, and atmospheric stability. Sophisticated wake models (like the Park model, Frandsen model, or CFD-based approaches) estimate these losses, typically 5-15% for well-designed wind farms.

Additional losses include electrical losses in cables and transformers (1-3%), turbine availability (accounting for downtime due to maintenance and failures, typically 95-98%), environmental factors (blade icing, high wind hysteresis, density corrections), and curtailment (forced reduction in production due to grid constraints or environmental restrictions). Conservative projects may also include generic uncertainty factors.

Uncertainty Quantification

Every AEP estimate includes uncertainty, typically expressed as a P50 value (median expected production) and P90 value (production expected to be exceeded 90% of the time). Uncertainty sources include measurement uncertainty, model uncertainty, inter-annual variability, and future climate uncertainty. Rigorous uncertainty quantification combines these factors probabilistically, providing project financiers with confidence intervals essential for investment decisions.

Offshore Wind Resource Assessment

Offshore sites present unique challenges and opportunities for wind resource assessment. Offshore wind resources are typically stronger and less turbulent than onshore sites, with excellent spatial uniformity. However, measurement and modeling are more challenging and expensive.

Floating LiDAR mounted on buoys has become the preferred measurement technique for many offshore projects, avoiding the extreme costs of offshore met masts. Satellite scatterometry provides valuable long-term reference data. Mesoscale modeling is essential given the absence of nearby weather stations in many offshore regions.

Offshore assessments must also consider marine-specific factors: salt spray effects on instruments, wave-induced motion of measurement platforms, marine boundary layer characteristics, and the possibility of low-level jets that aren't captured by standard wind profiles.

Conclusion

Wind resource assessment combines sophisticated measurement technologies, statistical analysis, and numerical modeling to characterize wind patterns and predict energy production. As wind energy projects grow larger and more capital-intensive, the importance of accurate resource assessment only increases. Modern assessment techniques, supported by improving models and longer-term datasets, enable confident predictions that support multi-billion-dollar investment decisions, advancing the deployment of clean wind energy worldwide.
