CHAPTER 6

Project Development and Economics

From Concept to Commercial Operation

The Wind Project Development Process

Developing a wind energy project is a complex, multi-year process involving technical analysis, regulatory approvals, community engagement, and financial structuring. Understanding this development lifecycle is essential for anyone involved in the wind energy industry, whether as developers, investors, regulators, or community stakeholders.

A typical wind farm development timeline spans 4-7 years from initial site identification to commercial operation, though timelines vary significantly by region and project specifics. Offshore projects typically require even longer development periods, often 7-10 years, due to additional technical complexity and regulatory requirements.

Site Selection and Preliminary Assessment

Identifying Potential Sites

Wind project development begins with identifying locations having favorable wind resources, suitable land characteristics, and grid connection potential. Desktop studies utilizing wind resource databases, satellite imagery, and geographic information systems (GIS) help developers screen large regions to identify promising areas.

Key site selection criteria include wind resource quality (generally minimum average wind speeds of 6-7 m/s at hub height for economic viability), land availability and acquisition costs, proximity to transmission infrastructure, accessibility for construction and operations, and absence of fatal flaws such as critical habitats, aviation conflicts, or cultural heritage sites. Each criterion involves trade-offs—the windiest sites may be remote with poor transmission access, while sites near existing infrastructure may have moderate wind resources.

Securing Land Rights

Once potential sites are identified, developers must secure rights to use the land. For private land, this typically involves negotiating lease agreements with landowners, offering annual payments (often $3,000-$8,000 per turbine per year, or percentage of revenues) for the 25-30 year project lifespan. Lease negotiations must address compensation, liability, decommissioning requirements, and landowner rights regarding other land uses.

For public lands, developers typically apply for permits through government agencies, potentially through competitive auction processes. Offshore developments require seabed leases from national authorities, with some countries allocating offshore areas through competitive auctions that can generate billions of dollars in upfront payments for prime locations.

Detailed Site Assessment

Wind Resource Measurement

Securing land rights enables installation of meteorological measurement equipment. As discussed in Chapter 3, comprehensive wind resource assessment typically involves 1-3 years of on-site measurements using met towers or remote sensing equipment. This extended measurement period captures seasonal and inter-annual variations essential for accurate energy production estimates.

The investment in wind resource measurement is substantial—met tower installations can cost $100,000-$500,000 depending on height and instrumentation, while LiDAR units cost $200,000-$400,000 plus operational expenses. However, this investment is essential for reducing uncertainty in energy production estimates, directly impacting project bankability and financing costs.

Environmental and Technical Studies

Environmental impact assessments (EIAs) examine potential project effects on wildlife, habitats, water resources, noise, visual impacts, and cultural heritage. These studies typically span 12-24 months, involving baseline surveys of bird and bat populations, noise modeling, visual impact simulations, and archaeological assessments. EIA requirements vary significantly by jurisdiction, with some regions requiring minimal assessment while others demand comprehensive multi-year studies.

Technical studies address engineering feasibility and project design. Geotechnical investigations characterize soil conditions for foundations. Electrical studies examine grid connection options and upgrade requirements. Transportation studies verify component delivery routes. Shadow flicker modeling predicts when rotating blades will cast moving shadows on nearby residences. Ice throw analysis (in cold climates) maps potential hazards from ice shedding from blades. Each study informs project design and identifies potential constraints requiring mitigation.

Permitting and Approvals

Navigating regulatory approvals represents one of the most time-consuming and uncertain phases of project development. Wind projects typically require numerous permits and approvals from multiple governmental levels and agencies.

Key Permits and Approvals

Planning permission or zoning approvals from local authorities represent the primary regulatory hurdle in most jurisdictions. These approvals often require public hearings where community members and stakeholders can voice support or opposition. Building permits authorize physical construction. Environmental permits address impacts on protected species, water quality, and other environmental considerations. Aviation permits ensure the project doesn't create hazards for aircraft operations. Grid connection agreements from transmission system operators authorize connection to the electrical grid.

For offshore projects, additional approvals cover seabed disturbance, navigation safety, fishing impacts, and maritime traffic management. The overlapping jurisdictions of coastal areas—national governments, regional authorities, environmental agencies, maritime agencies—create complex regulatory landscapes requiring expert navigation.

Community Engagement

Successful wind projects require community support or at least acceptance. Developers invest significant effort in community engagement, conducting public meetings, establishing stakeholder committees, and addressing community concerns. Transparency regarding project details, realistic representations of visual impacts, and responsiveness to community input can build trust and facilitate approvals.

Community benefit schemes have become increasingly common, offering local communities direct benefits beyond tax revenues. These may include community ownership shares, local employment and training programs, community funds supporting local projects, or reduced electricity rates for nearby residents. Well-designed benefit schemes can transform community opposition into support while ensuring local areas share in project benefits.

Project Finance

Wind energy projects require substantial capital investment—typically $1,200-$2,200 per kilowatt for onshore projects and $3,000-$5,500 per kilowatt for offshore projects. Securing financing is thus critical and often determines whether projects proceed.

Financing Structures

Corporate finance involves developers funding projects from their own balance sheets, retaining full ownership and flexibility but bearing all risks and capital requirements. This approach suits large, established developers but is often impractical for smaller players or very large projects.

Project finance creates a special purpose vehicle (SPV)—a legally independent entity owning the project—and secures loans based solely on the project's projected cash flows rather than developer credit. Lenders receive security interests in all project assets and revenue streams. Project finance enables developers to leverage their equity, potentially funding multiple projects simultaneously, but requires detailed documentation, higher transaction costs, and typically demands proven technology and contracted revenues.

Tax equity partnerships in the United States attract investors motivated primarily by tax benefits (production tax credits or investment tax credits) rather than project cash flows. These complex structures split project ownership between tax equity investors (receiving tax benefits and early cash flows) and developers/strategic investors (receiving long-term cash flows). Tax equity markets are limited and specialized, with a small number of institutions providing most tax equity capital.

Revenue Contracts

Long-term revenue certainty dramatically improves project bankability and reduces financing costs. Several contract structures provide this certainty:

Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) are long-term contracts (typically 10-25 years) where an off-taker (utility, corporate buyer, or government entity) agrees to purchase project electricity at specified prices. PPAs transfer price risk from the project to the off-taker, enabling lower financing costs. Corporate PPAs, where companies like Google, Amazon, or industrial firms contract directly with wind projects, have become increasingly common.

Feed-in tariffs (FiTs) offer guaranteed prices for renewable electricity, typically for 15-20 years. FiTs were instrumental in driving early wind deployment in countries like Germany and Spain, offering simplicity and long-term certainty. However, many regions have transitioned away from FiTs toward competitive auctions or market-based mechanisms to reduce costs.

Contracts for Differences (CfDs) provide price floor protection while limiting upside. If market electricity prices fall below the CfD strike price, the counterparty pays the project the difference; if market prices exceed the strike price, the project pays back the excess. CfDs have become popular in European offshore wind markets, allocated through competitive auctions.

Cost Structure and Economics

Capital Costs

Wind project capital costs comprise several major categories. Turbine supply and installation typically represents 60-75% of total costs for onshore projects. Balance of system costs—foundations, electrical infrastructure, roads, buildings—account for 15-25%. Grid connection costs vary dramatically based on distance to transmission infrastructure, from minimal for well-sited projects to 20% or more for remote sites requiring extensive transmission upgrades. Development and project management costs, including wind resource assessment, environmental studies, permits, legal fees, and developer overhead, add 5-10%. Finally, financing costs during construction add several percentage points.

Offshore projects have different cost distributions. While turbines still represent the largest cost category (30-40% of total), offshore foundations, installation vessels, and submarine cables are much more expensive than onshore equivalents, collectively accounting for 40-50% of costs. These higher capital costs are partially offset by superior wind resources and larger turbines, yielding competitive energy costs despite higher upfront investments.

Operating Costs

Annual operating expenses for wind projects typically range from 1.5-3.0% of capital costs for onshore projects, somewhat higher for offshore. Major operating cost categories include scheduled and unscheduled maintenance, insurance, land lease payments, property taxes, grid connection charges, administration, and management. Offshore projects face higher O&M costs due to challenging access conditions and harsh marine environments, though improving technology and operational experience are reducing these costs.

Levelized Cost of Energy

The levelized cost of energy (LCOE) metric enables comparison of electricity generation costs across technologies with different cost structures and operational characteristics. LCOE represents the average price required per unit of electricity generated to recover all costs over the project lifetime, accounting for capital costs, operating costs, financing costs, and energy production.

Global weighted-average LCOE for onshore wind has fallen from approximately $100/MWh in 2010 to $35-45/MWh in 2025, making onshore wind one of the cheapest sources of new electricity generation globally. Offshore wind LCOE has declined even more dramatically, from $180/MWh in 2010 to $60-90/MWh in 2025, with the best projects achieving costs below $50/MWh. These cost reductions result from larger turbines, improved capacity factors, reduced financing costs, optimized operations, and economies of scale.

Risk Management

Wind projects face numerous risks that must be identified, quantified, and managed to ensure project success and protect stakeholder interests.

Development Risks

Development risks include permit denial, community opposition, and inability to secure viable grid connection terms. Developers manage these risks through careful site selection, proactive stakeholder engagement, regulatory expertise, and portfolio diversification across multiple projects. Development capital is typically equity rather than debt, acknowledging the high-risk nature of early-stage development.

Construction Risks

Construction risks encompass cost overruns, schedule delays, and performance shortfalls. Fixed-price turnkey EPC (engineering, procurement, construction) contracts transfer most construction risks to contractors. Performance guarantees ensure turbines meet specified output levels. Liquidated damages clauses penalize contractors for delays. Insurance covers major construction incidents. Rigorous contractor selection and project management minimize these risks.

Operational Risks

Once operational, projects face technology performance risks (turbines may underperform or fail), resource risks (actual wind may differ from predictions), and price risks (electricity or subsidy prices may change). Long-term turbine warranties (typically 5-20 years) mitigate technology risks. Conservative energy assessments with P90 production estimates (production levels expected to be exceeded 90% of the time) provide downside protection against resource risk. Long-term revenue contracts eliminate price risk during contract periods.

Project Decommissioning

Wind projects have finite lifespans, typically 25-30 years, after which they must be decommissioned or repowered. Decommissioning involves dismantling turbines, removing foundations (though some foundation elements may be left in place if environmentally acceptable), and restoring sites to original conditions or agreed end states.

Financial assurance for decommissioning is increasingly required by regulators and landowners. Developers may establish decommissioning funds during project operations or provide bonding or letters of credit to ensure resources are available for proper site restoration. Repowering—replacing old turbines with new, more efficient models—represents an alternative to full decommissioning, potentially extending site use while dramatically increasing energy production using the same land and infrastructure.

Conclusion

Wind project development is a sophisticated process requiring expertise across technical, environmental, regulatory, financial, and community engagement domains. Success demands patience, capital, risk tolerance, and skill in navigating complex stakeholder landscapes. As the wind industry matures, development processes become more streamlined, but challenges remain—particularly in securing social license, grid connections, and favorable policy frameworks. Nevertheless, improving economics and growing climate urgency drive continued wind deployment, creating opportunities for skilled developers to deliver clean energy projects that benefit communities, investors, and the environment.
