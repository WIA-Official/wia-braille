CHAPTER 04

CFC and HCFC Phase-out Technologies

4.1 Refrigeration and Air Conditioning Sector

Refrigeration and air conditioning represents the largest application sector for CFCs and HCFCs, accounting for approximately 70-80% of historical ODS consumption. The transition to ozone-safe alternatives in this sector has been one of the most significant technological transformations in modern industrial history.

4.1.1 Domestic Refrigeration

Household refrigerators and freezers historically used CFC-12 (R-12) as refrigerant and CFC-11/HCFC-141b for foam insulation. The phase-out required comprehensive redesign:

Component Old Technology (ODS) New Technology (ODS-free) Performance Impact
Refrigerant CFC-12 (R-12) HFC-134a, R-600a (isobutane), R-290 (propane) Equal or better efficiency
Compressor CFC-compatible Redesigned for new refrigerants, improved efficiency 10-30% efficiency gain
Lubricant Mineral oil POE (polyolester) for HFCs, mineral oil for hydrocarbons Compatible performance
Foam Insulation CFC-11, HCFC-141b Cyclopentane, HFC-245fa, CO₂, water-blown Comparable insulation value
Safety Systems Not required Enhanced for flammable refrigerants (hydrocarbons) Added safety features
Hydrocarbon Refrigerants: R-600a Success Story

Isobutane (R-600a) has become the dominant refrigerant in new domestic refrigerators globally:

  • Market Share: >60% of new refrigerators worldwide use R-600a
  • ODP: 0 (zero ozone depletion)
  • GWP: 3 (negligible climate impact)
  • Energy Efficiency: 5-15% better than HFC-134a systems
  • Charge Size: 40-50g (vs. 150g for R-12) - reduced refrigerant inventory
  • Safety: Flammable but extremely low charge makes risk minimal with proper design
  • Economics: Lower cost than HFCs, no patent restrictions
  • Environmental Profile: Near-zero direct emissions impact, natural substance

4.1.3 Air Conditioning Systems

Air conditioning encompasses diverse applications from small room units to large industrial chillers, each requiring tailored phase-out approaches.

AC Type Typical Capacity Old Refrigerant Primary Alternatives Status (2024)
Room AC/Small Split 1-10 kW R-22 (HCFC-22) R-32, R-410A, R-290 90%+ transitioned
VRF/Multi-Split 10-100 kW R-22, R-407C R-32, R-410A, R-454B 80%+ transitioned
Packaged Rooftop 50-500 kW R-22 R-410A, R-454B, R-32 75%+ transitioned
Chillers (Centrifugal) 500-5000+ kW CFC-11, CFC-12, R-123 R-134a, R-513A, R-1233zd(E) 95%+ transitioned
Chillers (Screw) 100-2000 kW R-22 R-134a, R-515A, NH₃ 85%+ transitioned
Mobile AC 5-15 kW CFC-12, R-134a R-1234yf, R-152a, CO₂ 50%+ transitioned
R-32: Next-Generation AC Refrigerant

Difluoromethane (R-32) is rapidly becoming the global standard for residential and light commercial air conditioning:

  • ODP: 0 (ozone-safe)
  • GWP: 675 (68% lower than R-410A's 2,088)
  • Efficiency: 5-10% better than R-410A in most applications
  • Charge Reduction: 20-30% less refrigerant required vs. R-410A
  • Simplified Chemistry: Single component (not a blend), easier recycling
  • Global Adoption: Mandated in Japan, India, dominant in Asia, growing in Europe
  • Safety: A2L classification (mildly flammable, low toxicity) - requires updated standards
  • Performance: Comparable cooling capacity, slightly higher discharge temperature
  • Economics: Cost-competitive with R-410A, no significant price premium
  • Market Penetration: >70% of new residential AC units in Asia-Pacific region

4.2 Foam Blowing Agents

CFCs (primarily CFC-11 and CFC-12) and HCFCs (mainly HCFC-141b and HCFC-142b) were extensively used as blowing agents in foam manufacturing. The phase-out required development of new foaming technologies across multiple foam types.

4.2.1 Rigid Polyurethane Foam

Rigid PU foam is critical for thermal insulation in refrigerators, freezers, buildings, and industrial applications. The transition from ODS blowing agents presented significant technical challenges.

4.2.2 Flexible Polyurethane Foam

Flexible PU foam (furniture, mattresses, automotive seating) historically used auxiliary blowing agents (mainly CFC-11, later HCFC-22) in addition to water/CO₂. The phase-out was more straightforward than rigid foam:

  • Primary Solution: 100% water-blown (chemical blowing with CO₂ generation)
  • Advantages: No ODP, no GWP, cost-effective, no performance loss
  • Process Adjustments: Modified formulations, catalyst systems, processing equipment
  • Status: >99% of flexible foam production now water-blown globally
  • Quality: Equal or superior to ODS-blown foam
  • Co-benefits: Simplified production, eliminated ODS handling/storage

4.2.3 Extruded Polystyrene (XPS) Foam

XPS foam boards for building insulation historically used CFC-12, then HCFC-142b. The transition required significant process modifications:

4.3 Aerosol Propellants

Aerosol products were among the first applications to phase out CFCs, beginning with voluntary action in the late 1970s before the Montreal Protocol even existed. The transition demonstrated that ODS elimination was technically feasible and economically viable.

4.3.1 Aerosol Alternatives

Alternative Type Environmental Profile Applications Market Share
Hydrocarbons Propane, butane, isobutane blends ODP: 0, GWP: ~10 Personal care, household products ~60% globally
DME Dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃) ODP: 0, GWP: ~1 Personal care, specialty products ~15%
Compressed Gases CO₂, N₂, compressed air ODP: 0, GWP: 0-1 Food products, technical sprays ~10%
HFC-152a 1,1-Difluoroethane ODP: 0, GWP: 138 Personal care, dust removers ~8%
HFO-1234ze(E) Trans-1,3,3,3-tetrafluoroprop-1-ene ODP: 0, GWP: <1 Medical aerosols, specialty ~2%
Pump Sprays Mechanical pump systems No propellant needed Many household/personal care ~5% (not counted in propellant share)
Aerosol Phase-out: Early Success

The aerosol sector's rapid transition provided crucial lessons for other applications:

  • Timeline: USA, Canada, Scandinavia banned CFC aerosols 1978-1979
  • Speed: 85% reduction in CFC aerosol use achieved in just 5 years
  • Consumer Acceptance: Alternative products performed equally well
  • Economic Impact: Minimal disruption, some cost savings
  • Innovation Driver: Spurred development of improved formulations
  • Global Model: Demonstrated that rapid ODS phase-out was possible
  • Current Status: >99.9% of aerosols now ODS-free worldwide

4.3.2 Metered Dose Inhalers (MDIs)

Medical aerosols, particularly metered dose inhalers for asthma and COPD treatment, received essential use exemptions due to lack of suitable alternatives. The transition took longer but ultimately succeeded:

  • Original Propellants: CFC-11, CFC-12, CFC-114
  • Exemption Period: 1996-2010s (phased transition)
  • Alternative Developed: HFA-134a (1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane), HFA-227ea
  • Performance: Equal or better drug delivery efficiency
  • Patient Impact: Reformulation required relearning inhaler technique
  • Current Status: >95% of MDIs now HFA-based globally
  • Next Generation: HFO-1234ze(E) under development (ultra-low GWP)
  • Alternative Delivery: Dry powder inhalers (no propellant) gaining market share

4.4 Solvents and Cleaning Agents

ODS solvents were widely used for precision cleaning in electronics, metals, and other industries due to excellent solvency properties, low toxicity, and non-flammability.

4.4.1 Solvent Applications and Alternatives

Application ODS Used Primary Alternatives Transition Challenges
Electronics Cleaning CFC-113, methyl chloroform Aqueous cleaning, semi-aqueous, no-clean fluxes, HFE solvents Process redesign, validation
Metal Degreasing CFC-113, methyl chloroform, HCFC-141b Aqueous alkaline, hydrocarbons, brominated solvents, vapor degreasing with HFO Wastewater treatment, flammability
Precision Cleaning CFC-113 HFE-7100, nPB, modified alcohols Material compatibility
Dry Cleaning CFC-113 Perchloroethylene (being phased out), wet cleaning, hydrocarbon, GreenEarth® Different process parameters
Defluxing (PCBs) CFC-113 No-clean fluxes (eliminate cleaning), aqueous, semi-aqueous Manufacturing process changes
No-Clean Technology: Elimination Through Innovation

One of the most successful solvent alternatives was to eliminate the need for cleaning entirely:

  • No-Clean Flux: Flux residues designed to be benign, no removal required
  • Adoption: Now used in >60% of electronics assembly
  • Benefits: Zero solvent use, lower cost, higher throughput
  • Performance: Equal or better product reliability
  • Environmental: Eliminated solvent emissions AND waste streams
  • Energy: Reduced energy consumption (no cleaning/drying steps)
  • Innovation Driver: Forced industry to rethink manufacturing processes
  • Broader Impact: Demonstrated that prevention often superior to substitution

4.5.2 Halon Banking and Recycling

Given the criticality of some halon applications and limited alternatives initially, halon banking systems were established:

  • Concept: Collect halon from decommissioned systems, clean/recycle for critical uses
  • Global Network: Halon banks operating in 50+ countries
  • Inventory: ~100,000+ tonnes available globally for recycling
  • Critical Uses: Aviation, military, essential applications where alternatives insufficient
  • Decline: Stockpile gradually decreasing as alternatives improve and systems retire
  • Environmental Benefit: Prevents emission of banked halons (high ODP: 3-10)
  • Sunset: Most civilian applications converted to alternatives; remaining use primarily aviation

The successful phase-out of CFCs and HCFCs across these diverse applications demonstrates that environmental protection and technological progress can advance together. The lessons learned continue to inform efforts to address climate change through HFC phase-down under the Kigali Amendment.
