Airlock Architecture

Cylindrical module (1.0 m diameter, 1.5 m length) accommodates 2 persons + 50 kg luggage with 60-second full cycle time. Outer door (80 cm, 200 kg, hydraulic) and inner door (80 cm, 180 kg, electric) feature interlocking mechanism preventing simultaneous opening—critical safety preventing rapid decompression.

Pressure Equalization

Gradual pressurization/depressurization at 10 kPa/min rate (comfortable for human Eustachian tube adaptation) takes 60 seconds typical, enabling pressure difference <0.1 kPa before door unlock. Four pressure sensors provide redundant measurement with ±0.01 kPa precision.

UV-C sterilization (254 nm, 60 seconds, 99.9% kill) optional.

Pressure Equalization Physiology

The human Eustachian tube—connecting middle ear cavity to nasopharynx—equalizes pressure across the tympanic membrane (eardrum). Rapid pressure changes exceed the tube's natural equalization rate, causing barotrauma: negative pressure in middle ear (during decompression) pulls tympanic membrane inward, creating pain, reduced hearing, and potential membrane rupture; positive pressure (during compression) pushes membrane outward with similar consequences. The Eustachian tube opens passively during swallowing and yawning via tensor veli palatini muscle contraction, but this voluntary mechanism has limited rate capability. The 10 kPa/min specified rate (equivalent to 0.167 kPa/second or approximately 100 meters/minute altitude change in atmosphere) represents comfortable equalization matching typical aircraft cabin pressurization rates (8000 ft cabin altitude at 300 ft/min rate of climb/descent). Faster rates—while technically feasible—risk barotrauma in passengers unable to equalize through voluntary swallowing. The standard specifies this rate based on NASA research for space suit pressure changes and aviation medicine studies of safe decompression/recompression profiles. Pressure sensor precision (±0.01 kPa or ±0.1 millibar) enables smooth control avoiding oscillations that could cause repetitive stress on Eustachian tubes.

Timeline Verification Complexity

Verifying arrival in the correct timeline involves profound theoretical challenges. Unlike spatial navigation where GPS coordinates unambiguously identify location, temporal coordinates (date/time) alone don't guarantee timeline identity—multiple parallel timelines may share identical apparent dates. The chronosphere must verify timeline through multiple independent checks: quantum timeline markers (unique quantum entanglement signatures assigned at departure, verified via Bell inequality measurements at arrival—entanglement breaking indicates timeline branch), historical consistency database (comparing observed astronomical configurations, geological formations, archaeological remains against predicted values for specific timeline), physical constants stability (measuring fine structure constant α = e²/(4πε₀ℏc) ≈ 1/137.036, gravitational constant G, speed of light c—variations indicate different physics regime possibly from timeline branching), and causality loop detection (checking temporal navigation log for closed timelike curves that would indicate paradoxical timeline). The >99% stability requirement means temporal field fluctuations remain below threshold that could cause spontaneous timeline branching during measurement period. Historical consistency checking against predicted values uses Bayesian probability: P(correct timeline | observations) = P(observations | correct timeline) × P(correct timeline) / P(observations). For each verified match (star position, radiocarbon date, geological stratum), posterior probability increases; contradictions decrease probability, potentially triggering abort if confidence drops below threshold.
