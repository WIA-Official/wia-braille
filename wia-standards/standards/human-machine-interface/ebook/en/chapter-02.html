Chapter 2

Signal Types and Neural Encoding

Deep dive into biological signal acquisition, encoding formats, and compression methods

2.1 Understanding Biological Signals

The human body generates a symphony of electrical signals that encode our intentions, sensations, and physiological state. Understanding these signals is fundamental to building effective human-machine interfaces. Unlike digital signals with discrete values, biological signals are analog, noisy, and highly variable between individuals and even within the same person over time.

WIA-AUG-014 defines five primary signal categories, each with unique characteristics that influence how they must be acquired, processed, and interpreted. The choice of signal type dramatically affects system design, from electrode placement to sampling rates to decoding algorithms.

2.2 Type A: Neural Signals

Neural signals represent the electrical activity of neurons - the fundamental information processing units of the nervous system. These signals range from population-level activity measurable through the scalp to individual neuron spikes that require invasive recording.

A1: Electroencephalography (EEG)

EEG captures electrical activity from the brain through electrodes placed on the scalp. It's the least invasive neural recording method, making it ideal for consumer applications and research where surgery isn't justified. However, the skull significantly attenuates and blurs the signal.

EEG Technical Parameters

Parameter Value Notes
Channels 8-256 More channels = better spatial resolution
Sample Rate 250-1000 Hz 512 Hz typical for most applications
Resolution 16-24 bit Higher bits = better SNR
Bandwidth 0.1-100 Hz Different frequencies for different applications
Amplitude 10-100 μV Very small signals requiring amplification

EEG signals are typically categorized by frequency bands, each associated with different mental states and cognitive processes:

  • Delta (0.5-4 Hz): Deep sleep, unconsciousness
  • Theta (4-8 Hz): Drowsiness, meditation, creativity
  • Alpha (8-13 Hz): Relaxed wakefulness, eyes closed
  • Beta (13-30 Hz): Active thinking, focus, anxiety
  • Gamma (30-100 Hz): Higher cognitive function, binding

A2: Electrocorticography (ECoG)

ECoG places electrodes directly on the brain's cortical surface, providing dramatically better signal quality and spatial resolution than EEG. The signals aren't filtered through skull and scalp, resulting in higher amplitude, broader bandwidth, and lower noise. However, this requires neurosurgery and carries associated risks.

ECoG vs EEG Comparison

Aspect ECoG EEG
Invasiveness Requires craniotomy Non-invasive
Signal Amplitude 50-1000 μV 10-100 μV
Spatial Resolution ~3mm ~3cm
Bandwidth 0.1-500 Hz 0.1-100 Hz
Sample Rate 1-30 kHz 250-1000 Hz
Longevity Years (chronic implants) Unlimited (external)

A3: Local Field Potential (LFP)

LFPs represent the summed electrical activity of many neurons near an electrode. Recorded from inside the brain tissue, they provide excellent spatial and temporal resolution for understanding local neural processing. LFPs are primarily used in research and advanced BCIs.

A4: Single-Unit Activity

The highest resolution neural recording captures individual neurons firing action potentials (spikes). This requires microelectrodes positioned near specific neurons and sophisticated spike-sorting algorithms to isolate individual neuron signals from the background. While extremely informative, single-unit recording is technically challenging and currently limited to research applications.

2.3 Type B: Muscular Signals

Electromyography (EMG) measures the electrical activity of muscles during contraction. Unlike neural signals that require accessing the brain or nerves, EMG can be recorded from the skin surface or within muscles, making it practical for prosthetic control.

B1: Surface EMG (sEMG)

Surface electrodes placed on the skin detect the sum of electrical activity from underlying muscle fibers. sEMG is non-invasive, easy to use, and sufficient for many prosthetic control applications. However, signal quality depends heavily on electrode placement, skin preparation, and muscle depth. 

Surface EMG Signal Processing Pipeline:

Raw Signal (50-500 Hz) → Amplification (1000-10000x) → Filtering (bandpass 20-450 Hz)
         ↓
    Rectification → Smoothing (50-200ms window) → Feature Extraction
         ↓
    Classification → Motor Command → Device Control

B2: Intramuscular EMG

Fine wire or needle electrodes inserted into muscles provide higher signal quality and spatial selectivity than surface recording. This allows recording from deep muscles and distinguishing between closely-spaced muscles, enabling more sophisticated control strategies. The invasiveness limits long-term use.

B3: Mechanomyography (MMG)

MMG measures muscle activity mechanically rather than electrically - detecting the vibrations and dimensional changes of contracting muscle fibers. Accelerometers or microphones placed on the skin capture these mechanical signals. MMG is immune to electrical interference and doesn't require skin preparation, though it's less commonly used than EMG.

2.4 Type C: Biometric Signals

Biometric signals provide context about the user's physiological and emotional state, enabling adaptive systems that respond to stress, fatigue, or arousal levels.

Signal Type Measurement HMI Application
C1: ECG Heart electrical activity Stress detection, arousal level, health monitoring
C2: GSR Skin conductance Emotional state, cognitive load assessment
C3: Respiration Breathing rate/depth Relaxation state, exertion level
C4: Temperature Body temperature Thermal comfort, inflammation detection

2.5 Type D: Motion Signals

Inertial measurement units (IMUs) combine accelerometers, gyroscopes, and magnetometers to track movement and orientation. These signals are crucial for prosthetics, exoskeletons, and augmented reality applications.

IMU Sensor Specifications

  • D1 Accelerometer: Measures linear acceleration in 3 axes (±2g to ±16g range typical)
  • D2 Gyroscope: Measures angular velocity (±250°/s to ±2000°/s range typical)
  • D3 Magnetometer: Measures magnetic field for absolute orientation
  • D4 Pressure/Force: Force-sensitive resistors or load cells at contact points

Modern IMUs sample at 50-1000 Hz with 16-bit resolution, enabling precise motion tracking with latency under 5ms. Sensor fusion algorithms combine data from all sensors to estimate orientation, position, and velocity with high accuracy despite individual sensor drift and noise.

2.6 Signal Encoding Format

WIA-AUG-014 defines a standardized packet format for encoding all signal types, ensuring interoperability between devices and systems. The format balances efficiency with extensibility. 

interface SignalPacket {
  header: {
    version: number;           // Protocol version (1-255)
    signalType: SignalType;    // A1-E4 enumeration
    timestamp: bigint;         // Microseconds since epoch
    channelCount: number;      // Number of channels
    sampleCount: number;       // Samples per channel
    resolution: number;        // Bits per sample (8/16/24/32)
  };

  metadata: {
    deviceId: string;          // Source device UUID
    sessionId: string;         // Current session UUID
    sequenceNumber: number;    // Packet sequence (overflow wraps)
    quality: number;           // Signal quality 0-100
  };

  payload: {
    samples: Int32Array;       // Interleaved channel data
    markers: EventMarker[];    // Synchronization markers
    checksum: number;          // CRC-32 checksum
  };
}

interface EventMarker {
  timestamp: bigint;           // When event occurred
  type: string;                // Event type identifier
  value?: number;              // Optional event value
}

Data Interleaving

For multi-channel signals, samples are interleaved rather than blocked: [Ch1_S1, Ch2_S1, Ch3_S1, Ch1_S2, Ch2_S2, Ch3_S2, ...]. This organization enables:

  • Efficient temporal alignment across channels
  • Streaming processing with minimal buffering
  • Reduced latency for real-time applications
  • Simple indexing: sample = data[sampleIndex * channelCount + channelIndex]

2.7 Compression Methods

Biological signals often contain redundancy that can be exploited for compression, reducing bandwidth requirements and storage costs. WIA-AUG-014 defines three compression methods with different tradeoffs.

Method 1: Delta Encoding

Delta encoding stores the difference between consecutive samples rather than absolute values. Since biological signals typically change slowly relative to sampling rate, deltas are smaller and can be represented with fewer bits. 

Original:  [1000, 1002, 1005, 1003, 1007, 1010]
Deltas:    [1000,    2,    3,   -2,    4,    3]
Bits:      16-bit original → 8-bit deltas (2x compression)

Advantages: Simple, low latency (<1ms), works well for slowly-varying signals like EMG baseline
Disadvantages: Error propagation if packet lost, poor compression for rapidly-changing signals
Compression Ratio: 2-4x typical

Method 2: Wavelet Compression

Wavelet transform decomposes signals into frequency components at multiple scales. Low-amplitude high-frequency components (often noise) can be discarded or quantized aggressively while preserving signal features.

Advantages: High compression ratios, preserves important signal features, adaptable to signal characteristics
Disadvantages: Higher latency (2-5ms), computationally intensive, lossy compression
Compression Ratio: 5-10x typical

Method 3: Lossless (LZ4)

LZ4 is a general-purpose lossless compression algorithm optimized for speed. It identifies repeated patterns in data and encodes them efficiently. Perfect for applications requiring bit-exact reconstruction.

Advantages: Lossless, very low latency (<0.5ms), guaranteed reconstruction
Disadvantages: Lower compression ratios than lossy methods
Compression Ratio: 1.5-2x typical

Application Recommended Method Rationale
Motor control (Tier 1) Delta or None Latency critical, bandwidth usually acceptable
EEG analysis Wavelet High channel count, tolerance for small distortion
Clinical recording Lossless (LZ4) Must preserve all signal information
Research data Lossless (LZ4) Enable future reanalysis with new methods

2.8 Signal Quality Metrics

Assessing signal quality is essential for reliable HMI operation. WIA-AUG-014 requires continuous quality monitoring and reporting.

Required Quality Metrics

  • Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR): Power ratio of signal to noise, measured in dB. Target >40 dB for reliable decoding.
  • Channel Correlation: Cross-correlation between channels. High correlation (>0.7) may indicate electrode bridging or poor placement.
  • Baseline Stability: Variance in signal baseline over time. Excessive drift indicates electrode issues or movement artifacts.
  • Artifact Rate: Frequency of detected artifacts (spikes, saturation, disconnection). Target <5 per minute.
  • Impedance (for biopotential signals): Electrode-skin impedance. Higher impedance increases noise. Target <10 kΩ for EEG, <50 kΩ for EMG.

Key Takeaways

  • WIA-AUG-014 defines five signal categories (A-E) with specific subcategories for different recording modalities
  • Neural signals range from non-invasive EEG to invasive single-unit recording, trading invasiveness for signal quality
  • Surface EMG is the most practical muscle signal for prosthetic control, balancing ease-of-use with information content
  • Signal encoding uses interleaved multi-channel format with timestamps, metadata, and error checking
  • Three compression methods (Delta, Wavelet, LZ4) serve different applications based on latency, quality, and bandwidth requirements
  • Continuous quality monitoring ensures reliable operation and alerts to electrode or system problems
  • Signal parameters (sample rate, resolution, bandwidth) must match application requirements and sensor capabilities
