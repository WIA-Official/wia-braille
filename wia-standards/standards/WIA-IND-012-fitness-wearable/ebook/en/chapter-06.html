GPS and global navigation satellite systems enable precise outdoor activity tracking essential for runners, cyclists, hikers, and outdoor enthusiasts. 6.1 Satellite Navigation Fundamentals

Global Positioning System (GPS) and other global navigation satellite systems (GNSS) revolutionized navigation, surveying, and countless applications including fitness tracking. Understanding the technology's principles, capabilities, and limitations ensures appropriate use and interpretation of location data. Satellite navigation represents one of the most sophisticated and globally accessible technologies, embodying 弘益人間 through universal availability regardless of geographic location or economic status.

Trilateration Principles

GNSS positioning relies on trilateration—determining position based on distances to known reference points (satellites). Each satellite continuously broadcasts precise timing signals synchronized by onboard atomic clocks. Receivers measure signal transit time from multiple satellites, calculating distance to each satellite (time × speed of light). With distances to at least four satellites and knowing satellite positions (broadcast in navigation messages), receivers solve geometric equations determining three-dimensional position and synchronizing the receiver clock. This elegant solution provides worldwide positioning without ground-based infrastructure.

GPS Constellation Architecture

The US GPS constellation comprises 31 active satellites in six orbital planes at approximately 20,200 km altitude, completing orbits every 12 hours. This configuration ensures that 6-12 satellites are visible from any location worldwide at any time, providing redundancy for accurate positioning. Satellites transmit signals on multiple frequency bands—L1 (1575.42 MHz) used by most consumer receivers, and L2/L5 frequencies offering improved accuracy and interference resistance for dual-frequency receivers increasingly common in high-end fitness wearables.

Alternative GNSS Constellations

Multiple satellite navigation systems provide global coverage with combined benefits exceeding any single system: GLONASS (Russia) operates 24 satellites providing global coverage particularly strong in high northern latitudes, Galileo (European Union) features 30 satellites with advanced signals offering improved accuracy, BeiDou (China) provides 35 satellites with global coverage and regional augmentation, and regional systems like QZSS (Japan) and NAVIC (India) supplement global systems with enhanced performance in specific geographic areas. Modern fitness wearables support multi-constellation GNSS, accessing 50+ satellites for superior accuracy and reliability in challenging environments.

6.2 GNSS Accuracy and Error Sources

GPS accuracy varies substantially based on environmental conditions, receiver quality, and satellite geometry. Understanding error sources enables accuracy optimization and appropriate metric interpretation. WIA-IND-012 establishes accuracy reporting standards ensuring transparent performance communication.

Atmospheric Effects

Satellite signals traverse the ionosphere (60-1000 km altitude) and troposphere (0-50 km) before reaching receivers. Both atmospheric layers refract signals, delaying transmission and introducing errors. Ionospheric delay varies with solar activity, time of day, and location—typically 5-15 meters in mid-latitudes but potentially exceeding 50 meters near the equator during high solar activity. Dual-frequency receivers can measure and correct ionospheric delay with high precision. Single-frequency receivers use model-based corrections achieving partial compensation. Tropospheric delay depends on temperature, pressure, and humidity—approximately 2-25 meters depending on satellite elevation angle. Advanced correction models reduce tropospheric errors but cannot eliminate them entirely.

Satellite Geometry and DOP

Satellite geometry—the spatial distribution of visible satellites relative to the receiver—profoundly affects positioning accuracy. Optimal geometry features widely distributed satellites across the sky, while poor geometry occurs when visible satellites cluster in one region. Dilution of Precision (DOP) metrics quantify geometry effects: Position DOP (PDOP) indicates overall three-dimensional positioning accuracy, Horizontal DOP (HDOP) addresses two-dimensional accuracy particularly relevant for ground-based navigation, and Vertical DOP (VDOP) reflects altitude accuracy typically worse than horizontal accuracy. Low DOP values (1-2) indicate excellent geometry and sub-meter accuracy potential, while high values (>6) suggest degraded accuracy potentially exceeding 20 meters. WIA-IND-012 requires recording DOP values with position data, enabling quality assessment and filtering.

Multipath and Urban Canyon Effects

Satellite signals reflecting off buildings, terrain, or vehicles before reaching the receiver create multipath interference—the receiver detects both direct and reflected signals, introducing positioning errors. Urban environments with tall buildings ("urban canyons") suffer severe multipath, often degrading accuracy to 10-50 meters and sometimes causing complete position loss. Modern receivers employ sophisticated signal processing to detect and reject multipath, but complete elimination remains impossible. Understanding these limitations manages user expectations for urban running or cycling activities where GPS accuracy degrades substantially compared to open areas.

6.3 Assisted GPS and Fast Acquisition

Traditional GPS receivers require 30-60 seconds for initial position acquisition—downloading satellite orbit data (ephemeris) and completing geometric calculations. Assisted GPS (A-GPS) dramatically reduces acquisition time to seconds, improving user experience particularly for brief outdoor activities.

A-GPS Architecture

A-GPS leverages cellular or Wi-Fi connectivity to download satellite orbit predictions, approximate position (from cell tower databases), and current time from internet servers. This auxiliary data eliminates the need for receivers to download ephemeris from satellites—a slow process—enabling rapid signal acquisition and positioning. Cold start acquisition (no prior satellite data) reduces from 30-60 seconds to 5-15 seconds. Warm start (recent but outdated ephemeris) improves from 15-30 seconds to 1-5 seconds. Hot start (current ephemeris and approximate position) achieves sub-second acquisition.

Extended Ephemeris

Extended ephemeris data provides satellite orbit predictions for several days or weeks, stored in receiver memory. Combined with accurate real-time clock, receivers can determine approximate satellite positions without internet connectivity or satellite downloads. This capability enables hot start performance even after extended periods without GPS use, particularly valuable for wearables that may sit unused for days between outdoor activities.

6.4 Power Management for GPS

GPS represents the dominant power consumer in fitness wearables during outdoor activities, dramatically reducing battery life from multiple days to 6-20 hours depending on usage patterns and optimization strategies. Sophisticated power management balances tracking accuracy with battery longevity.

Adaptive Sampling Strategies

During steady-state movement (constant speed and direction), frequent position updates provide minimal additional information. Adaptive algorithms reduce GPS sampling from continuous (1 Hz) to periodic (0.1-0.2 Hz) during steady movement, resuming higher rates when accelerometer detects speed or direction changes. This strategy can extend battery life 50-100% while maintaining route accuracy. Dead reckoning—estimating position from previous position, speed, and direction—fills gaps between GPS fixes using accelerometer and compass data, producing smooth tracks despite intermittent satellite reception.

Context-Aware GPS Control

Intelligent GPS management detects activity context and adjusts accordingly: indoor detection (using Wi-Fi, cellular, or sustained GPS failure) automatically disables GPS, conserving power when satellite signals are unavailable. Stationary detection (minimal accelerometer activity) reduces update frequency or temporarily suspends GPS. Low battery conditions trigger aggressive power saving, reducing GPS accuracy but extending operational time. These context-aware strategies embody user-centric design, optimizing the power-accuracy trade-off for different scenarios.

6.5 Route Tracking and Mapping

GPS enables detailed route visualization, enabling users to review paths taken, share routes with others, and navigate previously traveled paths. Route tracking applications span recreational fitness to competitive racing and navigation.

Track Smoothing and Filtering

Raw GPS tracks exhibit noise—erratic position jumps due to signal interference, multipath, and satellite geometry changes. Track smoothing algorithms apply filtering to remove implausible positions while preserving route details: outlier detection identifies and removes positions physically impossible given speed and direction constraints, Kalman filtering optimally combines GPS measurements with motion model predictions, reducing noise while maintaining responsiveness to actual direction changes, and Douglas-Peucker algorithms simplify tracks while preserving critical waypoints, reducing file size for storage and transmission. WIA-IND-012 specifies standard track formats (GPX, TCX, FIT) ensuring interoperability across devices and applications.

Route Sharing and Navigation

Social fitness platforms enable route sharing, allowing users to discover popular running, cycling, or hiking paths. Route navigation provides turn-by-turn guidance following saved or downloaded routes. Breadcrumb navigation displays the current position relative to the intended route, alerting users when they deviate. These features democratize access to quality training routes, supporting the 弘益人間 principle by connecting global fitness communities.

6.6 Elevation and Climb Metrics

Elevation data adds critical context to outdoor activities, particularly for cycling, hiking, and trail running where terrain significantly impacts effort and performance. GPS-derived elevation exhibits substantial noise, requiring fusion with barometric pressure sensors for accuracy.

GPS-Based Elevation

GPS vertical accuracy is approximately 1.5-2× worse than horizontal accuracy due to satellite geometry—satellites distribute widely in azimuth but cluster toward the horizon vertically. Typical vertical accuracy ranges from 5-15 meters in good conditions, degrading to 20-50 meters in challenging environments. This noise makes GPS-only elevation unreliable for climb metrics. Post-processing using high-resolution digital elevation models (DEM) improves accuracy by correcting GPS positions to known terrain elevations, though this requires internet connectivity and detailed terrain data coverage.

Barometric Elevation

Barometric pressure sensors provide superior short-term elevation accuracy (sub-meter resolution) compared to GPS. Pressure-altitude conversion requires calibration to account for weather-induced pressure changes—absolute pressure at given altitude varies with atmospheric conditions. Auto-calibration uses GPS elevation at activity start to establish reference pressure, enabling accurate relative elevation change detection throughout the activity. This approach achieves 1-3 meter elevation accuracy for climb/descent calculations, dramatically improving metric quality for hilly or mountainous routes.

Climb Categorization

Elevation profiles enable automatic climb detection and categorization. Metrics include total elevation gain (cumulative uphill vertical distance), maximum gradient (steepest sustained section), and climb categorization (Category 4 through HC in cycling nomenclature, based on elevation gain and gradient). These metrics provide objective difficulty quantification, enabling route comparison and training intensity management.

6.7 Distance and Pace Accuracy

Distance and pace—fundamental metrics for runners and cyclists—depend critically on GPS accuracy and processing algorithms. Understanding accuracy factors enables appropriate metric interpretation and validation.

Distance Calculation Methods

Total distance sums segment lengths between successive GPS positions. Two primary calculation approaches exist: great circle distance uses spherical geometry accounting for Earth's curvature, providing accurate results for long distances. Euclidean distance treats short segments as straight lines, introducing negligible error for typical GPS update intervals. Both approaches accumulate errors from GPS noise. Filters removing outliers and smoothing tracks improve distance accuracy, potentially reducing errors from 3-5% to 1-2% in good conditions.

Footpod and Accelerometer Calibration

Foot-mounted accelerometers (footpods) or wrist-based accelerometer algorithms estimate distance from step detection and stride length calculation, providing GPS-independent distance metrics useful indoors or when GPS is unavailable. Calibration against GPS-measured distances in outdoor activities improves accelerometer-based distance accuracy to within 2-3%. This hybrid approach combines GPS for outdoor activities with accelerometer fallback for tunnels, dense tree cover, or indoor running, ensuring continuous distance tracking.

6.8 Privacy Considerations

Location data reveals highly sensitive information about individuals' movements, routines, residences, and behaviors. WIA-IND-012 establishes comprehensive privacy requirements ensuring user control and data protection while enabling beneficial location-based features.

Privacy Zones and Data Redaction

Privacy zones enable users to define geographic areas (typically home and work locations) where GPS recording is automatically suspended or position data is obscured. When activities start or end within privacy zones, the actual start/end positions are hidden or replaced with approximate locations displaced by 200-500 meters. This prevents revealing precise home addresses while maintaining activity data utility. Social sharing features must respect privacy zone settings, ensuring inadvertent location disclosure doesn't occur through route visualization.

Aggregated Route Data

Platforms aggregating routes from many users to identify popular running or cycling paths must implement privacy protections: k-anonymity ensures no individual can be identified from aggregated data by requiring minimum user counts (k) before exposing popular segments. Differential privacy adds statistical noise preventing individual route reconstruction while preserving aggregate statistics. Temporal aggregation prevents real-time tracking by delaying route publication. These protections enable community benefits while safeguarding individual privacy, embodying 弘益人間 through ethical data usage.

6.9 Indoor Positioning Systems

GPS fails indoors where satellite signals cannot penetrate buildings. Alternative positioning technologies fill this gap for indoor fitness activities including treadmill running, indoor cycling, and gym workouts.

Wi-Fi and Bluetooth Positioning

Wi-Fi access points and Bluetooth beacons serve as indoor positioning reference points. Fingerprinting approaches build databases mapping signal strengths to locations, enabling position estimation from observed signals. Trilateration uses signal strength or time-of-flight measurements to calculate distances to multiple access points. Accuracy varies from 2-10 meters depending on environment and infrastructure density. While insufficient for detailed route tracking, indoor positioning provides valuable context including gym location, floor identification, and approximate activity area.

Accelerometer-Based Indoor Tracking

Pedestrian dead reckoning uses accelerometer step detection, compass heading, and step length estimation to track indoor movement from a known starting position. Cumulative errors cause drift over time, but for short-duration activities (gym workouts lasting 30-90 minutes), acceptable accuracy is achievable. Integration with building floor plans enables constraint-based corrections—positions must lie within building boundaries and on accessible floors, reducing drift and improving estimates.

6.10 Future GNSS Technologies

Next-generation GNSS technologies promise improved accuracy, reliability, and power efficiency, enhancing fitness tracking capabilities while maintaining the global accessibility central to the 弘益人間 philosophy.

Dual-Frequency Consumer Receivers

Dual-frequency receivers accessing L1 and L5 GPS signals (or equivalent on other constellations) enable ionospheric error correction, improving accuracy to sub-meter levels in open-sky conditions. Chip-scale atomic clocks provide stable timing references, enabling extended GPS autonomy and improved weak-signal performance. These technologies, once limited to survey-grade equipment costing thousands of dollars, are migrating to consumer wearables, democratizing precision positioning.

Real-Time Kinematic (RTK) Positioning

RTK positioning uses correction data from nearby base stations to achieve centimeter-level accuracy in real-time. Base stations broadcast correction signals via internet connectivity, eliminating most atmospheric and orbital errors. Consumer RTK services are emerging, potentially enabling fitness wearables to achieve professional survey accuracy for specialized applications like precise course measurement or performance analysis requiring millimeter-level position accuracy.
