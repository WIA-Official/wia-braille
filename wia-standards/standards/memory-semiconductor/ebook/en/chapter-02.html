Chapter 2: DRAM Technology and DDR Standards

Dynamic Random Access Memory (DRAM) has been the dominant main memory technology for over four decades. Its simple one-transistor, one-capacitor (1T1C) cell structure provides an optimal balance of density, speed, cost, and manufacturability. This chapter explores DRAM fundamentals, the evolution of DDR standards, and the advanced technologies driving modern memory systems.

2.1 DRAM Cell Structure and Operation

The DRAM cell is elegantly simple: a single access transistor connected to a storage capacitor. This minimal structure enables high density but introduces unique challenges.

2.1.1 The 1T1C Cell

Each DRAM cell consists of:

  • Access Transistor: NMOS transistor controlled by the wordline (row select)
  • Storage Capacitor: Stores charge representing a logic '1' or '0'
  • Bitline: Column line used to read/write data through the access transistor

When the wordline is activated (high voltage), the access transistor connects the storage capacitor to the bitline, allowing charge to be read or written. The stored charge is extremely small, typically 10-30 femtocoulombs (fC), requiring sensitive sense amplifiers for detection.

2.1.2 Capacitor Structures

As DRAM has scaled to smaller process nodes, maintaining sufficient capacitance has become increasingly challenging. Modern DRAM uses several advanced capacitor structures:

  • Trench Capacitors: Capacitor formed in a deep trench etched into the silicon substrate, maximizing surface area without increasing cell footprint
  • Stacked Capacitors: Cylindrical or fin-shaped structures built above the access transistor, using high-k dielectrics for increased capacitance
  • High-k Dielectrics: Materials like ZrO₂ or HfO₂ with dielectric constants 30-40x higher than SiO₂, enabling smaller physical dimensions

Modern DRAM at the 10nm class node achieves approximately 30 fF (femtofarad) capacitance using stacked capacitor structures with crown or cylindrical geometries.

2.1.3 Read and Write Operations

Read Operation

Reading from DRAM is a destructive process that requires careful sequencing:

  1. Precharge: Bitlines precharged to Vdd/2 (half supply voltage)
  2. Row Activation: Wordline driven high, connecting capacitor to bitline
  3. Charge Sharing: Capacitor charge shares with bitline, creating small voltage difference
  4. Sense Amplification: Sense amplifier detects and amplifies the voltage difference
  5. Column Selection: Column address selects specific bits to output
  6. Restoration: Sense amplifier restores full voltage to the capacitor

The voltage difference created during charge sharing is extremely small, typically 100-200mV. Differential sense amplifiers can reliably detect these small signals, amplifying them to full logic levels (0V or Vdd).

Write Operation

Writing follows a similar sequence but forces the bitline to the desired voltage before activation:

  1. Row Activation: Open the target row by activating its wordline
  2. Bitline Drive: Drive bitline to 0V (write '0') or Vdd (write '1')
  3. Charge Transfer: Capacitor charges/discharges through access transistor
  4. Wordline Deactivation: Close access transistor, isolating stored charge

2.1.4 Refresh Requirement

DRAM capacitors leak charge through several mechanisms:

  • Junction Leakage: Reverse-biased PN junction at capacitor/substrate interface
  • Gate Leakage: Tunneling current through access transistor gate oxide
  • Subthreshold Leakage: Weak inversion current when transistor is nominally off

To prevent data loss, every DRAM row must be refreshed periodically. JEDEC standards specify:

Standard Refresh Period Total Rows Refresh Commands/Period
DDR4 (Normal) 64 ms 8,192 - 65,536 8,192 (REF commands)
DDR4 (Extended) 32 ms Same 4,096
DDR5 (Normal) 32 ms 16,384 - 131,072 16,384

Refresh operations consume significant power and reduce available bandwidth. At higher temperatures, leakage increases, requiring more frequent refresh (2x or 4x normal rate above 85°C).

2.2 DRAM Organization and Architecture

2.2.1 Bank Structure

Modern DRAM is organized hierarchically to enable concurrent operations:

  • Cells: Individual 1T1C storage elements arranged in rows and columns
  • Arrays: Large matrices of cells (e.g., 512 rows × 1024 columns)
  • Subarrays: Arrays divided into independently operable sections
  • Banks: Collections of arrays that can be accessed independently
  • Bank Groups: (DDR4+) Groups of banks with additional timing constraints
  • Ranks: Sets of chips accessed simultaneously across a memory channel

DDR4 Bank Organization

DDR4 introduced bank groups to improve performance:

  • 16 Total Banks: Organized as 4 bank groups × 4 banks per group
  • Intra-Bank Group: Longer timing constraints (tCCD_L = 6 cycles typical)
  • Inter-Bank Group: Shorter constraints (tCCD_S = 4 cycles), enabling higher throughput
  • Independent Operation: Different bank groups can activate rows concurrently

DDR5 Enhancements

DDR5 doubles the bank structure for even better parallelism:

  • 32 Total Banks: 8 bank groups × 4 banks per group
  • Dual-Channel Architecture: Each DIMM module contains two independent 32-bit channels
  • Same Bank Refresh: Refresh one bank while accessing others
  • Improved Efficiency: Better utilization of available bandwidth

2.2.2 Addressing Scheme

DRAM addresses are multiplexed to reduce pin count. A typical DDR4/DDR5 addressing scheme:

  • Row Address: 16-18 bits, selects one of 65K-256K rows
  • Column Address: 10-11 bits, selects columns within the active row
  • Bank Address: 2 bits (4 banks per group)
  • Bank Group Address: 2-3 bits (4-8 bank groups)

The row and column addresses are sent sequentially on the same address pins, reducing package complexity but requiring multi-cycle addressing.

2.3 DDR Evolution: From DDR to DDR5

2.3.1 DDR (DDR1) - 2000

The original Double Data Rate SDRAM transferred data on both rising and falling clock edges:

  • Data Rate: 266-400 MT/s
  • Voltage: 2.5V
  • Prefetch: 2n (two data words per clock cycle)
  • Banks: 4 banks

2.3.2 DDR2 - 2003

DDR2 doubled the prefetch to increase data rates without increasing array access frequency:

  • Data Rate: 533-1066 MT/s
  • Voltage: 1.8V (28% power reduction)
  • Prefetch: 4n
  • Banks: 4 or 8 banks
  • ODT: On-die termination for improved signal integrity

2.3.3 DDR3 - 2007

DDR3 continued the trend of higher data rates and lower power:

  • Data Rate: 1066-2133 MT/s
  • Voltage: 1.5V (1.35V for DDR3L low-voltage variant)
  • Prefetch: 8n
  • Banks: 8 banks
  • Features: Write leveling, fly-by topology, reset pin

2.3.4 DDR4 - 2014

DDR4 introduced significant architectural changes:

  • Data Rate: 1600-3200 MT/s (officially), up to 4800+ MT/s (enthusiast)
  • Voltage: 1.2V (20% power reduction vs DDR3)
  • Prefetch: 8n (same as DDR3, but internal organization differs)
  • Banks: 16 banks (4 bank groups × 4 banks)
  • Capacity: Up to 64GB per module (using 3D stacking)
  • Features: Bank groups, improved refresh (REFab), DBI (Data Bus Inversion), CRC

DDR4 Timing Parameters

Parameter Description Typical Value (DDR4-3200)
tCL CAS Latency 14-22 cycles
tRCD RAS to CAS Delay 14-22 cycles
tRP Row Precharge Time 14-22 cycles
tRAS Row Active Time 32-52 cycles
tRC Row Cycle Time 46-74 cycles
tRFC Refresh Cycle Time 350-560 ns

2.3.5 DDR5 - 2020

DDR5 represents the most significant generational improvement in years:

  • Data Rate: 3200-8400 MT/s (officially), roadmap to 12800+ MT/s
  • Voltage: 1.1V (8% power reduction vs DDR4)
  • Prefetch: 16n (doubled from DDR4)
  • Banks: 32 banks (8 bank groups × 4 banks)
  • Capacity: Up to 128GB per module initially, 256GB+ roadmap

DDR5 Major Features

1. Dual-Channel Architecture: Each DDR5 DIMM contains two independent 32-bit channels, effectively doubling the number of channels in a system. What was previously a single 64-bit channel in DDR4 becomes two 32-bit channels in DDR5.

2. On-Module PMIC: Power Management IC integrated on the DIMM module provides better voltage regulation, reducing motherboard complexity and improving signal integrity.

3. Same Bank Refresh: Allows refresh operations on one bank while other banks remain available for read/write operations, reducing refresh overhead.

4. Enhanced Error Correction:

  • On-Die ECC: Each chip includes internal error correction
  • ODECC: Corrects single-bit errors transparently
  • Optional Sideband ECC: Additional system-level ECC for critical applications

5. Improved Power Management:

  • More granular power states
  • Better voltage regulation through on-module PMIC
  • Lower operating voltage (1.1V vs 1.2V)
  • Improved DBI (Data Bus Inversion) to reduce I/O power

DDR5 Timing Parameters

Parameter DDR5-4800 DDR5-6400 Notes
tCL 40 cycles 52 cycles Higher absolute but similar ns
tRCD 39 cycles 52 cycles ~16 ns typical
tRP 39 cycles 52 cycles ~16 ns typical
tRAS 76 cycles 104 cycles ~32 ns typical

Note: DDR5 timing values are higher in cycles but similar in absolute time (nanoseconds) due to higher clock frequencies. The key improvement is bandwidth, not latency reduction.

2.4 LPDDR: Low Power DDR for Mobile

LPDDR (Low Power DDR) is optimized for mobile devices where power efficiency is paramount:

2.4.1 LPDDR4/4X

  • Data Rate: 3200-4266 MT/s
  • Voltage: 1.1V (0.6V I/O)
  • Deep Sleep Modes: Sub-milliwatt power consumption when idle
  • Temperature Compensated Refresh: Adjusts refresh rate based on temperature

2.4.2 LPDDR5/5X

  • Data Rate: 6400-8533 MT/s (LPDDR5X)
  • Voltage: 1.05V (0.5V I/O)
  • WCK (Write Clock): Additional clock for write operations, improving timing margin
  • Multi-Frequency Support: Can switch between speed grades for power optimization
  • Advanced Sleep Modes: Even lower idle power than LPDDR4

LPDDR5X achieves up to 8533 MT/s, providing 68 GB/s bandwidth per 64-bit channel, sufficient for demanding mobile applications like 8K video and on-device AI processing.

2.5 Memory Channels and Configurations

2.5.1 Single vs. Multi-Channel

Memory bandwidth scales with the number of independent channels:

Configuration Channels Total Width Bandwidth (DDR4-3200)
Single-Channel 1 64-bit 25.6 GB/s
Dual-Channel 2 128-bit 51.2 GB/s
Quad-Channel 4 256-bit 102.4 GB/s
Eight-Channel 8 512-bit 204.8 GB/s

Server platforms typically use quad-channel or eight-channel configurations to provide sufficient bandwidth for multi-core processors. Consumer platforms generally use dual-channel.

2.5.2 Ranks and Organization

A rank is a set of DRAM chips that are accessed simultaneously:

  • Single-Rank (1R): One set of chips per channel
  • Dual-Rank (2R): Two sets of chips per channel, selected via chip select signal
  • Quad-Rank (4R): Four sets of chips (less common in consumer space)

Dual-rank configurations often provide better performance than single-rank at the same capacity, as the memory controller can parallelize operations across ranks. However, loading increases, potentially limiting maximum achievable clock frequencies.

Chapter Summary

  • DRAM uses a simple 1T1C cell structure: one access transistor and one storage capacitor per bit
  • Modern DRAM capacitors use advanced structures (trench, stacked) with high-k dielectrics to maintain sufficient capacitance at small process nodes
  • Reading DRAM is destructive; sense amplifiers detect tiny voltage differences and restore data
  • Refresh is required every 32-64ms to prevent data loss from capacitor leakage
  • DDR has evolved from 266 MT/s (DDR1) to 8400+ MT/s (DDR5), with each generation improving speed, capacity, and power efficiency
  • DDR4 introduced bank groups for improved parallelism; DDR5 doubled this to 32 banks
  • DDR5's major innovations include dual-channel architecture per module, on-module PMIC, same-bank refresh, and on-die ECC
  • LPDDR variants prioritize power efficiency for mobile applications, achieving sub-milliwatt idle power
  • Multi-channel configurations multiply available bandwidth; servers typically use 4-8 channels
  • Dual-rank modules often outperform single-rank at same capacity due to better parallelization
