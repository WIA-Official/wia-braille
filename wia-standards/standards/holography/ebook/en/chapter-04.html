Hologram Types & Recording Geometries

Understanding the different types of holograms, their unique recording geometries, viewing characteristics, and applications in various technological domains.

Introduction to Hologram Classification

Holograms can be classified in multiple ways based on their recording geometry, viewing requirements, physical characteristics, and application purposes. Understanding these classifications is essential for selecting the appropriate hologram type for specific applications. The fundamental distinction lies in how the reference and object beams are arranged during recording and how the hologram is illuminated during reconstruction.

The three primary geometrical classifications are transmission holograms (light passes through), reflection holograms (light reflects from), and volume holograms (thick media with 3D interference patterns). Additionally, specialized types like rainbow holograms and image-plane holograms serve specific purposes in display and security applications.

Transmission Holograms

Transmission holograms are recorded with the reference and object beams incident from the same side of the recording medium. During reconstruction, light passes through the hologram, and the diffracted beam emerges from the opposite side. This geometry was used by Dennis Gabor in the first holograms (1948) and remains important for many applications.

Recording Configuration

The basic transmission hologram recording setup involves:

  • Beam arrangement: Reference and object beams illuminate the recording medium from the same side
  • Angle range: Typically 15° to 60° between beams
  • Medium thickness: Usually thin media (5-20 μm) for simple transmission types
  • Fringe orientation: Fringes lie primarily in the plane of the hologram

Reconstruction Geometry

The reconstruction conditions for transmission holograms follow precise angular relationships:

θreconstruction = θrecording
λreconstruction = λrecording × (dafter / dbefore)

Where d represents the fringe spacing before and after processing (accounting for shrinkage/swelling).

Off-Axis vs. In-Line Transmission Holograms

Off-Axis (Leith-Upatnieks)

  • Reference beam at significant angle (30-60°)
  • Separates virtual and real images
  • Twin image elimination
  • High spatial frequencies required
  • Cleaner reconstruction
  • Most common type today

In-Line (Gabor)

  • Reference and object beams collinear
  • Virtual and real images overlap
  • Twin image problem
  • Low spatial frequencies
  • Historical importance
  • Limited modern use

Reflection Holograms

Reflection holograms represent one of the most important advances in display holography. Developed by Yuri Denisyuk in 1962, these holograms can be viewed in ordinary white light, making them practical for art, security, and commercial applications. The key innovation is recording with reference and object beams from opposite sides of the medium.

Denisyuk Configuration

In the Denisyuk recording geometry:

  • Reference beam illuminates the recording medium from one side
  • Object is positioned on the opposite side, illuminated by the transmitted reference beam
  • Reflected object beam interferes with reference beam inside the medium
  • Creates volume grating with fringes perpendicular to the surface

Bragg Diffraction and White-Light Viewing

The remarkable property of reflection holograms—white light viewability—arises from Bragg diffraction in the volume grating. The Bragg condition selectively reflects only wavelengths that satisfy:

λview = 2n × Λ × cos(θ)

Where:

  • n = refractive index of the recording medium (~1.5)
  • Λ = fringe spacing (typically 0.25-0.35 μm for visible light)
  • θ = internal angle between incident light and fringes

When illuminated with white light, only the wavelength matching the Bragg condition is reflected—typically a narrow spectral band (~20-50 nm). This wavelength selectivity acts as a spectral filter, allowing monochromatic reconstruction from polychromatic illumination.

Color Shift in Reflection Holograms

Processing-induced shrinkage affects the reconstructed color. If emulsion shrinks by 10%, fringe spacing decreases by 10%, and the Bragg wavelength shifts:

λfinal = λrecording × 0.9

A hologram recorded at 633 nm (red HeNe laser) might reconstruct at 570 nm (yellow-green) after processing. This is why reflection holograms often appear greenish when recorded with red lasers—the shrinkage blue-shifts the color.

Compensation strategies:

  • Record at longer wavelength (e.g., 660 nm → 594 nm orange)
  • Use low-shrinkage materials (photopolymers: 0.5-2% shrinkage)
  • Pre-swell emulsion before exposure
  • Apply controlled post-processing expansion

Rainbow Holograms

Rainbow holograms (invented by Stephen Benton, 1969) represent a clever modification of transmission holography that enables white-light viewing. The key innovation is trading vertical parallax for color dispersion, creating the characteristic "rainbow" effect where different colors appear at different viewing heights.

Two-Step Recording Process

Rainbow holograms require a two-step transfer process:

  1. Master Hologram: Record a standard transmission hologram of the object
  2. Transfer Step: View the master hologram through a horizontal slit, recording this view as a new hologram
  3. Result: The slit image is encoded; viewing the transfer hologram through different parts of the reconstructed slit shows different wavelengths

Horizontal slit trades vertical parallax for wavelength dispersion.

Characteristics and Applications

  • White-light viewing: Can be viewed with sun or incandescent bulbs
  • Horizontal parallax only: 3D effect when moving left-right, not up-down
  • Color separation: Different colors at different vertical viewing angles
  • Brightness: Generally brighter than reflection holograms
  • Mass production: Easily embossed for security and packaging
  • Common uses: Credit cards, product authentication, magazine covers, decorative art

Why "Rainbow"?

The name "rainbow hologram" comes from the visible spectrum separation when viewing with white light. As you move your eye vertically, you see the object in different colors—red at the top, green in the middle, blue at the bottom—similar to viewing through a prism. This is the price paid for white-light convenience: you sacrifice vertical parallax and introduce chromatic dispersion.

Volume Holograms and Thick Media

Volume holograms are recorded in thick media where the hologram thickness is large compared to the fringe spacing. This creates a three-dimensional interference pattern rather than a surface grating, leading to unique properties essential for applications like holographic data storage and wavelength-selective optical elements.

Volume vs. Thin Hologram Criterion

The Klein-Cook parameter Q determines whether a hologram behaves as "thin" or "volume":

Q = (2π × λ × d) / (n × Λ²)

Classification:

  • Q < 1 → Thin hologram (Raman-Nath regime)
  • Q > 10 → Volume hologram (Bragg regime)
  • 1 < Q < 10 → Intermediate regime

Example Calculation

For a reflection hologram recorded at 532 nm in 15 μm thick emulsion (n=1.5) with fringe spacing 0.3 μm:

Q = (2π × 532×10⁻⁹ × 15×10⁻⁶) / (1.5 × (0.3×10⁻⁶)²) ≈ 37

Since Q >> 10, this is definitely a volume hologram, exhibiting strong Bragg selectivity.

Coupled Wave Theory

For volume gratings, Kogelnik's coupled wave theory predicts diffraction efficiency:

η = sin²(π × Δn × d / (λ × cos(θ)))

Where:

  • Δn = refractive index modulation
  • d = hologram thickness
  • λ = wavelength
  • θ = Bragg angle inside the medium

Maximum efficiency (η = 100%) occurs when the argument equals π/2:

Δn × d = λ / 2

Multiplexing Capabilities

Volume holograms enable storing multiple holograms in the same medium through various multiplexing techniques:

Method Principle Selectivity Capacity
Angular Different reference angles Δθ ≈ λ/d ~100-1000 holograms
Wavelength Different recording wavelengths Δλ ≈ λ²/d ~10-50 holograms
Phase-code Orthogonal phase patterns Correlation-based ~100-1000 holograms
Shift Lateral or axial displacement ~beam diameter ~100-500 holograms

The theoretical storage capacity of a volume hologram is given by:

Nmax ≈ (V / λ³) × (Δn)²

Where V is the volume of the recording medium. This formula suggests that a 1 cm³ volume at λ=500nm with Δn=0.01 could theoretically store over 10⁶ holograms!

Specialized Hologram Types

Image-Plane Holograms

In standard holograms, the object is at a different position than the hologram. Image-plane holograms record the object at or very near the hologram plane, creating a "contact copy" effect with unique properties:

  • Very bright (high efficiency possible)
  • Limited depth (object essentially flat)
  • Good for 2D images that need 3D "pop-out" effect
  • Used in some security applications

Embossed Holograms

Most holograms seen in everyday life (credit cards, product packaging) are embossed holograms—mass-produced from a master:

  1. Master creation: Record high-quality hologram in photoresist
  2. Metallization: Coat with nickel to create metal master (shim)
  3. Embossing: Hot-press shim into thermoplastic film
  4. Metallization: Coat embossed film with aluminum for reflection
  5. Result: Millions of copies from one master, each costing pennies

Edge-Lit Holograms

These specialized holograms are recorded on the edge of a glass or plastic substrate. Light couples into the substrate through total internal reflection, and the hologram diffracts it out at specific points, creating unique display effects used in automotive heads-up displays and novel lighting.

Key Takeaways

  • Transmission holograms require coherent laser light but offer excellent 3D reproduction for scientific and optical applications
  • Reflection holograms (Denisyuk) use Bragg diffraction to enable white-light viewing, ideal for art and display applications
  • Rainbow holograms trade vertical parallax for white-light convenience, making them perfect for security and mass production
  • Volume holograms in thick media (Q > 10) enable wavelength/angular selectivity and support multiplexing for data storage
  • The Klein-Cook parameter Q determines thin vs. volume behavior: Q = (2πλd)/(nΛ²)
  • Coupled wave theory predicts volume hologram efficiency: η = sin²(πΔnd/(λcosθ))
  • Multiplexing in volume holograms can store hundreds to thousands of holograms in the same medium
  • Material shrinkage during processing blue-shifts the reconstruction wavelength—critical for color accuracy
