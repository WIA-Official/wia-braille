Diffraction Theory & Bragg's Law

Exploring the physics of diffraction, volume hologram behavior, and the principles that govern holographic reconstruction through Bragg selectivity.

Understanding Diffraction

Diffraction is the phenomenon that allows holograms to work. When light encounters an obstacle or aperture comparable in size to its wavelength, it bends around it, creating interference patterns. In holography, the recorded interference fringes act as a diffraction grating—when illuminated, they diffract light to recreate the original wavefront.

The key to understanding holographic reconstruction is recognizing that a hologram is essentially a complex diffraction grating with varying spatial frequencies. Different parts of the hologram diffract light at different angles, reconstructing the complete three-dimensional wavefront that originally came from the object.

Fresnel Diffraction

Fresnel diffraction describes wave propagation in the near-field regime, where we cannot make far-field approximations. This is particularly relevant for digital holography and short-distance reconstruction. The Fresnel diffraction integral describes how a wavefront U(ξ,η,0) at plane z=0 propagates to create field U(x,y,z) at distance z:

U(x,y,z) = (exp(ikz) / iλz) × ∬ U(ξ,η,0) × exp{ik[(x-ξ)² + (y-η)²] / 2z} dξdη

Where:

  • k = 2π/λ = wave number
  • (ξ,η) = coordinates in source plane
  • (x,y) = coordinates in observation plane
  • z = propagation distance

Physical Interpretation

The Fresnel integral represents each point in the source plane as emitting a spherical wave. The observed field is the superposition of all these spherical wavelets, accounting for their relative phases based on path length differences. This is Huygens' principle in mathematical form.

Fraunhofer Diffraction

In the far field, where observation distance z is much larger than the size of the diffracting aperture, we can use the Fraunhofer approximation. This simplifies the diffraction integral to a Fourier transform relationship:

U(x,y,z) ≈ (exp(ikz) / iλz) × exp[ik(x² + y²) / 2z] × F{U(ξ,η,0)}

Where F{...} denotes the Fourier transform. This elegant result shows that the far-field diffraction pattern is simply the Fourier transform of the aperture function. This principle is fundamental to Fourier optics and computer-generated holography.

Fraunhofer Distance Criterion

The Fraunhofer approximation is valid when:

z >> D² / λ

Where D is the aperture size. For a 1 cm aperture and λ = 532 nm:

z >> (0.01)² / (532×10⁻⁹) ≈ 188 meters

This large distance requirement is why digital holography typically uses Fresnel or angular spectrum methods rather than Fraunhofer approximation.

Diffraction Efficiency

Diffraction efficiency measures how effectively a hologram converts incident light into the reconstructed wave. It's defined as the ratio of diffracted power to incident power:

η = (Idiffracted / Iincident) × 100%

Efficiency depends on many factors including hologram type, recording material, modulation depth, and reconstruction geometry.

Hologram Type Typical Efficiency Limiting Factors Theoretical Maximum
Thin Amplitude 1-6% Absorption losses, limited modulation 6.25%
Thin Phase 10-33% Higher order diffractions 33.9%
Thick Transmission 30-80% Material absorption, Bragg detuning 100%
Thick Reflection 50-95% Surface reflections, scattering 100%

Volume Holograms and Bragg's Law

When hologram thickness becomes comparable to or larger than the fringe spacing, we enter the regime of volume holograms. These thick holograms exhibit fundamentally different behavior from thin holograms, governed by Bragg's law—originally developed for X-ray diffraction in crystals.

Klein-Cook Parameter

The transition from thin to thick hologram is characterized by the Klein-Cook parameter Q:

Q = (2πλd) / (nΛ²)

Where:

  • λ = wavelength in vacuum
  • d = hologram thickness
  • n = average refractive index
  • Λ = grating period

Classification:

  • Q < 1: Thin (Raman-Nath) regime
  • Q ≈ 1: Intermediate regime
  • Q > 10: Thick (Bragg) regime

Example: Determining Hologram Type

Given:
• Wavelength λ = 532 nm
• Thickness d = 15 μm
• Refractive index n = 1.5
• Fringe spacing Λ = 1 μm

Calculation:
Q = (2π × 532×10⁻⁹ × 15×10⁻⁶) / (1.5 × (1×10⁻⁶)²)
Q = 33.4

Since Q >> 10, this is definitely a volume hologram exhibiting Bragg selectivity.

Bragg's Law for Volume Holograms

Volume holograms behave like three-dimensional diffraction gratings. Bragg's law determines the condition for constructive interference and efficient diffraction:

2Λ sin(θB) = mλ

Where:

  • Λ = spacing between recorded fringes
  • θB = Bragg angle (measured from fringe planes)
  • m = diffraction order (usually m = 1)
  • λ = wavelength

Bragg Diffraction Geometry

        Incident beam        Diffracted beam
              ↓                    ↑
               ╲                  ╱
                ╲    θB    θB    ╱
                 ╲      ↓  ↓      ╱
                  ╲═══════════╱     ← Fringe plane
                   ║         ║
                   ║  Λ      ║      ← Grating spacing
                   ║         ║
                  ╱═══════════╲
                 ╱             ╲

Light must satisfy Bragg condition (equal angles on both sides of fringe planes) for efficient diffraction.

Bragg Selectivity

Volume holograms exhibit remarkable selectivity—they only diffract light efficiently when Bragg conditions are satisfied. This selectivity applies to both angle and wavelength.

Angular Selectivity

The angular bandwidth over which diffraction remains efficient:

Δθ ≈ λ / (2d cos(θB))

For a thick hologram (d = 15 μm, λ = 532 nm, θB = 30°):

Δθ ≈ 532×10⁻⁹ / (2 × 15×10⁻⁶ × 0.866) ≈ 0.02 radians ≈ 1.2°

This narrow angular acceptance enables multiplexing—recording many holograms in the same volume at different angles, each reconstructible independently.

Wavelength Selectivity

Similarly, volume holograms are wavelength-selective:

Δλ / λ ≈ λ / (2d sin(θB))

For the same hologram:

Δλ / 532 nm ≈ 532×10⁻⁹ / (2 × 15×10⁻⁶ × 0.5) ≈ 0.035
Δλ ≈ 19 nm

This wavelength selectivity allows white-light viewing of reflection holograms—they reflect only the wavelength that satisfies Bragg condition, appearing in color despite white illumination.

Coupled Wave Theory

Kogelnik's coupled wave theory provides analytical solutions for diffraction efficiency in volume holograms. For a sinusoidal phase grating with refractive index modulation Δn:

η = sin²(πΔn d / λ cos(θ))

This shows:

  • Efficiency oscillates with modulation depth
  • 100% efficiency achievable when argument equals π/2
  • Requires: Δn × d = λ cos(θ) / 2

Transmission Geometry

Configuration: Reference and object on same side

Fringes: Parallel to surface

Peak efficiency:

η = sin²(πΔn d / λ cosθ)

For 100% efficiency:

Δn · d ≈ λ/2

Example: λ=532nm, need Δn·d ≈ 266 nm

Reflection Geometry

Configuration: Reference and object from opposite sides

Fringes: Perpendicular to surface

Peak efficiency:

η = tanh²(πΔn d / λ)

For 90% efficiency:

Δn · d ≈ 0.5λ

Example: λ=532nm, need Δn·d ≈ 266 nm

Practical Implications

Holographic Multiplexing

Bragg selectivity enables storing multiple holograms in the same volume through multiplexing techniques:

Multiplexing Method Principle Capacity Application
Angular Different reference angles ~1000 holograms Holographic data storage
Wavelength Different colors 3-10 holograms Color holography
Spatial Different positions Volume dependent Distributed storage
Phase-code Orthogonal phase patterns 100-1000 High-density storage

Storage Capacity of Volume Holograms

The theoretical information storage capacity of a volume hologram is given by:

Nmax = V / λ³ × (Δn)usable²

For a 1 cm³ crystal with λ = 532 nm and Δn = 0.01:

Nmax = 10⁻⁶ / (532×10⁻⁹)³ × (0.01)²
Nmax ≈ 6.6 × 10¹¹ bits ≈ 83 GB

Grating Vector Representation

Volume holograms are often analyzed using the grating vector K, which points perpendicular to the fringe planes with magnitude:

|K| = 2π / Λ

The Bragg condition can be elegantly expressed using vector notation:

kdiffracted = kincident + K

This momentum conservation equation shows that the grating vector must bridge the incident and diffracted wave vectors for efficient diffraction.

Key Takeaways

  • Fresnel diffraction describes near-field propagation essential for digital holography and reconstruction
  • Volume holograms (Q > 10) exhibit Bragg selectivity, diffracting only specific angles and wavelengths
  • Bragg's law (2Λ sinθ = λ) determines the condition for efficient diffraction in volume holograms
  • Angular selectivity (Δθ ≈ λ/2d) enables multiplexing thousands of holograms in the same volume
  • Wavelength selectivity allows volume reflection holograms to be viewed in white light
  • Coupled wave theory predicts diffraction efficiency can reach 100% with proper index modulation
  • Volume hologram storage capacity scales as V/λ³, enabling terabyte-scale data storage
