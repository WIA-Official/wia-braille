4.1 Introduction to Linear Propulsion

Levitation suspends the vehicle, but propulsion moves it. Maglev systems employ linear motors—essentially conventional rotary motors "unrolled" into a straight configuration. The WIA-AUTO-020 standard recognizes two primary propulsion technologies: Linear Synchronous Motors (LSM) and Linear Induction Motors (LIM). Each offers distinct performance characteristics, efficiency profiles, and implementation complexities that make them suitable for different applications.

Unlike conventional rail where rotating wheels provide propulsion through adhesion (friction), maglev propulsion operates through electromagnetic forces with no mechanical contact. This contactless thrust generation enables higher speeds, lower maintenance, and superior energy recovery through regenerative braking. Understanding these propulsion systems is essential for designing high-performance maglev transportation aligned with the 弘益人間 philosophy.

4.2 Linear Synchronous Motor (LSM)

The Linear Synchronous Motor represents the most advanced propulsion technology for high-speed maglev applications. LSM operates on the same fundamental principle as rotary synchronous motors, but with the stator "unrolled" along the guideway and the rotor (field poles) mounted on the vehicle.

4.2.1 Operating Principle

LSM propulsion relies on the interaction between a traveling magnetic wave in the guideway stator and magnetic poles on the vehicle. The guideway contains three-phase windings energized with AC current. These create a magnetic field that moves along the guideway at synchronous speed. Vehicle-mounted magnets (superconducting or permanent) lock onto this traveling field and are pulled forward.

Synchronous Speed:
v_sync = f × τ

Where:
  v_sync = Synchronous speed (m/s)
  f = Supply frequency (Hz)
  τ = Pole pitch (meters)

Example: f=100 Hz, τ=1.2 m → v_sync = 120 m/s = 432 km/h

The thrust force generated by an LSM is described by:

F_thrust = 3 × (B × I × L × N) × cos(θ) × η

Where:
  B = Air gap flux density (T)
  I = Current per phase (A)
  L = Active conductor length (m)
  N = Number of turns per coil
  θ = Load angle (electrical degrees)
  η = Efficiency factor (0.90-0.95)

The load angle θ represents the phase difference between rotor and stator magnetic fields. Maximum thrust occurs at θ ≈ 90°. Beyond this, the system loses synchronism and thrust collapses—a condition called "pull-out" that must be avoided through careful control.

4.2.2 LSM System Architecture

LSM implementations follow two primary configurations: short stator and long stator.

Configuration Stator Location Rotor Location Advantages Disadvantages
Short Stator On vehicle Passive guideway Lower guideway cost, simpler infrastructure Heavy vehicle, power pickup complexity
Long Stator Along guideway On vehicle (magnets) Lightweight vehicle, no power pickup, higher efficiency High guideway cost, complex power distribution

High-speed maglev systems universally employ long stator LSM for several compelling reasons:

  • Vehicle weight reduction improves acceleration and reduces levitation power
  • Eliminates high-current power collection at 500+ km/h (major technical challenge)
  • Enables segmented power control—only energize guideway sections with active vehicles
  • Facilitates regenerative braking energy recovery to grid

4.2.3 Power Electronics and Vector Control

LSM requires sophisticated power electronics to generate three-phase AC at variable frequency and amplitude. Modern systems employ field-oriented control (FOC) or vector control to independently manage flux-producing and torque-producing current components.

Vector Control Equations:

i_d = flux-producing current (align with rotor field)
i_q = torque-producing current (perpendicular to rotor field)

Thrust: F = k × i_q (for constant flux)

Field Weakening (high speed):
  i_d < 0 to reduce back-EMF
  Extends speed range beyond base speed
Component Specification Purpose
Grid Connection 20-35 kV AC, 3-phase Substation power input
Rectifier PWM active rectifier AC→DC conversion, power factor correction
DC Link 2-4 kV DC Energy storage, voltage smoothing
Inverter IGBT-based, 0-150 Hz DC→3-phase AC for LSM
Filter LC output filter Harmonic reduction

Inverter switching frequency typically operates at 1-5 kHz, balancing switching losses against harmonic performance. Modern silicon carbide (SiC) power devices enable higher switching frequencies with lower losses compared to traditional silicon IGBTs.

4.2.4 LSM Performance Specifications

Power Rating 5-10 MW per section Guideway section ~5-10 km
Frequency Range 0-150 Hz Corresponds to 0-650 km/h @ τ=1.2m

4.3 Linear Induction Motor (LIM)

The Linear Induction Motor provides a simpler, more robust alternative to LSM, particularly suited for medium-speed applications. LIM operates on induction motor principles, with the primary (stator) creating a traveling magnetic field that induces currents in a conductive reaction plate, generating thrust through electromagnetic interaction.

4.3.1 Operating Principle

Unlike LSM which requires synchronized operation, LIM operates asynchronously. The stator's traveling field moves faster than the vehicle (slip speed), inducing eddy currents in the aluminum or copper reaction plate on the guideway. These currents create a magnetic field that interacts with the stator field, producing thrust.

Slip:
s = (v_sync - v_actual) / v_sync

Typical slip: 2-8%

Example: v_sync = 500 km/h, s = 0.05
         → v_actual = 475 km/h

The thrust equation for LIM is more complex than LSM due to slip-dependent behavior:

F_lim = (3 × V² × R_r) / (ω × ((R_s + R_r)² + (X_s + X_r)²))

Where:
  V = Applied voltage per phase (V)
  R_r, R_s = Rotor and stator resistance (Ω)
  X_r, X_s = Rotor and stator reactance (Ω)
  ω = Angular frequency (rad/s)

Maximum thrust occurs at: s = R_r / √(R_s² + (X_s + X_r)²)

4.3.2 LIM Advantages and Limitations

Aspect Advantage Limitation
Complexity Simpler control—no synchronization required -
Robustness Tolerates speed/load variations better -
Guideway Passive reaction plate (lower cost than LSM coils) -
Efficiency - Lower (75-85%) due to slip losses
Power Factor - Lower (0.60-0.80) requires larger inverters
Air Gap - More sensitive to gap variation (10-25 mm)

4.4 Propulsion Performance Analysis

Understanding the relationship between thrust, speed, and power is critical for system design and energy optimization.

4.4.1 Acceleration Profile

Maglev acceleration follows a controlled profile balancing passenger comfort with performance:

a(t) = a_max × (1 - e^(-t/τ))

Where:
  a_max = Maximum acceleration (1.5-2.0 m/s²)
  τ = Time constant (5-10 seconds)

This provides "jerk-limited" acceleration for comfort.

Integrating acceleration over time yields the speed profile:

v(t) = v_max × (1 - e^(-a_max×t/v_max))

Time to 500 km/h (138.9 m/s) with a_max=2.0 m/s²:
  t ≈ 70 seconds
  Distance ≈ 4.8 km

4.4.2 Power-Speed Relationship

The power required for propulsion varies with speed according to resistance forces:

P(v) = F_total(v) × v

F_total = F_gradient + F_acceleration + F_aerodynamic

F_aerodynamic = ½ × ρ × C_d × A × v²

Where:
  ρ = Air density (1.225 kg/m³)
  C_d = Drag coefficient (0.15-0.25 for streamlined maglev)
  A = Frontal area (10-15 m²)
Speed (km/h) Aerodynamic Force (kN) Power Required (MW) Notes
200 25 1.4 Low-speed cruise
400 100 11.1 4× force, 8× power vs. 200 km/h
500 156 21.7 Rated cruise speed
600 225 37.5 Maximum design speed

This cubic relationship between power and speed (P ∝ v³ for aerodynamic drag) drives optimal cruise speed selection—typically 500-550 km/h balances travel time against energy consumption.

4.4.3 Efficiency Optimization

Total propulsion system efficiency chains multiple conversion stages:

η_total = η_grid × η_rectifier × η_DC_link × η_inverter × η_motor

Typical values:
  η_grid = 0.98 (transmission losses)
  η_rectifier = 0.97 (active PWM rectifier)
  η_DC_link = 0.99 (minimal resistive losses)
  η_inverter = 0.96 (IGBT switching + conduction)
  η_motor = 0.93 (LSM) or 0.80 (LIM)

LSM Total: 0.98 × 0.97 × 0.99 × 0.96 × 0.93 = 0.84 (84%)
LIM Total: 0.98 × 0.97 × 0.99 × 0.96 × 0.80 = 0.72 (72%)

4.5 Regenerative Braking

One of maglev's most significant advantages is highly efficient regenerative braking, converting kinetic energy back to electrical energy for grid return or vehicle auxiliary systems.

4.5.1 Energy Recovery Principles

During braking, the linear motor operates in generator mode. The vehicle's kinetic energy drives the motor, which generates electrical power fed back through the inverter (now operating as a rectifier) to the DC link and ultimately the grid.

E_kinetic = ½ × m × v²

For 500-ton train at 500 km/h:
  E_kinetic = ½ × 500,000 kg × (138.9 m/s)²
  E_kinetic = 4,825 MJ = 1,340 kWh

Recoverable energy (η_regen = 0.75):
  E_recovered = 0.75 × 1,340 = 1,005 kWh

At $0.10/kWh → $100.50 saved per braking event

4.5.2 Regeneration System Architecture

Component Function Specification
Motor (Generator Mode) Converts kinetic → electrical energy Same LSM/LIM hardware
Inverter (Rectifier Mode) Converts AC → DC Bidirectional power flow
DC Link Capacitor Absorbs power transients 10-50 kWh energy storage
Grid-Tie Inverter Returns power to AC grid Active power factor control
Brake Chopper (Backup) Dissipates excess energy if grid cannot absorb Resistor bank rated for emergency

Key Takeaways

  • LSM achieves 90-95% efficiency and enables speeds >600 km/h through synchronous operation with guideway stator coils
  • LIM offers simpler control and lower guideway cost but with reduced efficiency (75-85%) and speed capability
  • Propulsion power scales with v³ due to aerodynamic drag—doubling speed requires 8× power
  • Vector control enables independent management of flux and torque, optimizing performance across the speed range
  • Regenerative braking recovers 70-85% of kinetic energy, dramatically reducing operational costs and energy consumption
  • Long-stator LSM dominates high-speed applications due to lightweight vehicles and elimination of high-power sliding contacts
