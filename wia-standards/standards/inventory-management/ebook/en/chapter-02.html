Chapter 2: Inventory Management Fundamentals

Understanding the fundamental principles of inventory management is essential for building efficient supply chain operations. This chapter explores the core concepts, classification systems, control methodologies, and cost structures that form the foundation of modern inventory management practices.

2.1 Inventory Types and Classifications

Inventory represents a significant investment for most organizations, often accounting for 20-40% of total assets. Properly classifying inventory enables organizations to apply appropriate management strategies to different categories of goods.

Primary Inventory Categories

Inventory Classifications: 1. Raw Materials ├─ Components for production ├─ Packaging materials └─ Consumables and supplies 2. Work-in-Progress (WIP) ├─ Partially completed products ├─ Assembly stages └─ Quality control holds 3. Finished Goods ├─ Completed products ready for sale ├─ Packaged and labeled items └─ Quality-approved stock 4. Maintenance, Repair, Operations (MRO) ├─ Spare parts and tools ├─ Cleaning supplies └─ Office supplies 5. Transit Inventory ├─ In-transit between locations ├─ Shipping and receiving └─ Returns processing

Raw Materials include all inputs used in the manufacturing process. These materials have not yet been processed and represent the beginning of the production cycle. Managing raw material inventory requires careful coordination with suppliers to ensure continuous production without excessive stock levels.

Work-in-Progress (WIP) inventory consists of partially completed goods that are still in the production process. High WIP levels indicate bottlenecks in production, while low levels suggest efficient manufacturing flow. Lean manufacturing principles emphasize minimizing WIP to reduce costs and improve agility.

Finished Goods are completed products ready for sale to customers. This inventory directly impacts customer service levels and sales revenue. Balancing finished goods inventory requires accurate demand forecasting and efficient production planning.

MRO Inventory supports operations but isn't directly part of the final product. While individually inexpensive, MRO items can represent significant costs in aggregate. Many organizations underestimate the importance of MRO inventory management.

2.2 Inventory Control Systems

Organizations use two fundamental approaches to inventory control: perpetual systems and periodic systems. The choice between these systems depends on business size, industry, technology availability, and accuracy requirements.

Perpetual Inventory System

Perpetual System Characteristics: • Real-time tracking of all transactions • Continuous balance updates • Immediate visibility into stock levels • Higher accuracy (typically 95-99%) • Requires automated systems (barcode, RFID, WMS) Transaction Events Tracked: ├─ Receipt of goods (PO receipts) ├─ Sales and shipments (sales orders) ├─ Returns and adjustments (RMAs, damages) ├─ Transfers between locations (stock moves) └─ Cycle count adjustments (inventory corrections)

Periodic Inventory System

Item Category Count Frequency Rationale Example Items
High-value items (A-class) Daily or Weekly High cost of stockouts Electronics, pharmaceuticals
Fast-moving items Weekly Rapid turnover Consumables, popular products
Standard items (B-class) Monthly Moderate value Tools, accessories
Slow-moving items (C-class) Quarterly Low value Fasteners, basic supplies

2.3 ABC Analysis Framework

ABC analysis categorizes inventory items based on their annual consumption value (unit cost × annual demand). This Pareto-based approach allows organizations to focus resources on the most important items.

The 80/20 Rule in Inventory

The Pareto Principle states that roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes. In inventory management, approximately 20% of SKUs account for 80% of inventory value. ABC analysis operationalizes this principle.

Category % of SKUs % of Value Control Level Service Level Review Frequency
Category A 15-20% 75-80% Tight control 99%+ Weekly
Category B 30-35% 15-20% Moderate 95% Monthly
Category C 45-55% 5-10% Loose 85-90% Quarterly
ABC Analysis Example: Step 1: Calculate annual consumption value Item | Units/Year | Cost/Unit | Annual Value A1 | 5,000 | $250 | $1,250,000 B1 | 12,000 | $15 | $180,000 C1 | 50,000 | $0.50 | $25,000 Step 2: Sort by annual value (descending) Step 3: Calculate cumulative percentage - Top 20% of items → Category A - Next 30% → Category B - Remaining 50% → Category C Step 4: Apply differentiated controls A: Weekly review, 99% service, EOQ calculations B: Monthly review, 95% service, min-max systems C: Quarterly review, 90% service, bulk orders

2.4 Inventory Holding Costs

Understanding the true cost of holding inventory is critical for making informed decisions. Total holding costs typically range from 20-35% of inventory value annually.

Cost Component Typical % Description Example Costs
Capital Cost 8-12% Opportunity cost of capital Interest, foregone returns
Storage Cost 3-6% Physical space Rent, utilities, insurance
Risk Cost 5-8% Potential losses Obsolescence, damage, theft
Service Cost 2-4% Management Staff, equipment, systems
Holding Cost Calculation: H = (CC + SC + RC + SVC) × Average_Inventory_Value Example: Average Inventory: $5,000,000 Capital Cost: 10% ($500,000) Storage Cost: 4% ($200,000) Risk Cost: 6% ($300,000) Service Cost: 3% ($150,000) Total: 23% ($1,150,000/year) Per Unit: $1,150,000 / 100,000 units = $11.50/unit/year

2.5 Practical Applications

Organizations should implement perpetual inventory systems with cycle counting, conduct annual ABC analysis, calculate true holding costs, and review classifications as business conditions change.

Key Takeaways

  • Inventory Classification: Different types require different management strategies based on their operational role
  • Perpetual vs. Periodic: Perpetual systems provide superior real-time accuracy despite higher costs
  • ABC Analysis: Focus intensive management on 20% of SKUs representing 80% of value
  • Holding Costs: True costs typically range 20-35% annually including all components
  • Differentiated Control: Apply appropriate service levels based on ABC classification
  • Continuous Review: Regularly update classifications as conditions change
