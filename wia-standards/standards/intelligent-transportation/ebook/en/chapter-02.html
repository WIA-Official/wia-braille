📊 Traffic Flow Theory and Modeling

Exploring the fundamental relationships between traffic flow, density, and speed that form the mathematical foundation of ITS.

2.1 The Fundamental Diagram of Traffic Flow

Traffic flow theory provides the mathematical foundation for understanding how vehicles move through roadway networks. At its core is the fundamental relationship between three key variables: flow (q), density (k), and speed (v). This relationship, first formalized by Bruce Greenshields in 1935, remains central to traffic engineering and ITS design today.

Fundamental Equation:
q = k × v

Where:

  • q = Traffic flow (vehicles/hour): The number of vehicles passing a point per unit time
  • k = Traffic density (vehicles/km): The number of vehicles occupying a unit length of roadway
  • v = Average speed (km/h): The mean velocity of all vehicles in the traffic stream

This elegantly simple equation has profound implications. It tells us that flow equals the product of how many vehicles are present (density) and how fast they're moving (speed). Understanding this relationship allows traffic engineers to predict congestion, optimize signal timings, and design roadway improvements.

2.2 Speed-Density Relationship: The Greenshields Model

Greenshields observed that as more vehicles enter a roadway (increasing density), average speed decreases linearly. This makes intuitive sense—drivers slow down when they see more vehicles ahead. The relationship can be expressed mathematically as:

v = vf × (1 - k/kj)

Where:

  • vf = Free-flow speed (km/h): The speed when density is near zero (empty roadway)
  • kj = Jam density (vehicles/km): The maximum density when vehicles are bumper-to-bumper and stopped

This linear model predicts that speed decreases proportionally with density. When k = 0 (no vehicles), speed equals free-flow speed. When k = kj (jam density), speed drops to zero. The model's simplicity makes it useful for initial analysis, though real-world data often shows slight deviations from perfect linearity.

2.3 Flow-Density Relationship and Capacity

By substituting the speed-density equation into the fundamental equation, we derive the flow-density relationship:

q = k × vf × (1 - k/kj)

This parabolic relationship reveals a critical insight: maximum flow (capacity) occurs at an intermediate density, not at zero or maximum density. We can find this optimal point using calculus:

Maximum Flow (Capacity):
qmax = (vf × kj) / 4

Occurring at Critical Density:
kc = kj / 2

This is a fundamental result in traffic engineering. It tells us that roadway capacity is reached when density is exactly half of jam density. Operating beyond this point (higher density) actually reduces flow—more vehicles but moving so slowly that fewer pass through per hour. This phenomenon explains why severe congestion can reduce throughput below moderate traffic levels.

2.4 Level of Service (LOS) Classification

The Highway Capacity Manual defines Level of Service (LOS) as a qualitative measure describing operational conditions within a traffic stream. LOS ranges from A (best) to F (worst) based on density, speed, and other factors. This classification helps engineers communicate traffic conditions to non-technical audiences and set performance targets.

ITS deployments typically aim to maintain LOS C or better during peak periods. Adaptive signal systems, ramp metering, and other strategies work to prevent degradation to LOS D or worse. Real-time monitoring of density and speed allows systems to detect deteriorating conditions and implement countermeasures before breakdown occurs.

2.5 Alternative Models: Greenberg and Underwood

While Greenshields' linear model works well for many conditions, researchers have developed alternative models that better fit certain traffic regimes:

Greenberg Model (Congested Traffic)

The Greenberg model, based on fluid dynamics analogies, performs better for congested conditions:

v = vm × ln(kj / k)

Where vm is the speed at maximum flow. This logarithmic relationship shows speed decreasing rapidly as density approaches jam density, which matches observed behavior in heavy congestion better than the linear Greenshields model.

Underwood Model (Free-Flow Traffic)

The Underwood model uses an exponential relationship that works well for light traffic:

v = vf × exp(-k/ko)

Where ko is the optimal density. This model captures the gradual speed reduction observed as density increases from very low levels.

Model Best Application Advantages Limitations
Greenshields General purpose, all conditions Simple, linear, easy to calibrate May not fit congested data well
Greenberg Congested, stop-and-go traffic Captures rapid speed drops near jam Undefined at zero density
Underwood Free-flow, light traffic Smooth transition from low density Doesn't reach zero speed at jam

2.6 Practical Applications in ITS

Traffic flow models aren't just theoretical—they're embedded in ITS algorithms that operate millions of traffic signals and manage highways worldwide. Here's how:

Incident Detection

Automatic incident detection algorithms monitor for violations of expected flow-density relationships. If observed conditions deviate significantly from model predictions, an incident is likely. For example:

Example Algorithm:

IF (occupancy > 25%) AND (speed < 30 km/h) AND (duration > 2 minutes) THEN
    TRIGGER incident alert
    NOTIFY operators
    UPDATE traveler information systems
END IF

Ramp Metering

Ramp meters regulate freeway entry to maintain density below critical levels. The metering rate is calculated based on current mainline flow and remaining capacity:

r = min(rmax, (C - qmainline) / nramps)

Where:

  • r = Metering rate (vehicles/hour)
  • rmax = Maximum metering rate (ensures reasonable ramp delays)
  • C = Mainline capacity
  • qmainline = Current mainline flow
  • nramps = Number of metered ramps affecting this section

Variable Speed Limits

Dynamic speed limit systems use flow models to determine safe speeds that maximize throughput while preventing breakdown:

VSL = vf × (1 - α × (k/kc))

Where α is an adjustment factor (typically 0.2-0.4). As density approaches critical density, the system reduces speed limits to prevent breakdown and maintain stable flow.

2.7 Example Calculations

Example 1: Basic Flow Calculation

Given:

  • Density: k = 50 veh/km
  • Average speed: v = 60 km/h

Calculate: Flow rate (q)

Solution:

q = k × v = 50 × 60 = 3,000 veh/h

Example 2: Capacity Calculation

Given:

  • Free-flow speed: vf = 100 km/h
  • Jam density: kj = 150 veh/km

Calculate: Maximum flow capacity and critical density

Solution:

qmax = (vf × kj) / 4 = (100 × 150) / 4 = 3,750 veh/h

kc = kj / 2 = 150 / 2 = 75 veh/km

At capacity, speed vc = vf × (1 - kc/kj)
                        = 100 × (1 - 75/150)
                        = 100 × 0.5 = 50 km/h

Example 3: Level of Service Determination

Given:

  • Observed density: k = 15 veh/km/lane
  • Free-flow speed: vf = 110 km/h
  • Current speed: v = 85 km/h

Determine: Level of Service

Solution:

Speed percentage = (85/110) × 100% = 77.3%
Density = 15 veh/km/lane

Referring to LOS table:
- Density 11-18 veh/km/lane
- Speed 70-85% of free-flow
→ Level of Service: C (Stable flow)

2.8 Shockwave Theory and Queue Analysis

When traffic conditions change suddenly (e.g., incident blocking lanes), a shockwave propagates through the traffic stream. Understanding shockwave dynamics helps predict queue formation and dissipation—critical for incident management.

The shockwave speed (w) between two traffic states is:

w = (q2 - q1) / (k2 - k1)

Where subscripts 1 and 2 represent upstream and downstream conditions. A negative shockwave speed indicates the disturbance propagates backward (upstream), which is typical when congestion forms.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Fundamental Equation: q = k × v relates flow, density, and speed—the foundation of all traffic flow theory.
  • Greenshields Model: Speed decreases linearly with density from free-flow speed to zero at jam density.
  • Capacity Occurs at kc: Maximum flow happens at critical density (kj/2), not at minimum or maximum density.
  • LOS Classification: Six levels (A-F) describe operational quality from free-flow to breakdown conditions.
  • Alternative Models: Greenberg (congested) and Underwood (free-flow) models capture non-linear speed-density relationships better in specific regimes.
  • ITS Applications: Flow theory enables incident detection, ramp metering, variable speed limits, and signal optimization algorithms.
