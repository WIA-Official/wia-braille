Chapter 4: Magnetic Levitation Systems

弘益人間 (Benefit All Humanity)
Eliminating mechanical friction through magnetic levitation enables energy-efficient, high-speed transportation for all.

4.1 Magnetic Levitation Fundamentals

Magnetic levitation (maglev) eliminates physical contact between pod and track, removing rolling resistance and enabling high-speed, low-wear operation. Unlike conventional rail where steel wheels on steel rails create friction and noise, maglev systems use electromagnetic forces to suspend the vehicle with a precisely controlled gap of 8-15 millimeters.

Physics of Magnetic Forces

Two primary maglev technologies apply to hyperloop systems, each exploiting different electromagnetic phenomena:

Technology Force Type Advantages Challenges
Electromagnetic Suspension (EMS) Attractive Works at all speeds, proven technology Requires active control, consumes power
Electrodynamic Suspension (EDS) Repulsive Inherently stable, lower power Requires minimum speed, larger gap

Electromagnetic Suspension (EMS)

EMS systems use conventional electromagnets that attract to ferromagnetic rails mounted on the track. The force equation:

F_lev = (B² × A) / (2μ₀)

Where:
F_lev = Levitation force (N)
B = Magnetic flux density (Tesla)
A = Pole face area (m²)
μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m (permeability of free space)
EMS Force Calculation: For 15,000 kg pod requiring levitation: F_required = m × g = 15,000 × 9.81 = 147,150 N With safety factor 2.0: F_design = 294,300 N Single magnet specifications: B = 1.2 Tesla (achievable with iron core) A = 0.4 m² (pole face area) F_magnet = (1.2² × 0.4) / (2 × 4π × 10⁻⁷) F_magnet = (0.576) / (2.513 × 10⁻⁶) F_magnet ≈ 229,250 N per magnet Number of magnets needed: 294,300 / 229,250 ≈ 1.3 Actual design: 4 magnets per side (8 total) for redundancy and distribution

EMS systems are inherently unstable—increasing gap reduces force, causing further gap increase in a positive feedback loop. Active control systems continuously adjust electromagnet current to maintain stable 10mm gap despite disturbances.

PID Control Algorithm

The controller adjusts electromagnet current based on gap error:

Control Equation: I(t) = K_p × e(t) + K_i × ∫e(t)dt + K_d × de(t)/dt Where: e(t) = g_target - g_measured (gap error) K_p = 50,000 A/m (proportional gain) K_i = 10,000 A/(m·s) (integral gain) K_d = 500 A·s/m (derivative gain) Example calculation at t=0: g_target = 10.0 mm g_measured = 10.3 mm (pod dropping slightly) e(t) = 10.0 - 10.3 = -0.3 mm = -0.0003 m I(t) = 50,000 × (-0.0003) + 10,000 × (integral term) + 500 × (derivative term) I(t) = -15 A + (other terms) Negative current → reduce magnet force → allow pod to rise back to target gap

4.3 Power Consumption and Efficiency

Maglev systems consume power continuously during operation. Understanding power requirements enables accurate energy budgeting:

Power Components

Component Power Draw Notes
Electromagnet Coils 40-60 kW Resistive heating in copper windings
Control Electronics 5-10 kW Sensors, processors, power converters
Cooling Systems 10-15 kW Remove heat from coils and electronics
Total Continuous 55-85 kW Throughout journey duration

Energy Efficiency Optimization

Several strategies minimize levitation power consumption:

  • Optimized Coil Design: Low-resistance copper windings with large cross-sectional area reduce I²R losses. Typical coil resistance: 0.05-0.1 ohms
  • Efficient Magnets: High-permeability iron cores concentrate flux, achieving required force with lower current
  • Temperature Management: Cooling systems maintain coil temperature below 80°C, preventing resistance increase and insulation degradation
  • Regenerative Capability: During braking, levitation system can recover energy by feeding back to pod batteries
Power Calculation for 600 km Journey: Journey duration: 35 minutes = 0.583 hours Levitation power: 70 kW average Energy consumed: 70 × 0.583 = 40.8 kWh Per passenger (28 passengers): 40.8 / 28 = 1.46 kWh Comparison to total trip energy (113 kWh): Levitation: 40.8 kWh (36%) Acceleration: 47 kWh (42%, net after regen) Cruising drag: 7.8 kWh (7%) Auxiliary: 17.4 kWh (15%)

4.4 Lateral Guidance System

In addition to vertical levitation, the maglev system must provide lateral guidance to keep the pod centered in the tube during curves and crosswinds.

Guidance Force Requirements

During curve negotiation, centripetal acceleration creates lateral loads:

Lateral Force in Curve: F_lateral = m × v² / R For sharpest allowed curve: m = 15,000 kg (loaded pod mass) v = 300 m/s R = 61,000 m (minimum radius for 0.15g lateral acceleration) F_lateral = 15,000 × (300)² / 61,000 F_lateral = 15,000 × 90,000 / 61,000 F_lateral = 22,131 N ≈ 22 kN Guidance magnets must provide: - Centering force when pod drifts laterally - Restoring force proportional to displacement - Sufficient range to handle maximum lateral load Guidance system stiffness: K_lateral ≥ 100,000 N/m Maximum lateral displacement: ±50 mm Maximum restoring force: ±50 kN

4.5 Fault Tolerance and Redundancy

Safety-critical levitation systems require redundancy to handle component failures without compromising passenger safety:

Redundancy Strategy

  • N+2 Architecture: Eight levitation magnets where six can support full load. Any two can fail without emergency landing
  • Dual Sensors: Each gap measurement uses two independent sensors with cross-checking algorithms
  • Redundant Controllers: Primary and backup control computers with automatic failover in <10ms
  • Emergency Landing Skids: Mechanical backup allowing safe 0.3g deceleration to rest if maglev completely fails
  • Battery Backup: Onboard batteries maintain levitation for 15+ minutes after main power loss

Failure Mode Analysis

Failure Mode Probability Response Outcome
Single magnet failure 1 × 10⁻⁴ per hour Redistribute load to remaining magnets Continue to next station
Gap sensor failure 5 × 10⁻⁵ per hour Switch to redundant sensor No passenger impact
Control computer fault 1 × 10⁻⁵ per hour Automatic failover to backup <10ms interruption
Complete power loss 1 × 10⁻⁶ per hour Battery backup activates 15 min to reach station
Multiple critical failures <1 × 10⁻⁹ per hour Deploy landing skids, emergency brake Safe stop, minor discomfort

Key Takeaways

  • Electromagnetic Suspension (EMS) generates 229,250 N force per magnet using 1.2 Tesla flux density and 0.4 m² pole face area, requiring 8 magnets total for 15,000 kg pod with safety factor
  • Active PID control maintains stable 10mm levitation gap by adjusting electromagnet current at 1000 Hz update rate using proportional, integral, and derivative feedback terms
  • Levitation power consumption averages 70 kW continuous operation, comprising 55% coil resistance losses, 15% control electronics, and 30% cooling systems
  • Lateral guidance forces of 22 kN enable curve negotiation at 300 m/s on 61 km radius curves with 0.15g lateral acceleration and ±50mm maximum displacement
  • N+2 redundancy architecture allows continued operation with any two magnet failures, supported by dual sensors, redundant controllers, and emergency landing skids
  • Levitation energy represents 36% of total trip energy (40.8 kWh of 113 kWh for 600 km journey), making it second only to acceleration in energy consumption
