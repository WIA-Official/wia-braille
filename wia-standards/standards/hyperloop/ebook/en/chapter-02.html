Chapter 2: Tube Infrastructure and Vacuum Systems

弘益人間 (Benefit All Humanity)
Building robust infrastructure that serves humanity for generations requires careful engineering of materials, structures, and maintenance systems.

2.1 Tube Geometry and Design

The hyperloop tube provides the fundamental pathway and environment for pod travel. Unlike conventional rail where tracks simply guide vehicles, the hyperloop tube must simultaneously serve as structural support, pressure vessel, vacuum chamber, and aerodynamic shroud. This multifunctional requirement demands careful attention to geometry, materials, and construction techniques.

Cross-Sectional Design

The standard hyperloop tube employs a circular cross-section with 3.3 meters internal diameter. This dimension balances several competing requirements:

Tube Geometry: Inner Diameter (D): 3.3 meters Wall Thickness (t): 20-30 mm Cross-sectional Area: A = π(D/2)² A = π × (1.65)² A ≈ 8.55 m² Pod Cross-Section: 5.72 m² Area Ratio (β): β = 5.72 / 8.55 = 0.67

The 0.67 area ratio means the pod occupies 67% of the tube's cross-section. This relatively high ratio minimizes tube cost and material usage but creates aerodynamic challenges. At high speeds, air compressed between pod and tube wall can reach sonic velocity—a phenomenon called "choking" governed by the Kantrowitz limit.

Material Selection

Three primary material families suit hyperloop tube construction, each with distinct advantages:

Material Density (kg/m³) Yield Strength (MPa) Applications
Steel (ASTM A36) 7,850 250-400 Underground, high-load sections
Aluminum Alloy 6061 2,700 240-290 Elevated sections, weight-critical spans
Carbon Fiber Composite 1,600 500-1000 Specialized segments, repairs

Steel offers proven fabrication techniques, excellent strength, and cost-effectiveness for underground installations where weight matters less. Aluminum's lower density—only 34% of steel—dramatically reduces support requirements for elevated sections, despite slightly lower strength. Carbon composites serve niche applications where their exceptional strength-to-weight ratio justifies higher costs.

2.2 Vacuum System Architecture

Maintaining the tube at 100 Pa operating pressure represents one of hyperloop's most demanding engineering challenges. At this pressure, the tube contains only 1/1000th of normal atmospheric air mass, creating a near-vacuum environment throughout potentially hundreds of kilometers.

Operating Pressure Calculations

The relationship between pressure, temperature, and air density follows the ideal gas law:

ρ = (P × M) / (R × T)

Where:
ρ = Air density (kg/m³)
P = Pressure (Pa)
M = Molar mass of air (0.029 kg/mol)
R = Gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K)
T = Temperature (K)
At 100 Pa, 20°C (293 K): ρ = (100 × 0.029) / (8.314 × 293) ρ = 2.9 / 2436 ρ ≈ 0.0012 kg/m³ Comparison to atmospheric density (1.225 kg/m³): Reduction factor = 1.225 / 0.0012 ≈ 1,021×

This dramatic density reduction delivers hyperloop's fundamental advantage, but achieving and maintaining it requires sophisticated vacuum systems.

Vacuum Pump Configuration

The vacuum system employs distributed pumping with stations positioned every 5-10 kilometers along the tube. Each station contains:

Component Specification Purpose
Primary Rotary Pumps 1,000-5,000 m³/hr, 4-6 units Maintain operating pressure
Booster Pumps 10,000+ m³/hr, 2 units Initial evacuation, leak response
Pressure Sensors ±0.1 Pa accuracy, every 500m Continuous monitoring
Control System PLC with redundant logic Automated pump management
Emergency Valves Pneumatic, fail-safe Section isolation

Leak Rate Management

No physical system achieves perfect vacuum seal. The tube continuously leaks small amounts of air through welds, seals, and material porosity. Acceptable operation requires leak rates below the pumping system's capacity to remove inflowing air.

Leak Rate Equation: Q_leak = V × (dP/dt) Where: Q_leak = Total leak rate (Pa·m³/s) V = Tube volume (m³) dP/dt = Pressure rise rate (Pa/s) For 100 km tube: V = π × (1.65)² × 100,000 = 855,000 m³ Acceptable pressure rise: 1 Pa/hour = 2.78 × 10⁻⁴ Pa/s Q_leak = 855,000 × 2.78 × 10⁻⁴ Q_leak = 238 Pa·m³/s Required pumping speed: >300 m³/s (with safety margin)

2.3 Thermal Management

Tubes exposed to sunlight experience significant thermal loading. Solar radiation can heat exposed surfaces 30-50°C above ambient temperature, causing thermal expansion that must be accommodated without compromising structural integrity or vacuum seal.

    │    Very poor heat transfer medium      │
    └────────────────────────────────────────┘

Thermal expansion for a 100-kilometer tube section:

Thermal Expansion: ΔL = α × L × ΔT For steel tube: α = 12 × 10⁻⁶ /°C (thermal expansion coefficient) L = 100,000 m ΔT = 50°C (peak temperature rise) ΔL = 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 100,000 × 50 ΔL = 60 meters This 60-meter expansion must be accommodated!

Expansion joints positioned every 100-500 meters allow each tube segment to expand and contract independently. Each joint includes:

  • Bellows or slip joint allowing ±300mm axial movement
  • Vacuum-tight seal maintaining <0.1 Pa·l/s leak rate
  • Lateral restraint preventing sideways deflection
  • Inspection access for maintenance

2.4 Structural Support Systems

The tube support structure must safely carry dead loads (tube weight), live loads (pod passage), and environmental loads (wind, seismic) while maintaining precise alignment for magnetic levitation operation.

Pylon Design for Elevated Sections

Elevated tube sections rest on pylons spaced 30-50 meters apart. Each pylon must support:

Load Calculation (per 50m span): Dead Load (tube weight): Steel tube: 7,850 kg/m³ × tube volume Volume per meter: π × [(1.665)² - (1.65)²] = 0.156 m³ Weight: 0.156 × 7,850 = 1,224 kg/m 50m span: 61,200 kg Live Load (pod passage): Pod weight: 15,000 kg Dynamic factor: 1.5× (impact, vibration) Live load: 22,500 kg Environmental Loads: Wind: 1-3 kN/m (depends on height, location) Seismic: 0.3-0.5g lateral acceleration Total Design Load: ~100-150 kN per pylon

Seismic Isolation

Earthquakes pose particular challenges for hyperloop systems. The tube must remain intact and maintain vacuum seal during seismic events, while ground motion can displace supports by meters.

Seismic Design Element Specification Function
Elastomeric Bearings ±300mm displacement Absorb horizontal motion
Friction Pendulum 2-5 second period Isolate from ground acceleration
Expansion Joints Every 100-500m Allow independent segment movement
Emergency Valves Seismic triggers Isolate damaged sections

2.5 Construction and Installation

Hyperloop tube construction employs techniques adapted from pipeline, tunnel, and aerospace industries. The construction sequence typically follows:

  1. Route Survey and Geotechnical Investigation: Detailed mapping identifies optimal alignment, geological hazards, and foundation requirements
  2. Foundation Construction: Pour concrete foundations for pylons with seismic isolation systems
  3. Tube Fabrication: Manufacture tube sections in 10-30 meter lengths at factory facilities ensuring quality control
  4. Transport and Assembly: Deliver sections to construction site and weld end-to-end with full-penetration welds
  5. Weld Inspection: X-ray and ultrasonic testing verify weld integrity (critical for vacuum seal)
  6. Vacuum Testing: Test each section for leak rate before connecting to main system
  7. System Integration: Install pumps, sensors, expansion joints, and control systems

Quality Control Requirements

Vacuum integrity demands exceptional quality control throughout construction:

  • Weld Quality: All longitudinal and circumferential welds require 100% radiographic inspection. Maximum allowable defect: 2mm diameter
  • Leak Testing: Each tube section undergoes helium leak testing. Maximum allowable leak rate: 10⁻⁶ Pa·m³/s per weld
  • Alignment: Tube centerline maintained within ±10mm vertically, ±20mm horizontally over any 100m section
  • Surface Finish: Internal surface smoothness Ra < 3.2 μm to minimize aerodynamic drag and prevent vacuum pump contamination

Key Takeaways

  • Hyperloop tubes employ 3.3m diameter circular cross-sections with 20-30mm wall thickness, constructed from steel, aluminum, or carbon fiber composites depending on application requirements
  • Maintaining 100 Pa operating pressure requires distributed vacuum pumps every 5-10km, each providing 1,000-5,000 m³/hr pumping capacity to compensate for continuous air leakage
  • Thermal expansion from solar heating can cause 60m elongation over 100km tube length, requiring expansion joints every 100-500m to accommodate movement while maintaining vacuum seal
  • Seismic isolation systems using elastomeric bearings and friction pendulums protect tube integrity during earthquakes by allowing ±300mm ground displacement without structural damage
  • Leak rate management maintains acceptable operation when Q_leak = V × (dP/dt) remains below pumping system capacity, typically requiring leak rates below 10⁻⁶ Pa·m³/s per weld
  • Construction quality control demands 100% weld inspection, helium leak testing, and alignment verification within ±10mm to ensure long-term vacuum integrity and safe operation
