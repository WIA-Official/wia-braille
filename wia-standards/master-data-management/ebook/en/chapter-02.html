Chapter 2

Master Data Modeling and Design

Effective master data management begins with thoughtful data modeling and design. A well-designed master data model serves as the blueprint for your MDM implementation, defining the structure, relationships, and business rules that govern your critical business entities.

Understanding Entity-Relationship Modeling

Master data modeling uses entity-relationship (ER) modeling techniques adapted for the unique requirements of MDM. Unlike transactional database design, MDM models must accommodate:

  • Multiple source systems with different schemas and business rules
  • Temporal dimensions to track changes over time
  • Data quality metadata including confidence scores and data lineage
  • Complex hierarchies and relationships that may vary by context

Core Master Data Domains

Most organizations focus on a core set of master data domains. Let's examine each in detail:

Customer Master Data

Customer master data represents individuals and organizations who interact with your business. A comprehensive customer model includes:

Entity Attributes Relationships
Party PartyID, PartyType, CreatedDate Base entity for Person and Organization
Person FirstName, LastName, DateOfBirth, Gender Extends Party, has Addresses, ContactMethods
Organization LegalName, TaxID, Industry, NumberOfEmployees Extends Party, has Locations, Relationships
Address AddressLine1, City, State, PostalCode, Country Belongs to Party, has AddressType
ContactMethod ContactType, ContactValue, IsPrimary Email, Phone, Social media handles

Product Master Data

Product master data encompasses the goods and services your organization offers. Key considerations include:

Product Hierarchy Example:

Category
  └─ Product Family
      └─ Product Line
          └─ Base Product
              └─ Product Variant (SKU)
                  └─ Item (Serialized)

Example:
Electronics
  └─ Computers
      └─ Laptops
          └─ MacBook Pro
              └─ MacBook Pro 16" M3 512GB Space Gray
                  └─ Serial# C02XK12345

Product attributes vary significantly by industry but typically include:

  • Identifying attributes: SKU, UPC, GTIN, Part Number
  • Descriptive attributes: Name, Description, Brand, Specifications
  • Classification attributes: Category, Product Line, Material Group
  • Commercial attributes: Price, Cost, Currency, Tax Category
  • Operational attributes: Weight, Dimensions, Hazmat Class, Shelf Life

Supplier/Vendor Master Data

Supplier master data tracks organizations that provide goods or services to your company:

  • Legal Entity Information: Legal name, registration number, tax identifiers
  • Financial Details: Payment terms, bank accounts, credit limits
  • Compliance Data: Certifications, audit results, risk ratings
  • Performance Metrics: On-time delivery, quality scores, spend history
  • Relationship Data: Contracts, contacts, negotiated rates

Canonical Data Model Design

A canonical data model (CDM) represents the "gold standard" structure for master data within your organization. The CDM serves several purposes:

Benefits of a Canonical Data Model:
  • Provides a common language across business and IT stakeholders
  • Simplifies integration by defining standard structures for data exchange
  • Reduces the complexity of point-to-point integrations (N-to-N becomes N-to-1)
  • Enables data quality rules to be defined once and applied consistently
  • Facilitates compliance by establishing authoritative data definitions

CDM Design Principles

When designing a canonical data model, follow these principles:

  • Business-driven: Model should reflect business concepts, not technical implementation
  • Source-agnostic: Don't favor one source system's structure over others
  • Future-proof: Accommodate expected business changes and new data sources
  • Normalized where appropriate: Reduce redundancy while maintaining usability
  • Extensible: Allow for custom attributes without breaking the core model

Handling Complex Relationships

Master data often involves complex relationships that must be carefully modeled:

Hierarchies

Hierarchies represent parent-child relationships within a domain:

  • Product hierarchies: Category → Family → Line → Product → SKU
  • Organizational hierarchies: Corporation → Division → Department → Team
  • Customer hierarchies: Global Account → Regional Office → Local Branch
  • Location hierarchies: Country → Region → City → Site → Building → Floor

Advanced hierarchy modeling must support:

  • Multiple hierarchies: Same entity in different hierarchies (sales territory vs. support region)
  • Time-variant hierarchies: Hierarchy changes over time (reorganizations)
  • Multiple parents: Entity belongs to multiple parent nodes (matrix organizations)
  • Circular references: Detection and prevention of circular parent-child relationships

Many-to-Many Relationships

Some business relationships are many-to-many, requiring associative entities:

Example: Product-Supplier Relationship

Product ←→ ProductSupplier ←→ Supplier

ProductSupplier attributes:
  - ProductID (FK)
  - SupplierID (FK)
  - PreferredSupplier (Boolean)
  - LeadTimeDays (Integer)
  - MinimumOrderQuantity (Decimal)
  - UnitPrice (Decimal)
  - EffectiveDate (Date)
  - ExpirationDate (Date)

Temporal Modeling

Master data changes over time, and MDM systems must track these changes. There are three main approaches:

Type 1: Overwrite

Simply update the record with new values. No history is maintained.

  • Pros: Simple, no storage overhead
  • Cons: Historical data lost, can't reconstruct past states
  • Use case: Correcting errors, updating non-critical attributes

Type 2: Add New Row

Create a new version of the record when changes occur, keeping all versions.

  • Pros: Complete history, can reconstruct any point in time
  • Cons: Increased storage, more complex queries
  • Use case: Regulatory requirements, audit trails, trend analysis

Type 3: Add New Column

Add separate columns to track current and previous values (or specific dates).

  • Pros: Easy to query current vs. previous, limited storage overhead
  • Cons: Limited history (typically only 1-2 previous values)
  • Use case: Tracking specific attribute changes (e.g., customer segment)
Hybrid Approach: Many MDM implementations use a hybrid approach, applying different temporal strategies to different attributes based on business requirements. For example, customer name changes might use Type 2 (full history) while email address updates use Type 1 (overwrite).

Extensibility and Customization

Your data model must accommodate organization-specific and industry-specific requirements. Common extensibility patterns include:

1. Custom Attributes Table

CREATE TABLE CustomerCustomAttribute (
    CustomerID VARCHAR(50),
    AttributeName VARCHAR(100),
    AttributeValue VARCHAR(4000),
    DataType VARCHAR(20),
    EffectiveDate DATE,
    ExpirationDate DATE
);

2. JSON/XML Extension Fields

CREATE TABLE Customer (
    CustomerID VARCHAR(50) PRIMARY KEY,
    FirstName VARCHAR(100),
    LastName VARCHAR(100),
    -- ... standard fields ...
    CustomAttributes JSON,
    ExtensionData XML
);

3. Typed Extension Tables

-- Base customer table with common attributes
CREATE TABLE Customer ( ... );

-- Industry-specific extension for healthcare
CREATE TABLE CustomerHealthcare (
    CustomerID VARCHAR(50) PRIMARY KEY,
    PatientID VARCHAR(50),
    PrimaryPhysician VARCHAR(100),
    InsuranceProvider VARCHAR(100),
    FOREIGN KEY (CustomerID) REFERENCES Customer(CustomerID)
);

Reference Data Management

Reference data (code lists, valid values, lookup tables) plays a critical role in master data management:

  • Country codes: ISO 3166 country list
  • Currency codes: ISO 4217 currency list
  • Industry classifications: NAICS, SIC codes
  • Product categories: Organization-specific taxonomies
  • Status codes: Active, Inactive, Suspended, etc.

Reference data should be:

  • Centrally managed: Single source of truth for code lists
  • Version controlled: Track changes to reference data over time
  • Synchronized: Distribute to all consuming systems
  • Governed: Changes require approval and impact analysis

Model Validation and Testing

Before implementing your data model, validate it against real-world scenarios:

Data Model Validation Checklist:
  • Can the model accommodate all known use cases?
  • Does it support required reporting and analytics?
  • Can it handle the expected data volume and query load?
  • Does it comply with relevant regulations and standards?
  • Is it flexible enough for anticipated future requirements?
  • Can source system data be mapped to the model without loss of critical information?
  • Are all relationships properly defined and enforced?
  • Is the model documented and understandable to business stakeholders?

Summary

A well-designed master data model is the foundation of MDM success. It must balance standardization with flexibility, accommodate multiple source systems while providing a unified view, and support both current and future business needs. By following the principles and patterns outlined in this chapter, you can create a robust data model that serves as the blueprint for your MDM implementation.
