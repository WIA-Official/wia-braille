Chapter 2: Passive House Design Principles

"The most sustainable energy is the energy you don't need to use. Passive House design represents the ultimate expression of this philosophy, achieving extraordinary comfort with minimal energy input."
— Dr. Wolfgang Feist, Passive House Institute

2.1 The Passive House Standard

The Passive House (Passivhaus in German) standard represents one of the most rigorous and successful approaches to ultra-low energy building design. Developed in Germany in the early 1990s by Dr. Wolfgang Feist and Professor Bo Adamson, the standard has grown from a research project to a globally recognized certification system with over 60,000 certified buildings worldwide.

At its core, the Passive House standard is based on a simple yet profound insight: by dramatically improving the building envelope and minimizing heat loss, you can reduce heating and cooling loads to such an extent that conventional heating and cooling systems become largely unnecessary. The small amount of energy still required can be delivered through the ventilation system or other minimal heating sources.

Passive House Performance Criteria

To achieve Passive House certification, a building must meet the following requirements:

  • Space Heating Demand: ≤ 15 kWh/m²/year or heating load ≤ 10 W/m²
  • Space Cooling Demand: ≤ 15 kWh/m²/year (with additional moisture removal if needed)
  • Primary Energy Demand: ≤ 120 kWh/m²/year (includes all energy: heating, cooling, hot water, appliances)
  • Air Tightness: ≤ 0.6 air changes per hour at 50 Pascals pressure (ACH50)
  • Thermal Comfort: Not more than 10% of hours over 25°C in any given year

2.2 The Five Principles of Passive House Design

Achieving Passive House performance requires adherence to five fundamental principles that work synergistically to create an ultra-efficient building envelope:

2.2.1 Continuous Insulation

Thermal insulation is the foundation of Passive House design. However, it's not just about the amount of insulation, but also about its continuous, uninterrupted application. Thermal bridges—areas where heat can bypass insulation—are the enemy of high-performance building envelopes.

Insulation Levels: Passive Houses typically employ insulation levels far exceeding conventional construction. While a standard building might use R-13 to R-20 insulation in walls, Passive Houses commonly use R-40 to R-60 or higher. Roof insulation often exceeds R-60, and even foundation slabs may have R-30 or more insulation beneath them.

Thermal Bridge-Free Design: Every connection, penetration, and transition in the building envelope must be carefully detailed to maintain continuity of insulation. This requires:

  • Continuous exterior insulation wrapping the entire building envelope
  • Careful detailing at foundation-to-wall, wall-to-roof, and wall-to-window junctions
  • Thermal breaks in structural connections that penetrate the insulation layer
  • Insulated window frames and optimized glazing edge conditions

U-Value Calculation

U = 1 / Rtotal

Where: U = Overall heat transfer coefficient (W/m²K)

Rtotal = Sum of all thermal resistances (m²K/W)

Target for Passive House: U ≤ 0.15 W/m²K for opaque surfaces

2.2.2 High-Performance Windows and Doors

Windows represent one of the weakest thermal links in any building envelope. In conventional buildings, windows can account for 25-30% of heating energy loss despite representing only 15-20% of the envelope area. Passive House standards demand extraordinary window performance.

Triple-Glazed Windows: Passive House windows typically feature three panes of glass with low-emissivity coatings and gas fills (argon or krypton) between panes. This achieves:

Component Conventional Passive House
Overall U-value 2.0-3.0 W/m²K ≤ 0.8 W/m²K
Glazing U-value 2.5-2.8 W/m²K ≤ 0.6 W/m²K
Frame U-value 1.5-2.0 W/m²K ≤ 0.8 W/m²K
Solar Heat Gain 0.3-0.5 0.5-0.6 (optimized)

Window Installation: Proper installation is as critical as window performance. Passive House windows are typically installed in the insulation layer rather than the structural wall, minimizing thermal bridging. Installation must ensure:

  • Continuity of air barrier from wall to window frame
  • Continuity of insulation around the window perimeter
  • Proper flashing and weatherproofing
  • Thermal break between window frame and structural opening

Strategic Orientation: Window placement is optimized for passive solar gain in heating-dominated climates or shading in cooling-dominated climates. South-facing windows (in the Northern Hemisphere) can be net energy contributors even in winter, while north-facing glazing is minimized.

2.2.3 Airtight Construction

Air leakage represents a major source of heat loss and moisture problems in conventional buildings. Passive House standards require exceptional airtightness, verified through blower door testing.

The 0.6 ACH50 Standard: Passive House requires that buildings achieve no more than 0.6 air changes per hour when subjected to 50 Pascals of pressure difference (ACH50). To put this in perspective:

  • Conventional construction: 5-15 ACH50
  • Energy Star homes: 3-5 ACH50
  • High-performance homes: 1-3 ACH50
  • Passive House: ≤ 0.6 ACH50

Achieving Airtightness: This level of airtightness requires meticulous attention to detail throughout construction:

Critical Airtightness Details

  • Continuous Air Barrier: A single, continuous membrane or assembly that wraps the entire conditioned space
  • Sealed Penetrations: Every pipe, wire, duct, and other penetration must be carefully sealed
  • Window and Door Integration: Airtight connection between frames and air barrier
  • Service Cavity: Often a separate cavity is provided for services to avoid penetrating the air barrier
  • Quality Assurance: Blower door testing during construction allows identification and correction of leaks

Benefits Beyond Energy: Airtightness provides numerous benefits beyond energy savings, including improved indoor air quality (controlled ventilation only), enhanced comfort (no drafts), better sound insulation, and protection against moisture infiltration and structural damage.

2.2.4 Thermal Bridge-Free Design

Thermal bridges are localized areas of higher heat flow through the building envelope, occurring wherever the insulation is penetrated or reduced. Common thermal bridges include structural elements (studs, joists, beams), window and door frames, foundation-wall connections, and service penetrations.

Impact of Thermal Bridges: While individually small, thermal bridges can collectively account for 20-30% of total heat loss in an otherwise well-insulated building. They also create cold spots on interior surfaces, leading to condensation, mold growth, and discomfort.

Thermal Bridge-Free Construction Strategies:

Continuous Exterior Insulation

By placing a continuous layer of rigid insulation on the exterior of the structure, thermal bridges through the structural frame are eliminated. This approach allows the structure to remain at a moderate temperature, protected from thermal cycling.

Advanced Framing Techniques

When insulation must be placed between structural members, advanced framing reduces the amount of framing material (which conducts heat more readily than insulation):

  • 2x6 or 2x8 studs on 24-inch centers instead of 2x4 on 16-inch centers
  • Single top plates and minimal headers
  • Two-stud corners and minimal structural elements at wall intersections
  • Insulated headers

Thermal Break Products

Proprietary products like Schock Isokorb or Armatherm provide structural support while incorporating insulation to minimize thermal bridging at balconies, canopies, and other structural penetrations.

Psi-Value (Linear Thermal Transmittance)

Ψ = (Ueffective × A - Σ Ui × Ai) / L

Where: Ψ = Linear thermal transmittance (W/mK)

L = Length of thermal bridge (m)

Target for Passive House: Minimize Ψ-values, typically < 0.01 W/mK

2.2.5 Heat Recovery Ventilation

In airtight buildings, mechanical ventilation is essential to maintain indoor air quality. Passive House leverages this requirement by recovering heat from exhaust air before it leaves the building.

Heat Recovery Ventilator (HRV) Operation: An HRV uses a heat exchanger to transfer heat from warm, stale exhaust air to cool, fresh incoming air in winter (or vice versa in summer). This allows continuous fresh air supply without the energy penalty of conventional ventilation.

Passive House HRV Requirements: To meet Passive House standards, HRVs must achieve:

Parameter Minimum Requirement
Heat Recovery Efficiency ≥ 75% (typically 80-95%)
Electrical Efficiency ≤ 0.45 Wh/m³
Ventilation Rate 0.3 ACH (air changes per hour) minimum
Filter Rating ≥ F7 (MERV 13)

System Components: A complete Passive House ventilation system includes:

  • Heat Recovery Core: Counter-flow or cross-flow heat exchanger
  • Filters: High-efficiency particulate filters for incoming air
  • Fans: EC (electronically commutated) motors for maximum efficiency
  • Ducting: Insulated supply and return ducts to minimize energy loss
  • Controls: Variable speed control responsive to occupancy and air quality sensors
  • Frost Protection: Preheating or defrost cycle for cold climates

Indoor Air Quality Benefits: HRV systems provide superior indoor air quality compared to conventional buildings by:

  • Continuously removing pollutants, odors, and excess moisture
  • Filtering incoming air to remove particulates, pollen, and allergens
  • Maintaining consistent fresh air supply regardless of weather
  • Controlling humidity levels to prevent mold and improve comfort
  • Distributing fresh air evenly throughout the building

2.3 Passive Solar Design

Beyond the five core principles, Passive House design incorporates passive solar strategies to minimize heating loads and maximize free solar heat gain.

2.3.1 Building Orientation

Optimal building orientation can reduce heating energy by 10-20% in heating-dominated climates. The ideal orientation:

  • Long axis running east-west to maximize south-facing surface area
  • Primary glazing on south facade (in Northern Hemisphere)
  • Minimal glazing on north facade
  • East and west glazing limited to functional requirements (views, daylight)

2.3.2 Thermal Mass

Thermal mass (concrete, masonry, water) absorbs heat during the day and releases it at night, moderating temperature swings. In Passive House design, thermal mass is typically placed on the interior side of insulation where it can interact with indoor air and solar gains.

Thermal Mass Sizing: The optimal amount of thermal mass depends on climate and use patterns. Too little provides insufficient temperature moderation; too much can make the building slow to respond to heating or cooling input. General guidelines:

  • Light construction (wood frame): 50-100 kg/m² floor area
  • Medium construction (some masonry): 100-200 kg/m² floor area
  • Heavy construction (concrete, masonry): 200-400 kg/m² floor area

2.3.3 Solar Shading

While solar gain is beneficial in winter, it can cause overheating in summer. Effective shading strategies are essential:

Shading Strategies

  • Fixed Overhangs: Sized to block high summer sun while admitting low winter sun
  • Movable Shading: Exterior blinds, shutters, or screens for flexible control
  • Vegetation: Deciduous trees provide summer shade and allow winter sun
  • Glass Selection: Lower solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) for east and west glazing
  • Interior Shading: Last resort, as heat is already inside the building

2.4 Passive House Planning Package (PHPP)

The Passive House Planning Package is a comprehensive energy modeling tool specifically designed for Passive House projects. Unlike simplified energy models, PHPP uses monthly energy balance calculations with detailed input parameters.

2.4.1 PHPP Inputs

PHPP requires detailed information about every aspect of the building:

  • Climate data (temperature, solar radiation, wind)
  • Building geometry and orientation
  • Envelope assembly U-values and areas
  • Thermal bridge lengths and Psi-values
  • Window specifications (U-value, g-value, installation details)
  • Airtightness test results
  • Ventilation system specifications
  • Internal heat gains (occupants, appliances, lighting)
  • Heating, cooling, and hot water systems

2.4.2 PHPP Outputs

PHPP provides comprehensive energy performance predictions:

Output Description
Heating Demand Annual space heating energy requirement (kWh/m²/year)
Cooling Demand Annual space cooling energy requirement (kWh/m²/year)
Heating Load Peak heating power requirement (W/m²)
Cooling Load Peak cooling power requirement (W/m²)
Primary Energy Total energy including heating, cooling, hot water, appliances (kWh/m²/year)
Overheating Frequency of temperatures exceeding 25°C
Pressurization Test Predicted airtightness (ACH50)

2.5 Case Study: Passive House in Practice

To illustrate these principles in action, consider the Waldsee BioHaus in Minnesota, one of the first certified Passive Houses in North America.

Project Details:

  • Location: Bemidji, Minnesota (cold climate, -40°C design temperature)
  • Size: 1,550 m² (16,700 ft²)
  • Function: Educational and residential facility
  • Certification: Passive House (2011)

Design Features:

  • Wall assembly: R-64 (11.3 m²K/W) with 16" double-stud walls
  • Roof: R-85 (15 m²K/W) with blown cellulose insulation
  • Foundation: R-60 (10.6 m²K/W) with exterior rigid insulation
  • Windows: Triple-pane, European import, U-0.14 (0.8 W/m²K)
  • Airtightness: 0.56 ACH50 (better than Passive House standard)
  • HRV: 84% efficient Zehnder system

Performance Results:

  • Heating demand: 14.6 kWh/m²/year (just under 15 kWh/m²/year standard)
  • Annual heating cost: $450 (vs. $3,500 for comparable conventional building)
  • Energy savings: 87% compared to code-compliant building
  • Thermal comfort: Indoor temperatures stable within 2°C year-round

Lessons Learned

The Waldsee BioHaus demonstrates that Passive House performance is achievable even in extreme climates. Key success factors included: early engagement of the design team with Passive House principles, use of PHPP for detailed modeling and optimization, rigorous quality control during construction, blower door testing at multiple stages to identify and fix air leaks, and comprehensive commissioning of all systems.

2.6 Integration with WIA-ENE-015

The WIA-ENE-015 standard fully incorporates Passive House principles as the foundation for zero energy building design. However, WIA-ENE-015 goes further by requiring:

  • On-site renewable energy generation sufficient to achieve net-zero annual energy consumption
  • Energy storage systems for grid resilience
  • Advanced building energy management systems (BEMS)
  • Smart grid integration capabilities
  • Lifecycle carbon accounting
  • Embodied energy minimization strategies

By starting with Passive House-level efficiency, the amount of renewable energy required to reach zero is dramatically reduced, making zero energy buildings more economically viable and technically achievable.

Chapter Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Passive House standard requires ≤15 kWh/m²/year heating/cooling demand and ≤0.6 ACH50 airtightness
  • Five core principles: continuous insulation, high-performance windows, airtight construction, thermal bridge-free design, heat recovery ventilation
  • Insulation levels (R-40 to R-60 walls, R-60+ roofs) far exceed conventional construction
  • Triple-glazed windows with U-values ≤0.8 W/m²K are essential
  • Airtightness verified by blower door testing prevents energy waste and moisture problems
  • Thermal bridges can account for 20-30% of heat loss and must be eliminated through careful detailing
  • HRVs with ≥75% efficiency recover heat from exhaust air while ensuring excellent indoor air quality
  • Passive solar design, thermal mass, and shading optimize free heating and cooling
  • PHPP software provides accurate energy modeling for Passive House design
  • WIA-ENE-015 builds on Passive House principles by adding renewable energy and smart systems
