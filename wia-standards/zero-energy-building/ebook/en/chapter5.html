Chapter 5: Energy Efficiency Standards

5.1 Energy Performance Metrics

Measuring and benchmarking energy performance is essential for zero energy buildings. Multiple metrics provide different perspectives on building efficiency and enable comparison across buildings, climates, and uses.

5.1.1 Energy Use Intensity (EUI)

Energy Use Intensity, measured in kWh/m²/year or kBtu/ft²/year, normalizes energy consumption by building size, enabling fair comparison between buildings of different sizes. EUI is the most common metric for building energy performance.

5.1.2 Site vs Source Energy

Site Energy: Energy consumed at the building as delivered by utilities. Simple to measure but doesn't account for generation and transmission losses.

Source Energy: Total energy required to generate and deliver energy to the building, including power plant efficiency and transmission losses. Provides more accurate picture of environmental impact. Source-to-site ratios: Electricity 2.5-3.0, Natural Gas 1.05, District Steam 1.35.

5.1.3 Carbon Intensity

Carbon intensity measures greenhouse gas emissions per unit energy consumption (kg CO2e/kWh or tons CO2e/year). Critical for emissions-based zero energy definitions and climate impact assessment.

Grid carbon intensity varies dramatically by location and time:
- Coal-heavy grids: 0.8-1.0 kg CO2/kWh
- Gas-dominant grids: 0.4-0.6 kg CO2/kWh
- Renewable-heavy grids: 0.05-0.2 kg CO2/kWh

5.2 International Energy Codes and Standards

5.2.1 ASHRAE Standards

ASHRAE 90.1: Energy Standard for Buildings Except Low-Rise Residential. Updated every 3 years with progressively stricter requirements. Widely adopted as basis for U.S. building codes.

ASHRAE 189.1: Standard for the Design of High-Performance Green Buildings. Goes beyond 90.1 with requirements for renewable energy, commissioning, and indoor environmental quality.

ASHRAE 55: Thermal Environmental Conditions for Human Occupancy. Defines comfort parameters critical for HVAC system design.

5.2.2 International Energy Conservation Code (IECC)

The IECC is the most widely adopted residential energy code in the United States, updated every 3 years. Requirements include minimum R-values for insulation, maximum U-factors for windows, air leakage limits, and minimum efficiency for HVAC and water heating equipment.

5.2.3 European Standards

Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD): EU directive requiring all new buildings to be nearly zero energy by 2021. Each member state implements through national building codes.

EN Standards: Detailed technical standards for energy performance calculation, HVAC systems, renewable energy integration, and building automation.

5.3.2 Lighting

LED technology has revolutionized lighting efficiency. Modern LEDs achieve 100-150 lumens per watt, compared to 15-20 for incandescent and 60-80 for fluorescent. Zero energy buildings should exclusively use LED lighting with daylight harvesting and occupancy controls.

Lighting Power Density (LPD): Maximum watts per square meter/foot for different space types. ASHRAE 90.1-2019 LPDs range from 0.5 W/ft² (corridors) to 1.2 W/ft² (retail). High-performance buildings target 30-50% below code LPDs.

5.3.3 Appliances and Plug Loads

ENERGY STAR certification identifies top-performing appliances and equipment. Zero energy buildings should specify ENERGY STAR or equivalent for all appliances, electronics, and plug loads where available.

Typical savings vs. standard equipment:
- Refrigerators: 15-30%
- Dishwashers: 10-20%
- Computers/Monitors: 25-40%
- Printers: 20-50%

5.4 Commissioning and Quality Assurance

5.4.1 Building Commissioning Process

Commissioning ensures that building systems are installed and operate according to design intent. Studies show that commissioned buildings use 10-20% less energy than non-commissioned buildings and experience fewer occupant complaints.

Commissioning Phases:

  1. Design Review: Verify design meets owner's project requirements and efficiency targets
  2. Submittal Review: Ensure specified equipment and materials are being provided
  3. Installation Verification: Inspect installation quality and completeness
  4. Functional Testing: Test all systems under multiple operating modes
  5. Training: Ensure operators understand and can maintain systems
  6. Documentation: Comprehensive as-built drawings and O&M manuals
  7. Monitoring: Post-occupancy monitoring to verify continued performance

5.4.2 Air Tightness Testing

Blower door testing is mandatory for zero energy buildings to verify air tightness. Testing should occur at multiple stages: rough-in (before insulation), final construction, and annually thereafter.

Testing Protocol:

  • Depressurize building to 50 Pascals
  • Measure airflow required to maintain pressure
  • Calculate ACH50 (air changes per hour at 50 Pa)
  • If target not met, use thermal imaging and smoke tests to locate leaks
  • Seal leaks and retest until target achieved

5.4.3 Performance Verification

Post-occupancy performance verification confirms that buildings achieve predicted energy performance. WIA-ENE-015 requires verification during the first two years of operation.

Verification Methods:

  • Utility Bill Analysis: Compare actual to predicted annual energy use
  • Submetering Data: Analyze detailed consumption by end use
  • Spot Measurements: Verify equipment operation (temperatures, pressures, flow rates)
  • Regression Analysis: Correlate energy use with weather and occupancy

5.5 Lifecycle Assessment and Embodied Energy

5.5.1 Embodied Energy

Embodied energy is the total energy required to extract, manufacture, transport, and install building materials. For high-performance buildings with very low operating energy, embodied energy can represent 20-40% of lifetime energy consumption.

Embodied Energy by Material (MJ/kg):
- Timber: 2-8
- Concrete: 1-2
- Steel: 20-35
- Aluminum: 150-200
- Glass: 12-25
- Insulation (fiberglass): 15-30
- Insulation (cellulose): 0.5-3

5.5.2 Embodied Carbon

Embodied carbon includes CO2 emissions from material production. Concrete and steel are particularly carbon-intensive due to chemical processes (cement calcination) and high-temperature manufacturing.

Reduction Strategies:

  • Use low-carbon concrete mixes (fly ash, slag cement, recycled aggregate)
  • Specify recycled steel and aluminum
  • Minimize structural material through efficient design
  • Use locally sourced materials to reduce transportation emissions
  • Select bio-based materials (wood, straw bale, hempcrete) that sequester carbon
  • Design for deconstruction and material reuse at end of life

5.5.3 Lifecycle Cost Analysis

Lifecycle cost analysis (LCCA) evaluates total costs over building lifetime including initial construction, operation, maintenance, and eventual disposal. LCCA reveals that high-performance features with higher first costs often provide significant net savings over 20-50 year building lifetimes.

LCCA Components:

  • Initial Costs: Construction, equipment, commissioning
  • Operating Costs: Energy, water, maintenance
  • Replacement Costs: Equipment replacement at end of service life
  • Residual Value: Building value at end of analysis period
  • Discount Rate: Time value of money (typically 3-7%)

5.6 WIA-ENE-015 Efficiency Requirements

Mandatory Performance Requirements

  • Building Envelope: Must meet or exceed Passive House standards or equivalent
  • HVAC: Minimum SEER 18 / HSPF 10 for heat pumps, or equivalent for other systems
  • Lighting: 100% LED with daylight harvesting and occupancy controls, LPD 50% below ASHRAE 90.1
  • Appliances: ENERGY STAR or equivalent for all applicable equipment
  • Air Tightness: Maximum 0.6 ACH50, verified by blower door testing
  • Commissioning: Full building commissioning per ASHRAE Guideline 0 or equivalent
  • Monitoring: Continuous monitoring of energy consumption by major end use
  • Embodied Carbon: Maximum 500 kg CO2e/m² for new construction (including structure and envelope)

Chapter Summary

  • EUI (kWh/m²/year) is primary metric for building energy performance
  • Source energy accounts for generation/transmission losses; multiply site electricity by 2.5-3.0
  • ASHRAE 90.1 and IECC are dominant energy codes in North America; EPBD in Europe
  • High-performance HVAC (SEER 18+, HSPF 10+) and 100% LED lighting are essential
  • Building commissioning reduces energy use 10-20% and improves occupant satisfaction
  • Blower door testing verifies air tightness target (0.6 ACH50 for WIA-ENE-015)
  • Embodied energy and carbon are significant in zero energy buildings; use low-carbon materials
  • Lifecycle cost analysis reveals long-term value of efficiency investments
