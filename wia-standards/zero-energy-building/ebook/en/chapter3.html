Chapter 3: Building Energy Management Systems (BEMS)

"You can't manage what you don't measure. Building Energy Management Systems transform data into actionable intelligence, enabling zero energy buildings to optimize performance in real-time."

3.1 Introduction to BEMS

Building Energy Management Systems (BEMS), also known as Building Management Systems (BMS) or Building Automation Systems (BAS), are computer-based control systems that monitor and manage a building's mechanical, electrical, and electromechanical equipment. In the context of zero energy buildings, BEMS play a critical role in ensuring that the building operates at peak efficiency while maintaining optimal comfort conditions.

Traditional buildings often operate with significant inefficiencies: heating and cooling systems that fight each other, lights that operate when spaces are unoccupied, HVAC systems that run at full capacity regardless of actual demand. BEMS eliminate these inefficiencies through intelligent monitoring, control, and optimization.

Core Functions of BEMS in Zero Energy Buildings

  • Monitoring: Real-time tracking of energy consumption, environmental conditions, and system performance
  • Control: Automated operation of HVAC, lighting, solar systems, battery storage, and other equipment
  • Optimization: Algorithms that minimize energy consumption while maintaining comfort and performance
  • Analysis: Data analytics to identify trends, inefficiencies, and opportunities for improvement
  • Reporting: Comprehensive energy and performance reports for operators and stakeholders
  • Integration: Coordination of all building systems for maximum efficiency and grid interaction

3.2 BEMS Architecture

Modern BEMS are layered systems with components ranging from field devices to cloud-based analytics platforms. Understanding this architecture is essential for designing effective zero energy building control strategies.

3.2.1 Field Level (Sensors and Actuators)

The field level consists of physical devices that interface with building systems:

Sensors

Sensors collect data about building conditions and system operation:

Sensor Type Measurements Applications
Temperature Air, surface, fluid temperatures HVAC control, comfort monitoring
Humidity Relative humidity, dew point Ventilation control, moisture management
Occupancy Motion, CO2, thermal presence Lighting, HVAC demand response
Light Illuminance levels Daylight harvesting, lighting control
Power Voltage, current, power factor Energy monitoring, load management
Environmental CO2, VOCs, particulates Indoor air quality management
Solar Irradiance, PV output Solar generation monitoring

Actuators

Actuators execute control commands to adjust building systems:

  • Damper Actuators: Control airflow in HVAC systems
  • Valve Actuators: Regulate water flow in heating and cooling loops
  • Variable Frequency Drives (VFDs): Adjust motor speeds for fans and pumps
  • Relay Switches: Turn equipment on and off
  • Lighting Dimmers: Adjust light output levels
  • Shade Controllers: Position blinds and shades for solar control

3.2.2 Automation Level (Controllers)

Controllers form the intelligence layer of BEMS, executing control logic based on sensor inputs and programmed strategies. Modern controllers are typically microprocessor-based devices with significant computational capability.

Types of Controllers:

  • Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs): Industrial-grade controllers for critical systems
  • Direct Digital Controllers (DDCs): Purpose-built for HVAC and building systems
  • Application-Specific Controllers: Specialized controllers for lighting, solar inverters, battery management, etc.
  • Room Controllers: Local zone control for individual spaces

Control Strategies:

Common BEMS Control Strategies

  • Occupancy-Based Control: Reduce lighting and HVAC when spaces are unoccupied
  • Optimal Start/Stop: Calculate minimum operating time to reach setpoints efficiently
  • Demand-Controlled Ventilation: Adjust fresh air based on actual occupancy (CO2 levels)
  • Load Shedding: Temporarily reduce non-critical loads during peak demand periods
  • Night Purge: Use cool night air for pre-cooling in summer
  • Economizer Control: Use outdoor air for cooling when conditions permit
  • Daylight Harvesting: Dim electric lights in response to available daylight
  • Thermal Storage Optimization: Shift cooling loads to off-peak hours using thermal mass or ice storage

3.2.3 Management Level (BEMS Software)

The management level provides the human interface to the BEMS, including visualization, control, and configuration capabilities.

Key Features:

  • Graphical User Interface: Intuitive dashboards showing real-time building status
  • Trending and Logging: Historical data storage and graphing
  • Alarm Management: Notification of abnormal conditions or equipment failures
  • Scheduling: Time-based operation of equipment and setpoints
  • Energy Reporting: Automated generation of energy consumption reports
  • Maintenance Management: Tracking of equipment runtime and maintenance schedules

3.2.4 Enterprise Level (Analytics and Integration)

Modern zero energy buildings increasingly leverage cloud-based platforms for advanced analytics, machine learning, and integration with external systems.

Advanced Capabilities:

  • Predictive Maintenance: Machine learning algorithms identify impending equipment failures
  • Fault Detection and Diagnostics (FDD): Automated identification of system inefficiencies
  • Energy Forecasting: Predict future energy consumption and generation
  • Optimization Algorithms: Continuous fine-tuning of control strategies
  • Portfolio Management: Centralized management of multiple buildings
  • Grid Integration: Coordination with utility demand response programs

3.3 Communication Protocols

Effective BEMS require standardized communication protocols to enable interoperability between devices from different manufacturers. Several protocols dominate the building automation landscape.

3.3.1 BACnet (Building Automation and Control Network)

BACnet is the most widely adopted open protocol for building automation, standardized as ASHRAE 135 and ISO 16484-5. BACnet enables communication between controllers, sensors, and management systems regardless of manufacturer.

Key Features:

  • Object-oriented architecture with standardized data models
  • Multiple transport options (IP, MSTP, LonTalk, ZigBee)
  • Extensive library of standardized object types and properties
  • Support for complex scheduling, trending, and alarm management
  • Testing and certification program ensuring interoperability

3.3.2 Modbus

Originally developed for industrial automation, Modbus is widely used in building systems, particularly for energy meters, solar inverters, and battery controllers.

Characteristics:

  • Simple master-slave architecture
  • Modbus RTU (serial) and Modbus TCP/IP variants
  • Lightweight protocol suitable for resource-constrained devices
  • Extensive adoption in energy monitoring equipment

3.3.3 MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport)

MQTT is a lightweight publish-subscribe protocol increasingly used for IoT applications in buildings, particularly for cloud integration and distributed sensing.

Advantages:

  • Extremely efficient, suitable for bandwidth-constrained networks
  • Publish-subscribe model enables flexible data distribution
  • Quality of Service (QoS) levels ensure reliable message delivery
  • Native support in cloud platforms (AWS IoT, Azure IoT, Google Cloud IoT)
  • Security through TLS/SSL encryption and authentication

3.3.4 WIA-ENE-015 Protocol Requirements

The WIA-ENE-015 standard requires that BEMS support at least one open, standardized protocol (preferably BACnet for local systems and MQTT for cloud integration) to ensure interoperability and avoid vendor lock-in. Proprietary protocols are permitted for internal system communication but must not prevent integration with standard monitoring and control platforms.

3.4 Energy Monitoring and Submetering

Detailed energy monitoring is fundamental to zero energy building operation. "You can't manage what you don't measure" is particularly true for energy. Submetering breaks down total building energy consumption into granular components, enabling identification of inefficiencies and verification of savings.

3.4.1 Metering Hierarchy

Effective energy monitoring follows a hierarchical structure:

Level Description Example Points
Level 0 Utility meters Total building electric, gas, water
Level 1 Major end uses HVAC, lighting, plug loads, solar generation
Level 2 System-level AHUs, chillers, pumps, boilers
Level 3 Equipment-level Individual motors, compressors, heaters
Level 4 Zone/space-level Floor, department, tenant spaces

WIA-ENE-015 Minimum Requirements:

  • Level 0 metering with 15-minute interval data
  • Level 1 metering for all major end uses (≥5% of total load)
  • Level 2 metering for HVAC systems
  • Renewable generation metering with 5-minute intervals
  • Battery storage metering (charge/discharge) with 1-minute intervals

3.4.2 Power Quality Monitoring

Beyond simple energy consumption, power quality monitoring tracks:

  • Voltage: Ensure stable supply within acceptable range
  • Current: Identify overloaded circuits
  • Power Factor: Optimize efficiency and avoid utility penalties
  • Harmonics: Detect distortion that can damage equipment
  • Demand: Track peak demand to minimize demand charges

3.5 Advanced BEMS Features for Zero Energy Buildings

3.5.1 Model Predictive Control (MPC)

Model Predictive Control uses mathematical models of building thermal behavior combined with weather forecasts to optimize HVAC operation hours in advance. Rather than reactive control (responding to current conditions), MPC anticipates future conditions and makes proactive adjustments.

Benefits:

  • 10-30% energy savings compared to conventional control
  • Improved comfort through reduced temperature fluctuations
  • Ability to shift loads to periods with lower electricity costs or higher solar generation
  • Optimal use of building thermal mass as energy storage

Implementation:

Algorithm: Model Predictive Control
Inputs:
  - Building thermal model (heat capacity, U-values, infiltration)
  - Current state (temperatures, occupancy, equipment status)
  - Weather forecast (temperature, solar radiation, wind)
  - Utility rates (time-of-use pricing, demand charges)
  - Occupancy schedule

Process:
  1. Predict building thermal behavior for next 24-48 hours
  2. Evaluate multiple control scenarios
  3. Select scenario minimizing cost while maintaining comfort
  4. Execute first control step
  5. Repeat at each control interval (typically 15-60 minutes)

Outputs:
  - Optimal HVAC setpoints and operating schedules
  - Pre-cooling/pre-heating commands
  - Load shifting recommendations

3.5.2 Renewable Energy Optimization

Zero energy buildings must intelligently manage variable renewable generation to maximize self-consumption and minimize grid dependence.

Strategies:

  • Load Matching: Shift flexible loads (water heating, battery charging, thermal storage) to periods of high solar generation
  • Battery Dispatch: Optimize battery charge/discharge to maximize self-consumption and peak shaving
  • Predictive Generation: Use solar forecasting to anticipate generation and plan loads accordingly
  • Grid Export Management: Control export to grid based on utility agreements and revenue optimization

3.5.3 Demand Response Integration

Zero energy buildings can participate in utility demand response programs, providing grid services while generating revenue or avoiding high electricity costs.

Demand Response Strategies:

  • Manual Demand Response: Operator receives notification and manually reduces loads
  • Automated Demand Response (ADR): BEMS automatically reduces loads in response to signals from utility
  • Load Shedding: Temporarily reduce or shut off non-critical loads
  • Temperature Setpoint Adjustment: Relax comfort setpoints during DR events
  • Thermal Pre-Cooling: Over-cool building before DR event to reduce cooling load during event
  • Battery Dispatch: Discharge batteries to reduce grid import
  • Solar Curtailment: Reduce solar export if requested by utility (rare)

3.5.4 Fault Detection and Diagnostics (FDD)

Automated FDD systems continuously monitor building systems for performance degradation or faults, enabling rapid correction and preventing energy waste.

Common Faults Detected:

System Typical Faults Energy Impact
HVAC Damper stuck, sensor drift, simultaneous heating/cooling 10-30% increase
Lighting Failed occupancy sensors, daylight sensors out of calibration 20-40% increase
Solar PV Shading, soiling, inverter failure, string faults 5-50% reduction in generation
Envelope Increased infiltration, failed weather stripping 5-15% increase

FDD Techniques:

  • Rule-Based: Expert-defined rules detecting known fault patterns
  • Model-Based: Comparison of actual performance to physics-based model predictions
  • Data-Driven: Machine learning algorithms identify anomalies in operational data
  • Hybrid: Combination of multiple approaches

3.6 Cybersecurity in BEMS

As BEMS become increasingly connected and cloud-enabled, cybersecurity becomes critical. A compromised BEMS could lead to discomfort, energy waste, or even life-safety issues.

3.6.1 BEMS Security Vulnerabilities

  • Weak Authentication: Default passwords, lack of multi-factor authentication
  • Unencrypted Communication: Sensitive data transmitted in clear text
  • Outdated Software: Unpatched vulnerabilities in controllers and management systems
  • Network Exposure: BEMS directly accessible from internet
  • Legacy Systems: Old equipment lacking security features

3.6.2 WIA-ENE-015 Security Requirements

Mandatory Security Measures

  • Network Segmentation: BEMS on separate VLAN from corporate network
  • Encryption: TLS/SSL for all external communication, optional but recommended for internal BACnet
  • Authentication: Strong passwords, multi-factor authentication for remote access
  • Access Control: Role-based permissions limiting user actions
  • Audit Logging: Comprehensive logging of all system changes and access attempts
  • Regular Updates: Process for timely patching of security vulnerabilities
  • Intrusion Detection: Monitoring for suspicious network activity

3.7 BEMS Case Study: Living Building Challenge Site

The Bullitt Center in Seattle exemplifies advanced BEMS implementation in a zero energy building. Completed in 2013, this 6-story commercial building achieves net-zero energy and water, and serves as a living laboratory for building performance.

BEMS Features:

  • Comprehensive Monitoring: Over 500 sensors tracking energy, water, indoor air quality, and occupant comfort
  • Real-Time Display: Public dashboard showing live building performance
  • Occupant Control: Individual workstation controls allow personalized comfort while BEMS manages overall efficiency
  • Weather Integration: BEMS uses weather forecasts to optimize thermal mass charging and natural ventilation
  • Solar Optimization: Battery dispatch algorithm maximizes solar self-consumption
  • Grid Services: Building participates in utility demand response program

Results:

  • Achieved net-zero energy for three consecutive years (2015-2017)
  • 38% lower energy use intensity than modeled, demonstrating importance of operations
  • BEMS-enabled adaptive control based on actual occupancy patterns
  • Detailed submetering enabled tenant engagement through energy feedback

Chapter Summary: Key Takeaways

  • BEMS are essential for zero energy buildings, enabling monitoring, control, optimization, and analytics
  • Architecture includes field level (sensors/actuators), automation level (controllers), management level (software), and enterprise level (cloud analytics)
  • Open protocols (BACnet, Modbus, MQTT) ensure interoperability and avoid vendor lock-in
  • Comprehensive submetering (minimum Levels 0-2) provides visibility into energy consumption
  • Advanced features include Model Predictive Control (10-30% savings), renewable optimization, demand response, and fault detection
  • Cybersecurity is critical with network segmentation, encryption, authentication, and access control
  • Real-world implementations demonstrate BEMS can improve performance beyond design predictions
  • WIA-ENE-015 requires open protocols, detailed metering, and robust security measures
