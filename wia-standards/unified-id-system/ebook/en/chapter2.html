Chapter 2: Current Identity Challenges

In this chapter: We examine the existing identity systems in North and South Korea, analyze interoperability challenges, privacy concerns, and the technical barriers that must be overcome to create a unified ID system.

2.1 South Korean Identity System

South Korea operates one of the world's most digitized identity systems, centered around the Resident Registration Number (RRN) introduced in 1968. While highly functional, the system faces significant privacy and security challenges.

Resident Registration Number (RRN) Structure

The 13-digit RRN format: YYMMDD-GNNNNNN

  • YYMMDD: Birth date (year, month, day)
  • G: Gender and century indicator (1/2 for 1900s, 3/4 for 2000s)
  • NNNNNN: Serial number and check digit

Privacy Problems with RRN

The RRN system has been criticized for privacy violations:

Critical Privacy Issues

  • Over-collection: Required for nearly all services, from banking to gaming
  • Data Breaches: Over 100 million RRN records leaked since 2000
  • Identity Theft: Stolen RRNs enable fraudulent account creation
  • Immutability: RRN cannot be changed, making breach impact permanent
  • Inference Attacks: Birth date and gender directly encoded in ID number

Recent Reforms and Alternatives

Initiative Description Status
i-PIN Alternative online identification number Low adoption due to inconvenience
Mobile ID Smartphone-based authentication Growing adoption for government services
Decentralized ID (DID) Blockchain-based self-sovereign identity Pilot projects in Busan and Seoul
MyData User-controlled data sharing Implemented in financial sector (2022)

2.2 North Korean Identity System

Information about North Korea's identity system is limited due to the country's isolation, but defector testimony and leaked documents provide insights into a fundamentally different approach.

Citizen Registration System

North Korea maintains a paper-based registration system managed by the Ministry of People's Security:

  • Registration Books: Physical ledgers maintained at neighborhood ("inminban") level
  • ID Cards: Paper or basic plastic cards with photo and basic information
  • Travel Permits: Required for inter-city travel, tracked separately
  • Ration Books: Household-level registration for food distribution

The Songbun System

North Korea's hereditary caste system is encoded into identity documents, creating severe social stratification:

Class Description Estimated Population
Core (핵심) Loyal to regime, descendants of revolutionaries ~25%
Wavering (동요) Neutral, merchants, former South Korean POWs ~55%
Hostile (적대) Landowners, religious, defector families ~20%

Songbun Implications for Unified ID

Any unified ID system must explicitly prevent the perpetuation of songbun-based discrimination. This requires:

  • Zero-knowledge verification to hide origin information
  • Legal prohibition on songbun-based data collection
  • Anonymization of historical records
  • Affirmative action to prevent algorithmic discrimination

Technology Gap

The digital divide between North and South Korean identity systems creates significant integration challenges:

Aspect South Korea North Korea Gap
Digitization 100% digital records ~5% digitized (estimates) Massive OCR/data entry required
Biometrics Fingerprint + facial recognition Limited, military/security only Population-scale biometric capture needed
Infrastructure Nationwide fiber/5G Limited internet, no consumer mobile data Offline-capable systems required
Trust in Digital High (99% smartphone ownership) Low (unfamiliarity, no access) Education and transition period needed

2.3 Interoperability Challenges

Creating a unified ID system requires solving multiple technical and semantic interoperability problems:

Data Format Incompatibility

  • Encoding: South uses digital databases; North uses paper records with inconsistent formats
  • Name Standards: Different romanization systems, potential character set issues
  • Address Systems: Completely different administrative divisions and addressing schemes
  • Date Formats: Both use YYYY-MM-DD, but North Korea officially uses Juche calendar (year 1 = 1912)

Semantic Differences

Even similar data fields have different meanings and usage patterns:

Field South Korea North Korea Harmonization Strategy
Residence Can change freely Restricted by permit Legal residence vs. current location
Occupation Self-reported, changeable State-assigned, tracked Current employment only, no historical tracking
Family Relations Nuclear family Extended family, songbun inheritance Immediate family only, optional extended

2.4 Privacy and Security Concerns

A unified ID system must balance multiple, sometimes conflicting, privacy requirements:

Protection from State Surveillance

North Korean citizens have legitimate fears of government tracking based on decades of oppressive surveillance. The unified system must provide:

  • Unlinkability: Prevent correlation of activities across services
  • Selective Disclosure: Share only required information for each transaction
  • Audit Trails: Citizens can see who accessed their data and when
  • Legal Protections: Constitutional guarantees against warrantless surveillance

Protection from Discrimination

The system must technically prevent discrimination based on origin:

Zero-Knowledge Origin Verification

Using cryptographic techniques, the system can prove a person is a citizen of unified Korea without revealing whether they originated from North or South. This prevents:

  • Employment discrimination against former North Koreans
  • Housing discrimination in desirable areas
  • Educational tracking based on origin
  • Informal caste systems from emerging

2.5 Family Reunification Challenges

One of the most emotionally significant uses of a unified ID system will be family reunification. Over 10 million Koreans were separated during the Korean War, and verifying family relationships across 70+ years presents unique challenges:

  • Name Changes: People may have changed names for various reasons (marriage, political pressure, new identity in South)
  • Missing Records: War destruction, defection, and poor record-keeping in North Korea
  • Multiple Generations: Original separated individuals are now elderly; verification may require DNA testing
  • False Claims: Property rights and inheritance may incentivize fraudulent family claims
  • Privacy: Some separated families may not want contact for personal or political reasons

Proposed Solutions in WIA-UNI-002

  1. DNA Registry: Voluntary genetic database for family matching
  2. Historical Records: Digitization of Red Cross family separation records
  3. Testimony Archive: Blockchain-anchored witness statements
  4. Privacy Preferences: Opt-in/opt-out for contact from claimed relatives
  5. Mediation Services: Professional support for reunification processes

2.6 Defector Protection

Approximately 34,000 North Korean defectors currently live in South Korea. A unified ID system must protect them from:

  • Retribution: Potential persecution for having defected
  • Family Pressure: Forced contact with family members still loyal to the North
  • Identity Exposure: Revealing their defector status to employers or neighbors
  • Extortion: Criminals threatening to reveal defector identity

2.7 Lessons from Failed Systems

Several identity integration efforts have failed or created new problems. WIA-UNI-002 learns from these mistakes:

Iraq: Sectarian Violence

Post-2003 Iraqi ID cards included ethnicity and religion fields, which enabled:

  • Targeted killings at checkpoints based on ID cards
  • Ethnic cleansing of mixed neighborhoods
  • Discrimination in employment and services

Lesson: Never encode protected characteristics in machine-readable ID fields.

India: Aadhaar Exclusion

India's biometric ID system inadvertently excluded millions:

  • Manual laborers with worn fingerprints couldn't enroll
  • Transgender individuals faced harassment during enrollment
  • Rural populations lacked access to enrollment centers
  • System outages prevented access to rations and benefits

Lesson: Design for edge cases and provide fallback mechanisms.

Estonia: Russian Minority Statelessness

Post-Soviet Estonia's citizenship rules left many Russian-speaking residents stateless:

  • Language requirements excluded elderly Russians
  • "Alien passports" created second-class citizenship
  • Decades of integration challenges

Lesson: Inclusive citizenship rules and language-agnostic systems.

Chapter Summary

Key Takeaways:

  • South Korea has a digital but privacy-problematic RRN system
  • North Korea relies on paper records and the oppressive songbun caste system
  • Technology gap requires offline-capable solutions and massive digitization effort
  • Privacy protections must prevent surveillance and discrimination
  • Family reunification requires DNA registry and historical record digitization
  • Defector protection demands identity sealing and legal amnesty
