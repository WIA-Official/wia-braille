Chapter 2: Core Concepts and Terminology

Understanding solar energy systems requires familiarity with specialized terminology and fundamental concepts that span physics, electrical engineering, and data science. This chapter provides comprehensive definitions and explanations of the key terms and concepts used throughout the WIA-ENE-005 standard, establishing a common vocabulary for all stakeholders.

Photovoltaic Fundamentals

At the heart of solar energy systems is the photovoltaic effect—the conversion of light energy into electrical energy. Understanding this process and its associated terminology is essential for working with solar systems.

Photovoltaic Effect

Photovoltaic (PV) Effect
The physical process by which a solar cell converts sunlight into electricity. When photons strike a semiconductor material, they transfer their energy to electrons, creating electron-hole pairs. This generates a voltage difference that drives electrical current when a circuit is connected.

Solar Cell Architecture

Modern solar cells employ sophisticated semiconductor structures to maximize conversion efficiency:

Cell Type Material Typical Efficiency Characteristics
Monocrystalline Silicon Single-crystal Si 18-24% High efficiency, uniform appearance, higher cost
Polycrystalline Silicon Multi-crystal Si 15-19% Lower cost, blue mottled appearance
PERC Passivated emitter Si 20-22% Enhanced rear surface, better low-light performance
Thin Film CdTe Cadmium Telluride 11-16% Low cost, good high-temp performance
Thin Film CIGS Copper Indium Gallium Selenide 13-18% Flexible substrate options
Bifacial Various Si types 19-23% (front) Captures light from both sides

Energy Conversion and Efficiency Metrics

Solar system performance is quantified using several key metrics that measure how effectively sunlight is converted to usable electricity.

Solar Panel Efficiency

Panel Efficiency (η)
The ratio of electrical power output to incident solar power input, typically measured under Standard Test Conditions (STC). Expressed as a percentage.
η = (Pout / (E × A)) × 100%
where Pout = power output (W), E = irradiance (W/m²), A = panel area (m²)

Standard Test Conditions (STC)

To enable fair comparison between solar panels, performance is measured under standardized conditions:

  • Irradiance: 1000 W/m² (equivalent to bright midday sun)
  • Cell Temperature: 25°C (77°F)
  • Air Mass: 1.5 (sun at 42° above horizon)
  • Spectral Distribution: Standard solar spectrum
Important: Real-world performance typically differs from STC ratings due to varying environmental conditions. Temperature coefficients and performance ratios account for these differences.

Performance Ratio (PR)

Performance Ratio
The ratio of actual energy output to theoretical maximum energy output under real-world conditions. A well-designed system typically achieves PR of 75-85%. Higher values indicate better system design and fewer losses.
PR = (Actual Energy Output) / (Theoretical STC Output × Actual Irradiation)

Electrical Characteristics

Solar panels and systems are characterized by specific electrical parameters that define their operating characteristics and performance.

I-V Curve and Operating Points

The current-voltage (I-V) curve describes the relationship between current and voltage for a solar panel under given conditions. Key points on this curve include:

Parameter Symbol Description Typical Range
Short-Circuit Current Isc Current when voltage is zero 8-11 A (residential panel)
Open-Circuit Voltage Voc Voltage when current is zero 35-45 V (residential panel)
Maximum Power Point Current Imp Current at maximum power 7-10 A (residential panel)
Maximum Power Point Voltage Vmp Voltage at maximum power 30-40 V (residential panel)
Maximum Power Pmax Peak power output (Imp × Vmp) 250-400 W (residential panel)

Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT)

MPPT
An electronic system that continuously adjusts the electrical load to ensure the solar array operates at its maximum power point despite changing environmental conditions. Modern inverters incorporate sophisticated MPPT algorithms that can track the optimal operating point with 99%+ efficiency.

Temperature Coefficients

Solar panel performance varies with temperature. Temperature coefficients quantify this relationship:

  • Power Temperature Coefficient: Typically -0.35% to -0.45% per °C. Panels lose efficiency as temperature increases above 25°C STC.
  • Voltage Temperature Coefficient: Typically -0.3% per °C. Voltage decreases with increasing temperature.
  • Current Temperature Coefficient: Typically +0.05% per °C. Current slightly increases with temperature.
Pactual = PSTC × [1 + γ × (Tcell - 25°C)]
where γ = power temperature coefficient (%/°C)

System Components and Architecture

A complete solar energy system comprises multiple interconnected components, each with specific functions and terminology.

Inverter Types and Topologies

Inverter Type Application Advantages Considerations
String Inverter Residential, Commercial Cost-effective, simple installation Single point of failure, panel-level shading issues
Microinverter Residential Panel-level optimization, redundancy Higher cost, more components
Central Inverter Utility-scale Economies of scale, high efficiency Requires high DC voltage, single failure impact
Hybrid Inverter All scales with storage Integrated battery management More complex, higher initial cost

Balance of System (BOS)

Balance of System
All components of a solar energy system except the photovoltaic panels themselves. This includes mounting structures, wiring, combiner boxes, disconnects, metering equipment, and monitoring systems. BOS typically represents 40-60% of total system cost.

Array Configuration

Solar panels are connected in series and parallel to achieve desired voltage and current levels:

String
Multiple solar panels connected in series. String voltage equals the sum of individual panel voltages, while current remains constant. Typical residential strings contain 8-14 panels.
Array
Multiple strings connected in parallel to form the complete solar installation. Array current equals the sum of string currents, while voltage remains constant (equal to string voltage).

Energy Storage Terminology

Battery storage is increasingly integrated with solar systems, necessitating additional terminology for energy storage concepts.

Battery Characteristics

Term Definition Typical Values
Capacity Total energy storage (kWh or Ah) 5-20 kWh (residential)
Usable Capacity Energy available for discharge (accounting for depth of discharge limits) 80-95% of total capacity
Power Rating Maximum charge/discharge rate (kW) 3-10 kW (residential)
Round-Trip Efficiency Ratio of energy out to energy in 85-95% (lithium-ion)
Cycle Life Number of charge/discharge cycles before degradation 3,000-10,000 cycles
Depth of Discharge (DoD) Percentage of capacity that can be used 80-100%

State of Charge (SoC)

State of Charge
The current energy level of a battery expressed as a percentage of total capacity. SoC = 100% indicates fully charged, SoC = 0% indicates fully discharged. Battery management systems continuously monitor and report SoC to optimize charging/discharging strategies.

Solar Resource and Environmental Terms

Accurate characterization of solar resources and environmental conditions is critical for system design and performance prediction.

Solar Irradiance and Insolation

Solar Irradiance
The instantaneous power density of sunlight, measured in watts per square meter (W/m²). Typical values range from 0 W/m² (night) to 1000 W/m² (clear sky, direct sun).
Solar Insolation
The total solar energy received over a specific time period, typically expressed in kilowatt-hours per square meter per day (kWh/m²/day). Annual insolation varies from about 3 kWh/m²/day in northern regions to 7+ kWh/m²/day in desert areas.

Solar Position and Geometry

Term Definition Impact on Performance
Azimuth Angle Horizontal direction of the sun or panel (0° = North, 90° = East, 180° = South, 270° = West) Determines optimal panel orientation
Altitude Angle Vertical angle of the sun above the horizon (0-90°) Varies with time of day and season
Tilt Angle Angle of solar panel from horizontal (0° = flat, 90° = vertical) Optimized based on latitude and seasonal priorities
Zenith Angle Angle between sun and vertical (90° - altitude angle) Affects air mass and spectral distribution
Incidence Angle Angle between sunlight and panel surface perpendicular 0° provides maximum energy capture

Peak Sun Hours (PSH)

Peak Sun Hours
A normalized measure of daily solar radiation, representing the equivalent number of hours with irradiance at 1000 W/m². For example, 5 PSH means the total daily solar energy equals 5 hours at full sun intensity, equivalent to 5 kWh/m²/day of insolation.

Grid Integration Concepts

Solar systems that connect to the electrical grid must conform to specific requirements and terminology related to grid interaction.

Grid-Connected vs. Off-Grid

Grid-Connected (Grid-Tied) System
A solar system that connects to the utility electrical grid. Excess energy is exported to the grid, and grid power supplements solar when needed. These systems typically don't require batteries, as the grid provides backup power.
Off-Grid (Stand-Alone) System
A solar system that operates independently without grid connection. Requires battery storage to provide power when solar production is insufficient. Common in remote locations where grid connection is impractical or unavailable.

Net Metering and Energy Compensation

Net Metering
A billing arrangement where excess solar energy exported to the grid is credited against energy consumed from the grid, typically at retail electricity rates. Net metering policies vary significantly by jurisdiction and utility company.
Feed-in Tariff (FiT)
A policy mechanism that pays solar system owners a fixed rate for electricity fed into the grid, often guaranteed for a specific contract period (10-20 years). FiT rates may differ from retail electricity prices.

Anti-Islanding Protection

Anti-Islanding
A safety feature that automatically disconnects solar inverters from the grid during power outages. This prevents "islanding"—a dangerous condition where the solar system continues to energize grid lines that utility workers assume are de-energized. Required by grid interconnection standards.

Performance Monitoring and Data

Modern solar systems generate extensive operational data that enables performance optimization and issue detection.

Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

KPI Description Target Range
Specific Yield Annual energy production per kW of installed capacity (kWh/kWp/year) 900-1800 (climate dependent)
Capacity Factor Ratio of actual to theoretical maximum annual output 12-25%
System Availability Percentage of time system is operational >98%
Inverter Efficiency DC to AC conversion efficiency 95-99%
Degradation Rate Annual reduction in panel output 0.3-0.8% per year

Data Acquisition and SCADA

SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition)
Industrial control system architecture used in large solar installations for real-time monitoring, data collection, and remote control. SCADA systems aggregate data from multiple sensors and devices, providing comprehensive system visibility and enabling automated control strategies.

WIA-ENE-005 Specific Terminology

The WIA-ENE-005 standard introduces specific terminology for its standardized data formats and protocols.

Data Objects

Solar Data Point (SDP)
A standardized data structure defined by WIA-ENE-005 that represents a single measurement or observation from a solar system component. SDPs include timestamp, device identifier, measurement type, value, and quality indicators. 
{
  "sdp": {
    "timestamp": "2025-12-25T14:30:00Z",
    "deviceId": "INV-001",
    "measurement": "AC_POWER",
    "value": 4850,
    "unit": "W",
    "quality": "GOOD",
    "standard": "WIA-ENE-005"
  }
}
System Profile
A comprehensive description of a solar installation's configuration, including panel specifications, inverter characteristics, array geometry, and expected performance parameters. System profiles enable standardized comparison and analysis across different installations.

Compliance Levels

WIA-ENE-005 defines three compliance levels that systems may achieve:

  • Level 1 - Basic Compliance: Minimum data reporting requirements, essential safety features
  • Level 2 - Standard Compliance: Comprehensive monitoring, standard data formats, basic analytics
  • Level 3 - Advanced Compliance: Full integration capabilities, predictive analytics, optimization features
