Chapter 5

System Design and Installation

WIA-ENE-005 Standard · English Edition

From Concept to Commissioning

Designing and installing a solar energy system requires balancing technical requirements, economic constraints, aesthetic considerations, and local regulations. Whether a residential rooftop array or a utility-scale solar farm, successful projects follow systematic processes ensuring safety, performance, and longevity.

The WIA-ENE-005 standard provides comprehensive guidelines for system design, covering site assessment, component selection, electrical design, structural engineering, and installation best practices. This chapter explores the complete lifecycle from initial feasibility through final commissioning.

Site Assessment and Feasibility

Every successful solar project begins with thorough site assessment. This critical phase determines technical feasibility and economic viability.

Solar Resource Analysis

Quantifying available solar energy requires analyzing historical weather data, considering geographic location, seasonal variations, and local climate patterns. Tools like PVGIS, NREL's PVWatts, and commercial platforms provide detailed solar resource information.

Key metrics include:

  • Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI): Total solar radiation received on a horizontal surface, measured in kWh/m²/day. Values range from 3-4 in cloudy northern regions to over 7 in sunny deserts.
  • Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI): Solar radiation received perpendicular to the sun's rays, excluding scattered light. Critical for concentrated solar systems.
  • Diffuse Horizontal Irradiance (DHI): Scattered radiation from clouds and atmosphere. Important for understanding performance during overcast conditions.
  • Peak Sun Hours: Equivalent hours per day of 1000 W/m² irradiance. A location receiving 5 kWh/m²/day has 5 peak sun hours.

Shading Analysis

Shading dramatically impacts solar system performance. Even partial shade on a single panel can reduce string output by 50% or more. Comprehensive shading analysis uses:

Sun Path Diagrams: Plotting the sun's trajectory throughout the year reveals when trees, buildings, or terrain cause shading. Digital tools like SolarPathfinder or Solmetric SunEye accelerate this process.

3D Modeling: Software platforms create detailed site models including all potential shade sources. These tools simulate shading hour-by-hour throughout the year, quantifying annual energy losses.

Drone Imagery and LiDAR: Advanced projects use aerial surveying to create precise digital elevation models, enabling highly accurate shading analysis for complex sites.

Structural Assessment

Solar installations must safely withstand environmental loads for 25+ years. Structural evaluation considers:

  • Roof Condition and Age: Installing solar on roofs near end-of-life is uneconomical. The roof should last as long as the solar system.
  • Load Capacity: Solar panels, racking, and potential snow accumulation add 3-5 pounds per square foot. Older buildings may require reinforcement.
  • Roof Type and Pitch: Different mounting systems suit different roof types (composition shingle, tile, metal, flat membrane). Steeper pitches may require specialized equipment for safe installation.
  • Wind and Snow Loads: Local building codes specify design loads based on historical weather extremes. Coastal and mountainous regions require particularly robust designs.

System Sizing and Configuration

Optimal system size balances available space, budget, energy needs, and economic incentives.

Energy Consumption Analysis

Understanding load profiles guides system sizing. Analyzing 12 months of utility bills reveals:

  • Annual consumption (kWh/year)
  • Seasonal variations (heating/cooling impacts)
  • Time-of-use patterns (if applicable to rate structure)
  • Future changes (electric vehicle, heat pump, growing family)

Systems can be sized to offset 100% of consumption (net-zero), a specific percentage, or to maximize economic return given budget constraints.

Array Sizing Calculations

Basic system sizing follows this process:

Step 1: Determine Daily Energy Need
Annual consumption ÷ 365 = Daily average (kWh/day)

Step 2: Account for System Losses
Typical losses (soiling, temperature, wiring, inverter, age) total 15-25%
Derate factor = 0.75 to 0.85 (conservative to optimistic)

Step 3: Calculate Required Array Size
Array size (kW) = Daily need (kWh/day) ÷ Peak sun hours ÷ Derate factor

Example:
Annual consumption: 10,000 kWh
Daily average: 27.4 kWh/day
Peak sun hours: 5 hours/day
Derate factor: 0.8
Required array: 27.4 ÷ 5 ÷ 0.8 = 6.85 kW
Rounded to available panel sizes: 7.2 kW (18 × 400W panels)

Tilt and Orientation Optimization

Panel angle and direction significantly impact production. General guidelines:

Location Optimal Tilt Orientation Production vs Flat
Northern Hemisphere = Latitude ± 15° South-facing +15% to +25%
Southern Hemisphere = Latitude ± 15° North-facing +15% to +25%
Tropics 10-15° (for rain cleaning) Less critical +5% to +15%

However, economic optimization may favor different angles. Time-of-use rates with afternoon peaks might justify west-facing arrays, sacrificing some total production for better peak alignment. Roof-mounted systems often use existing roof pitch rather than optimal tilt to reduce costs and aesthetic impact.

Electrical System Design

Electrical design ensures safe, efficient power delivery from panels to inverter to electrical service.

String Design

Panels connect in series to form strings. String design must satisfy:

Voltage Constraints:

  • Maximum system voltage (typically 600V residential, 1000V commercial, 1500V utility)
  • Inverter MPPT voltage window (panels must operate within this range)
  • Temperature effects (voltage increases in cold weather, potentially exceeding limits)

Current Constraints:

  • Inverter maximum input current per MPPT channel
  • Wire ampacity (current-carrying capacity)
  • Overcurrent protection device ratings

Power Matching:

  • All strings on one MPPT input should have similar power ratings (within ±5%)
  • Similar orientation and tilt to avoid mismatch losses
  • Identical panel models within each string

Wire Sizing

Proper conductor sizing balances safety and efficiency. Undersized wires create fire hazards; oversized wires waste money. The National Electrical Code (NEC) and local regulations specify minimum sizes based on:

  • Current capacity (ampacity): Wires must handle maximum current without overheating. Solar conductors operate in harsh environments (hot roofs, direct sun) requiring derating.
  • Voltage drop: Resistance causes voltage loss along conductors. NEC recommends limiting voltage drop to 3% from array to inverter. Longer wire runs require larger conductors to minimize losses.
  • Temperature ratings: Solar applications use wire rated 90°C or higher (THWN-2, USE-2, PV wire).

Overcurrent Protection and Disconnects

Multiple protection and disconnect devices ensure safety:

  • DC Disconnects: Allow isolating array from inverter for maintenance. Required to be accessible and lockable.
  • AC Disconnect: Isolates inverter from electrical panel. Must be visible from meter location.
  • Overcurrent Protection: Fuses or breakers protect conductors from short circuits and ground faults.
  • Ground Fault Protection: Detects and interrupts ground faults before they cause fires or shock hazards.
  • Arc Fault Protection: Required on most systems since 2011, detects and interrupts dangerous arcing conditions.

Mechanical Design and Installation

Proper mounting ensures panels survive decades of wind, rain, snow, and temperature extremes.

Mounting Systems

Racking systems must attach securely to roof structure (not just sheathing) and allow for thermal expansion. Common residential mounting types include:

Rail-Based Systems: Aluminum rails run parallel to roof ridge. Panels clamp to rails. Simple, versatile, cost-effective. Most common residential approach.

Rail-Less Systems: Direct mounting hardware attaches panels to roof without continuous rails. Reduces material and labor but less flexible for layout.

Ballasted Systems: For flat roofs, concrete or steel ballast holds racking through gravity alone, avoiding roof penetrations. Heavy and may exceed load capacity on older buildings.

Ground Mounts: Driven pilings, concrete piers, or helical screws anchor arrays to earth. Common for utility-scale installations and when roof space is insufficient.

Installation Best Practices

Quality installation determines system longevity and performance:

Roof Penetrations: Every penetration is a potential leak. Use proper flashing, sealants rated for 20+ year outdoor exposure, and proven waterproofing methods. Consider innovative attachment methods (QuickMount, Zilla Mounts) that improve waterproofing.

Conduit and Wire Management: Protect wiring from UV exposure, physical damage, and water intrusion. Maintain code-required clearances. Use UV-rated cable ties; standard ties become brittle and fail.

Grounding: Proper equipment grounding protects against lightning and faults. Bond all metallic components; use listed grounding hardware; verify continuity. Ground fault protection detects when grounding fails.

Module Installation: Handle panels carefully to avoid microcracks. Follow manufacturer torque specifications for clamps – too tight damages frames, too loose allows movement. Orient panels per design (typically landscape for residential, portrait for commercial). Leave appropriate spacing for airflow and thermal expansion.

Permitting and Inspection

Solar installations require various permits and inspections ensuring code compliance and safety.

Typical Permitting Requirements

  • Building Permit: Required for structural and electrical work. Includes plan review, fee payment, and inspection.
  • Electrical Permit: Sometimes separate from building permit, covers all electrical work.
  • Utility Interconnection Agreement: Permission to connect to grid and establish net metering. Utility may require additional equipment (external disconnect, revenue meter upgrade).
  • Homeowners Association (HOA) Approval: If applicable, though many jurisdictions limit HOA power to restrict solar.
  • Historic District or Landmark Approval: Additional requirements may apply for historically significant properties.

Inspection Process

Inspections verify compliance with codes, permits, and standards:

Rough-In Inspection: Before covers are installed, inspectors verify conduit, wire sizing, grounding, penetration sealing, and structural attachments.

Final Inspection: Complete installation review checking labeling, disconnects, overcurrent protection, conductor terminations, array installation quality, and overall code compliance.

Utility Inspection: Many utilities conduct separate inspections before granting permission to operate (PTO). They verify meter installation, disconnect accessibility, and protection settings.

Commissioning and Performance Verification

Commissioning confirms the system operates as designed and meets performance expectations.

Startup Testing

Before connecting to grid, verify:

  • Correct string voltages and polarities
  • Ground fault protection operation
  • Arc fault detection functioning
  • Inverter configuration (voltage, frequency, grid code compliance)
  • Communication and monitoring systems operational

Performance Testing

Under good sun conditions, measure:

  • DC power from each string
  • AC power output
  • System efficiency vs. expectations
  • Compare to design predictions accounting for current conditions

The WIA-ENE-005 standard requires documentation of commissioning results, providing baseline for future performance comparison.

Documentation and Owner Training

Comprehensive documentation and owner education ensure long-term success:

  • Complete system specifications and component datasheets
  • As-built electrical and structural drawings
  • Warranty information for all major components
  • Operation and maintenance procedures
  • Monitoring system access and interpretation
  • Troubleshooting guide for common issues
  • Contact information for service and support

Training owners to understand their system, monitor performance, and identify problems early maximizes lifetime energy production and return on investment.
