Defining and Classifying AI Bias

AI bias refers to algorithms systematically producing inaccurate or unfair results for specific groups. In medical AI, bias is especially serious as it directly impacts lives.

Types of Bias

  • Data Bias: Training data skewed toward certain populations, failing to reflect actual demographics
  • Algorithmic Bias: Model design favoring or disfavoring certain groups through optimization choices
  • Historical Bias: AI learning and reproducing past discrimination and inequality embedded in data
  • Measurement Bias: Disease definitions, diagnostic criteria, or measurement tools developed for specific groups being inaccurate for others
  • Selection Bias: Certain groups systematically excluded or underrepresented during data collection

Real Cases of Medical AI Bias

Case 1: Healthcare Resource Allocation AI Racial Discrimination (2019)

Research on AI used by US hospitals for millions of patients revealed Black patients with equal disease severity were significantly less likely to be classified as high-risk compared to White patients.

Obermeyer et al. Science (2019) Findings:

• Analysis: Major US hospital AI system, ~70 million patient records
• Problem: AI used "future healthcare costs" as proxy for health status
• Result: Black patients with same chronic conditions spent average $1,800 less on healthcare
• Cause: Healthcare access gaps, insurance differences, structural racism
• Impact: 46.5% of Black patients didn't receive needed additional care

Retraining the algorithm on "health status" instead reduced bias by 84%.

Case 2: Skin Cancer Detection AI Skin Tone Bias

Independent studies by Stanford and MIT found several skin cancer detection AI systems showed stark performance differences across skin tones: 91.3% accuracy for light skin vs. 60.7% for dark skin—a 30.6% gap.

Case 3: Pulse Oximeter Racial Bias

During COVID-19, 2020 research revealed widely-used pulse oximeters overestimated oxygen saturation in Black patients, failing to detect hypoxemia 3x more often than in White patients.

How Bias Amplifies Healthcare Disparities

Mechanisms:

  • Diagnostic Gap: AI failing to detect disease early in certain groups delays treatment and worsens outcomes
  • Treatment Access Gap: Biased patient screening systems deny minority patients access to advanced treatments or clinical trials
  • Resource Allocation Inequity: Biased AI in identifying high-risk patients misdirects healthcare resources away from those who truly need them
  • Trust Erosion: Repeated misdiagnosis or discrimination experiences with AI systems undermine trust in healthcare overall
  • Self-Fulfilling Prophecy: Biased AI giving low risk scores to certain groups → they don't receive preventive care → they experience worse outcomes → data fed back to AI reinforces bias

Bias Mitigation Strategies

1. Data-Level Interventions

  • Representative Data Collection: Deliberately include all populations
  • Data Augmentation: Synthetically generate underrepresented group data
  • Resampling: Oversample minority groups or undersample majority groups

2. Algorithm-Level Interventions

  • Fairness Constraints: Add fairness metrics as constraints or penalties during training
  • Adversarial Debiasing: Train models so protected attributes cannot be inferred from predictions
  • Group-Specific Thresholds: Use different decision thresholds per group to achieve fairness

3. Post-Processing Interventions

Adjust trained model outputs to meet fairness criteria.

4. Causal Inference Approaches

Model causal relationships rather than correlations to eliminate spurious associations causing bias.

Ethical Considerations and Limitations

1. Multiple Definitions of Fairness

Satisfying multiple fairness metrics simultaneously is often mathematically impossible. Ethical judgment is needed to prioritize which fairness to pursue.

2. Accuracy-Fairness Tradeoff

Increasing fairness may slightly decrease overall accuracy. In medicine, this requires stakeholder consensus.

3. Dilemma of Using Sensitive Attributes

Measuring and mitigating bias requires collecting race, gender, etc.—but this may conflict with privacy and anti-discrimination laws.
