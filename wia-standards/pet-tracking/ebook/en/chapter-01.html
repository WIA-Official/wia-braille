Chapter 1: Introduction to Pet Tracking Standards

"弘益人間 (Hong-ik Ingan): Technology serves humanity best when it protects those who cannot protect themselves—including our cherished animal companions."

1.1 The Need for Pet Tracking Standards

Every year, millions of pets go missing worldwide. In the United States alone, over 10 million dogs and cats are lost or stolen annually. Tragically, only 23% of lost dogs and 2% of lost cats without identification are ever reunited with their owners. Traditional identification methods like tags and microchips, while valuable, have significant limitations: tags can fall off, and microchips require the pet to be found and scanned at a facility with the proper equipment.

Real-time GPS tracking technology has emerged as a powerful solution to this crisis, enabling pet owners to locate their companions instantly from anywhere in the world. However, the pet tracking industry has evolved without unified standards, creating fragmentation and interoperability challenges. Different manufacturers use proprietary protocols, incompatible data formats, and closed ecosystems that lock users into specific platforms.

The Problem of Fragmentation

Challenge Impact on Users Impact on Industry
Proprietary Protocols Cannot switch devices or apps easily Limited market competition
Incompatible Data Formats Cannot share data with vets or services Reduced innovation opportunities
Closed Ecosystems Vendor lock-in, higher costs Barriers to new entrants
Lack of Standards Quality varies widely Consumer confusion and distrust
Privacy Concerns Unknown data handling practices Regulatory compliance risks

The WIA-PET-TRACKING standard addresses these challenges by establishing open, interoperable protocols that benefit both users and the industry while prioritizing pet safety and owner peace of mind.

1.2 Core Objectives of WIA-PET-TRACKING

The WIA-PET-TRACKING standard is designed with clear objectives that serve the interests of all stakeholders: pet owners, veterinarians, manufacturers, developers, and most importantly, the pets themselves.

Primary Objectives

  1. Safety First: Maximize the chances of lost pet recovery through reliable, accurate tracking
  2. Interoperability: Enable seamless integration across devices, apps, and services
  3. Privacy Protection: Safeguard location data and personal information
  4. Battery Efficiency: Balance tracking frequency with device longevity
  5. Global Compatibility: Support worldwide tracking across multiple GNSS systems and networks
  6. Open Access: Provide free, open standards accessible to all implementers
  7. Innovation Enablement: Create a foundation for future technological advances
  8. User Empowerment: Give pet owners control over their data and devices

1.3 Historical Context: Evolution of Pet Tracking

Understanding the evolution of pet tracking technology provides important context for the current standard and highlights how far the technology has progressed.

Timeline of Pet Identification and Tracking

Era Technology Capabilities Limitations
1950s-1970s ID Tags Name and phone number on collar Easily lost; requires finder to call
1980s-1990s Microchips (RFID) Permanent ID under skin Passive only; requires scanner; no location
2000-2010 Early GPS Trackers Active location tracking Large, heavy; poor battery life; expensive
2010-2015 Smartphone-Connected GPS Real-time updates; smaller devices Proprietary apps; limited battery; high cost
2015-2020 LTE & Multi-GNSS Trackers Global coverage; improved accuracy Subscription fees; incompatible systems
2020-Present Integrated Smart Devices Health + location; long battery; affordable Lack of standards; privacy concerns
2025+ WIA-PET-TRACKING Standard Interoperable; standardized; secure Adoption by industry needed

The GPS Revolution

The introduction of GPS (Global Positioning System) technology to consumer devices in the early 2000s transformed pet tracking from a passive to an active capability. Instead of waiting for someone to find and scan a lost pet, owners could now actively locate their animals in real-time.

However, early GPS trackers faced significant challenges:

  • Size and Weight: Early devices weighed 200-400 grams, too heavy for cats and small dogs
  • Battery Life: Initial models lasted only 2-4 hours on a single charge
  • Cost: Devices cost $300-500 plus monthly subscription fees of $20-40
  • Accuracy: GPS-only systems had 30-50 meter accuracy in ideal conditions
  • Coverage: Required cellular networks, limiting rural and international use

Over the past two decades, technological advances have addressed many of these limitations. Modern trackers weigh as little as 25-50 grams, last 24-72 hours per charge, cost $50-150, and achieve 5-10 meter accuracy using multi-GNSS systems. However, the lack of standardization remains a critical challenge that WIA-PET-TRACKING addresses.

1.4 Key Technologies in Modern Pet Tracking

Modern pet tracking systems integrate multiple technologies to provide reliable, accurate, and efficient location services. Understanding these technologies is essential for implementing the WIA-PET-TRACKING standard.

Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS)

Multiple satellite constellations provide global positioning capabilities:

System Country/Region Satellites Coverage Accuracy
GPS USA 31 Global 5-10m
GLONASS Russia 24 Global 5-10m
Galileo European Union 30 Global 1-5m
BeiDou China 35 Global 5-10m
QZSS Japan 7 Asia-Pacific 1-5m

Multi-GNSS devices that use signals from multiple systems simultaneously achieve better accuracy (3-5m) and faster time-to-first-fix, especially in challenging environments like urban canyons or dense forests.

Communication Networks

Pet trackers use various communication technologies to transmit location data:

NETWORK COMPARISON
==================

4G LTE:
  Coverage:    Excellent in urban/suburban areas
  Range:       5-50 km from tower
  Power:       Medium (10-20 mA transmit)
  Latency:     50-100 ms
  Data Rate:   1-50 Mbps
  Best For:    Real-time tracking in developed areas

NB-IoT (Narrowband IoT):
  Coverage:    Wide, penetrates buildings well
  Range:       10-50 km from tower
  Power:       Low (5-10 mA transmit)
  Latency:     1-10 seconds
  Data Rate:   20-250 kbps
  Best For:    Battery-efficient tracking

LoRaWAN:
  Coverage:    Long-range, limited infrastructure
  Range:       2-20 km (up to 50 km rural)
  Power:       Very low (1-5 mA transmit)
  Latency:     1-60 seconds
  Data Rate:   0.3-50 kbps
  Best For:    Rural areas, ultra-long battery life

Bluetooth LE:
  Coverage:    Short-range proximity
  Range:       10-100 meters
  Power:       Ultra-low (<1 mA)
  Latency:     10-100 ms
  Data Rate:   125-2000 kbps
  Best For:    Indoor tracking, home monitoring

Wi-Fi Positioning:
  Coverage:    Indoor and urban areas
  Range:       50-200 meters
  Power:       Medium (15-30 mA)
  Latency:     100-500 ms
  Data Rate:   1-100 Mbps
  Best For:    Indoor location, backup positioning

Hybrid Positioning Systems

The most effective pet trackers combine multiple positioning technologies for optimal results:

  1. GNSS (Outdoor): Primary positioning method for outdoor tracking
  2. Wi-Fi Positioning (Indoor/Urban): Uses known Wi-Fi access point locations
  3. Cellular Triangulation (Backup): Uses cell tower locations when GNSS unavailable
  4. Bluetooth Beacons (Indoor): Precise indoor positioning in equipped areas
  5. Accelerometer/Gyroscope (Motion): Dead reckoning when other signals lost

1.5 Standard Architecture Overview

The WIA-PET-TRACKING standard is organized into four phases, each building upon the previous layer to create a complete, interoperable ecosystem.

Four-Phase Architecture

+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    PHASE 4: INTEGRATION                           |
|  - Cross-platform compatibility    - Certification requirements  |
|  - Veterinary system integration   - Testing and validation       |
|  - Lost pet recovery networks      - Best practices and guides    |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                  ↑
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    PHASE 3: PROTOCOL                              |
|  - Communication protocols         - Geofence detection logic     |
|  - Data transmission standards     - Power management strategies  |
|  - Real-time streaming             - Network failover mechanisms  |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                  ↑
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    PHASE 2: API INTERFACE                         |
|  - REST APIs for tracking          - WebSocket streams            |
|  - Geofence management             - Alert notification APIs      |
|  - Device configuration            - History and analytics        |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                  ↑
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    PHASE 1: DATA FORMAT                           |
|  - Location data structures        - Geofence definitions         |
|  - Device metadata                 - Track history formats        |
|  - Timestamp standards             - Error and status codes       |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+

Phase Descriptions

Phase 1: Data Format establishes the fundamental data structures for representing location data, geofences, device information, and tracking history. This phase defines JSON schemas, data types, coordinate systems, and encoding standards that all implementations must follow.

Phase 2: API Interface defines the application programming interfaces that enable software applications to interact with tracking devices and services. This includes REST APIs for device management, WebSocket protocols for real-time location streams, and standardized endpoints for geofencing and alerts.

Phase 3: Protocol specifies the communication protocols, transmission strategies, and operational procedures for pet tracking systems. This phase covers GPS data collection, cellular/IoT communication, battery optimization, and geofence violation detection algorithms.

Phase 4: Integration provides guidelines for integrating pet tracking systems with external platforms including veterinary systems, lost pet recovery networks, pet care services, and consumer applications. This phase also defines certification requirements and testing procedures.

1.6 Standard Scope and Applicability

The WIA-PET-TRACKING standard is designed to be comprehensive yet flexible, supporting a wide range of use cases and implementation approaches.

In Scope

  • Real-time GPS tracking for dogs, cats, and other companion animals
  • Geofencing with customizable safe zones and alert triggers
  • Lost pet alert and recovery coordination systems
  • Location history tracking and analytics
  • Device management and configuration
  • Privacy-preserving location sharing
  • Multi-device and multi-pet tracking
  • Battery optimization and power management
  • Indoor and outdoor positioning
  • Global coverage across multiple GNSS and cellular networks

Out of Scope

  • Pet health monitoring (see WIA-PET-HEALTH-PASSPORT)
  • Pet emotion recognition (see WIA-PET-EMOTION)
  • Pet care robotics (see WIA-PET-CARE-ROBOT)
  • Livestock or wildlife tracking (different requirements)
  • Vehicle tracking or human tracking (separate standards)
  • Payment processing or e-commerce features

Supported Device Types

Device Type Form Factor Typical Weight Best For
GPS Collar Integrated into collar 50-150g Dogs, larger cats
Clip-on Tracker Attaches to existing collar 25-75g Dogs, cats
Smart Tag Lightweight tag 15-30g Cats, small dogs
Harness-Integrated Built into harness 100-200g (total) Active dogs
Pet Wearable Multi-function device 30-100g Health + location tracking

The WIA-PET-TRACKING standard defines three conformance levels to accommodate different implementation needs and device capabilities.

Implementations must clearly state their conformance level and may support features from higher levels without full conformance to that level.

1.8 Document Conventions

This standard uses specific terminology and conventions throughout all chapters.

Key Terms

  • SHALL/MUST: Indicates an absolute requirement for conformance
  • SHOULD: Indicates a strong recommendation; deviations must be justified
  • MAY: Indicates an optional feature or approach
  • Tracker: The physical device that determines and transmits location data
  • Client: Software application (mobile app, web portal) that consumes tracking data
  • Server: Backend system that receives, stores, and distributes tracking data
  • Geofence: A virtual geographic boundary defined by GPS coordinates
  • GNSS: Global Navigation Satellite System (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou)
