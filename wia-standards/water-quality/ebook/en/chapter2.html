Chapter 2: Water Quality Parameters and Testing Methods

WIA-ENE-018 Standard | Comprehensive Parameter Guide

2.1 Understanding Water Quality Parameters

Water quality is a multi-dimensional concept encompassing physical, chemical, and biological characteristics that determine water's suitability for specific uses. The WIA-ENE-018 standard organizes these parameters into a comprehensive monitoring framework that addresses all aspects of water quality assessment. This chapter provides detailed information about each parameter, its significance, measurement methods, and interpretation guidelines.

2.2 pH - The Master Variable

📊 Parameter Overview

Definition: pH measures the hydrogen ion concentration, indicating whether water is acidic, neutral, or alkaline.

Scale: 0-14 (7 is neutral, <7 is acidic, >7 is alkaline)

Drinking Water Standard: 6.5-8.5 (WHO, EPA guidelines)

Significance of pH

pH is often called the "master variable" in water chemistry because it influences virtually every chemical and biological process occurring in water. The pH level affects:

  • Chemical Speciation: pH determines the chemical forms of dissolved substances. For example, ammonia exists as toxic NH₃ at high pH and as less toxic NH₄⁺ at low pH.
  • Disinfection Efficiency: Chlorine disinfection is most effective at pH 6.5-7.5. At higher pH, hypochlorous acid converts to less effective hypochlorite ions.
  • Metal Solubility: Heavy metals like lead, copper, and iron are more soluble and potentially toxic at low pH levels, contributing to pipe corrosion.
  • Biological Activity: Most aquatic organisms thrive in pH ranges of 6.5-8.5. Outside this range, fish and other organisms experience physiological stress.
  • Coagulation Effectiveness: Water treatment coagulation processes require specific pH ranges for optimal particle removal, typically 5.5-7.5 for aluminum sulfate.

pH Measurement Methods

Electrochemical Method (Standard): A glass electrode pH sensor measures the electrical potential difference between the solution and a reference electrode. Modern pH sensors provide accuracy of ±0.01 pH units and can operate continuously in water systems.

Colorimetric Method: pH indicator dyes change color at specific pH values. While less accurate (±0.2-0.5 pH units), colorimetric methods provide quick field measurements and don't require electricity.

Ion-Selective Field Effect Transistor (ISFET): Solid-state sensors without glass membranes, offering durability and longer service life in harsh environments. Increasingly used in IoT water monitoring applications.

Quality Control for pH Measurement

  • Calibrate sensors daily using at least two buffer solutions (typically pH 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0)
  • Measure sample temperature; pH is temperature-dependent
  • Keep electrode membranes clean and hydrated
  • Replace electrodes when response time exceeds 60 seconds or slope efficiency drops below 95%
  • Verify measurements with secondary method quarterly

2.3 Dissolved Oxygen (DO)

📊 Parameter Overview

Definition: The amount of oxygen gas dissolved in water, essential for aquatic life and aerobic decomposition.

Units: mg/L or % saturation

Healthy Ecosystem Level: >6 mg/L

Significance of Dissolved Oxygen

Dissolved oxygen is one of the most important indicators of water quality and ecosystem health. Fish and other aquatic organisms require adequate oxygen to survive, with most species needing at least 5-6 mg/L. DO levels below 3 mg/L cause stress to most aquatic life, and levels below 1 mg/L (hypoxic conditions) can lead to fish kills.

DO levels are influenced by several factors:

  • Temperature: Cold water holds more oxygen than warm water. Water at 0°C can hold approximately 14.6 mg/L at sea level, while water at 30°C holds only 7.6 mg/L.
  • Atmospheric Pressure: Higher altitudes have lower atmospheric pressure, reducing oxygen solubility. DO saturation at 3,000 meters elevation is about 25% lower than at sea level.
  • Salinity: Saltwater holds less oxygen than freshwater. Seawater at typical salinity (35 ppt) holds about 20% less oxygen than freshwater at the same temperature.
  • Photosynthesis and Respiration: Aquatic plants and algae produce oxygen during daylight photosynthesis and consume oxygen at night through respiration, creating daily DO fluctuations.
  • Organic Pollution: Bacteria decomposing organic waste consume oxygen, potentially depleting DO levels and creating "dead zones" in polluted waters.

DO Measurement Technologies

Electrochemical (Clark Cell) Method: An oxygen-permeable membrane separates the water sample from an electrolyte solution. Oxygen diffuses through the membrane and is reduced at a cathode, producing a current proportional to oxygen concentration. This is the most common method for continuous monitoring.

Optical (Luminescence) Method: Based on the principle that oxygen quenches luminescence from certain dyes. A blue LED excites a luminescent dye, and the decay time of the luminescence is inversely related to oxygen concentration. Optical sensors have no membrane to maintain and are increasingly popular for long-term deployments.

Winkler Titration Method: A wet chemistry method considered the reference standard for DO measurement. Manganese sulfate and alkaline iodide reagents are added to the sample, fixing dissolved oxygen as a precipitate. After acidification, the precipitate releases iodine proportional to the original DO concentration, which is titrated with sodium thiosulfate. Accuracy: ±0.2 mg/L.

2.4 Turbidity

📊 Parameter Overview

Definition: Measure of water clarity; the degree to which light is scattered by suspended particles.

Units: Nephelometric Turbidity Units (NTU) or Formazin Nephelometric Units (FNU)

Drinking Water Standard: <1 NTU (WHO), <0.3 NTU after filtration (EPA)

Sources and Impacts of Turbidity

Turbidity in water can result from various suspended materials including:

  • Clay, silt, and fine organic particles
  • Algae and other microscopic organisms
  • Industrial wastes and sewage
  • Erosion from construction sites and agricultural runoff

High turbidity poses several problems for water quality:

  • Microbial Protection: Particles can harbor pathogens and shield them from disinfectants, reducing treatment effectiveness.
  • Aesthetic Concerns: Cloudy water is unpalatable and indicates potential contamination.
  • Ecosystem Impacts: High turbidity blocks sunlight, reducing photosynthesis and affecting aquatic food chains.
  • Treatment Challenges: Turbidity increases disinfection requirements and chemical usage in treatment plants.

Turbidity Measurement

Modern turbidimeters use nephelometric technology, measuring light scattered at 90 degrees from an incident beam. The WIA-ENE-018 standard requires turbidity sensors calibrated with formazin polymer standards or equivalent commercial standards. Continuous turbidity monitoring is essential in drinking water treatment to verify filtration effectiveness and detect source water changes.

2.5 Electrical Conductivity and Total Dissolved Solids

📊 Parameter Overview

Conductivity Definition: Measure of water's ability to conduct electrical current, indicating dissolved ionic substances.

Conductivity Units: microSiemens per centimeter (μS/cm) or milliSiemens per meter (mS/m)

TDS Definition: Total mass of dissolved inorganic and organic substances in water.

TDS Units: milligrams per liter (mg/L) or parts per million (ppm)

Relationship Between Conductivity and TDS

Electrical conductivity and TDS are closely related but not identical. Conductivity measures the water's ability to conduct electricity, which depends on the concentration and mobility of dissolved ions. TDS represents the actual mass of all dissolved substances, including both ionic and non-ionic compounds.

The approximate relationship is: TDS (mg/L) ≈ 0.5 to 0.9 × Conductivity (μS/cm)

The conversion factor varies depending on the ionic composition of the water. For natural waters, a factor of 0.67 is commonly used, while seawater uses approximately 0.55.

Water Quality Implications

TDS Range (mg/L) Classification Characteristics
<300 Excellent Low mineral content, soft water
300-600 Good Acceptable for drinking, moderate hardness
600-900 Fair Noticeable taste, higher hardness

>1,200 Unacceptable Not suitable for drinking

2.6 Heavy Metals Monitoring

Heavy metals are toxic elements that can accumulate in the environment and biological systems, posing serious health risks even at low concentrations. The WIA-ENE-018 standard emphasizes monitoring of priority heavy metals:

Lead (Pb)

Sources: Corrosion of lead pipes and solder, industrial discharge, atmospheric deposition
Health Effects: Neurological damage, especially in children; developmental delays; kidney damage; reproductive problems
WHO Guideline: 10 μg/L (0.010 mg/L)
EPA Action Level: 15 μg/L in drinking water

Lead contamination gained international attention following the Flint, Michigan water crisis (2014-2019), where changes in water chemistry caused lead leaching from pipes, exposing thousands of residents to elevated lead levels. This tragedy highlighted the critical importance of continuous water quality monitoring and proper corrosion control.

Mercury (Hg)

Sources: Industrial effluent, mining operations, coal combustion, natural geological sources
Health Effects: Neurological and kidney damage; bioaccumulates in fish
WHO Guideline: 6 μg/L (0.006 mg/L) for inorganic mercury
Methylmercury: More toxic organic form that accumulates in aquatic food chains

Cadmium (Cd)

Sources: Electroplating, battery manufacturing, mining, phosphate fertilizers
Health Effects: Kidney damage, bone fragility, carcinogenic
WHO Guideline: 3 μg/L (0.003 mg/L)

Arsenic (As)

Sources: Natural geological sources, mining, pesticides, wood preservatives
Health Effects: Skin lesions, cancer, cardiovascular disease
WHO Guideline: 10 μg/L (0.010 mg/L)

Arsenic contamination of groundwater affects millions of people worldwide, particularly in Bangladesh, India, Vietnam, and parts of the United States. The WIA-ENE-018 standard recommends regular arsenic monitoring for groundwater sources in geologically susceptible areas.

Heavy Metals Analysis Methods

Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS): Metal atoms in a flame or graphite furnace absorb light at characteristic wavelengths. Detection limits: 1-100 μg/L depending on the element.

Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS): Provides ultra-sensitive multi-element analysis with detection limits in the parts per trillion range. Preferred method for comprehensive heavy metals screening.

Electrochemical Methods: Anodic stripping voltammetry can detect trace metals (Pb, Cd, Cu, Zn) at μg/L levels using portable field instruments, enabling on-site testing.

2.7 Microbiological Parameters

Waterborne pathogens—bacteria, viruses, and parasites—cause millions of cases of illness annually. However, testing for every possible pathogen is impractical. Instead, water quality monitoring relies on indicator organisms that suggest fecal contamination and potential presence of pathogens.

Total Coliform Bacteria

Total coliforms are a group of bacteria commonly found in soil, vegetation, and the intestinal tracts of warm-blooded animals. Their presence in treated drinking water indicates treatment process failure or contamination in the distribution system.

Standard: Must be absent in 95% of drinking water samples

Escherichia coli (E. coli)

E. coli is a specific type of fecal coliform bacteria that indicates recent fecal contamination from warm-blooded animals. While most E. coli strains are harmless, their presence suggests possible presence of harmful pathogens.

Standard: Must be absent in all drinking water samples
Detection Time: 18-24 hours (culture methods) or 2-6 hours (rapid enzymatic methods)

Detection Methods

Membrane Filtration: Water sample is filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, which is then incubated on selective growth media. Coliform bacteria produce colored colonies that can be counted.

Most Probable Number (MPN): Statistical method based on growth in multiple tubes of selective broth. Results are reported as MPN per 100 mL.

Enzyme Substrate Tests: Modern rapid methods using fluorogenic or chromogenic substrates that produce color or fluorescence when metabolized by target bacteria. Results in 2-18 hours.

Molecular Methods: PCR and qPCR can detect specific pathogens or genes in hours with high sensitivity. Increasingly used for outbreak investigation and source tracking.

🔬 Emerging Concern: Antibiotic Resistant Bacteria

Antibiotic resistance is a growing global health threat. Water environments can serve as reservoirs for antibiotic-resistant bacteria and resistance genes. The WIA-ENE-018 framework is evolving to include monitoring protocols for antibiotic-resistant E. coli and other resistance indicators in water systems.

2.8 Nutrients: Nitrogen and Phosphorus

While nitrogen and phosphorus are essential nutrients for plant growth, excess levels in water bodies cause eutrophication—excessive algae growth that depletes oxygen and degrades water quality.

Nitrogen Forms

  • Ammonia (NH₃/NH₄⁺): Toxic to fish; indicates recent sewage contamination; measured by colorimetric or ion-selective electrode methods.
  • Nitrite (NO₂⁻): Intermediate oxidation state; indicates incomplete nitrification; toxic to fish and infants (methemoglobinemia).
  • Nitrate (NO₃⁻): Fully oxidized form; WHO guideline: 50 mg/L; EPA MCL: 10 mg/L (as nitrogen).
  • Total Nitrogen: Sum of all nitrogen forms; useful indicator of overall nutrient loading.

Phosphorus Forms

  • Orthophosphate (PO₄³⁻): Dissolved reactive form immediately available for algae uptake.
  • Total Phosphorus: Includes all dissolved and particulate forms; best indicator for eutrophication potential.

Target Levels to Prevent Eutrophication: Total Nitrogen <0.5 mg/L, Total Phosphorus <0.03 mg/L in lakes and reservoirs.

2.9 Water Quality Index (WQI)

Individual parameter measurements provide detailed information but can be overwhelming for non-technical stakeholders. The Water Quality Index (WQI) synthesizes multiple parameters into a single number that represents overall water quality.

WQI Calculation Methodology

The WIA-ENE-018 standard uses a weighted arithmetic WQI calculation:

  1. Select Parameters: Choose 6-10 key parameters relevant to the water use (drinking, recreation, aquatic life).
  2. Assign Weights: Each parameter receives a weight (w) from 1-5 based on its importance to water quality.
  3. Calculate Sub-Indices: Convert each parameter measurement to a 0-100 scale based on water quality standards.
  4. Compute WQI: WQI = Σ(w × sub-index) / Σw
WQI Value Water Quality Color Code
90-100 Excellent Green
70-89 Good Blue
50-69 Moderate Yellow
25-49 Poor Orange
0-24 Very Poor Red

💡 Best Practice

When implementing WQI monitoring, maintain consistency in parameter selection and weighting to enable meaningful trend analysis over time. Document any changes to WQI methodology for historical data interpretation.

2.10 Quality Assurance in Water Testing

Accurate, reliable water quality data requires rigorous quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) procedures throughout the testing process:

Sample Collection and Preservation

  • Use clean, appropriate containers (glass for organics, plastic for metals)
  • Collect samples from representative locations
  • Rinse containers with sample water before filling
  • Add preservatives promptly (acid for metals, refrigeration for microbiological samples)
  • Transport samples in coolers at 4°C
  • Analyze within holding time limits (varies by parameter)

Analytical Quality Control

  • Calibrate instruments daily with certified standards
  • Analyze blank samples to check for contamination
  • Run duplicate samples to assess precision (target: ±10% RSD)
  • Analyze matrix spikes to evaluate accuracy (target: 90-110% recovery)
  • Participate in proficiency testing programs
  • Maintain control charts to track instrument performance

2.11 Summary

Comprehensive water quality assessment requires monitoring multiple parameters that collectively characterize the physical, chemical, and biological properties of water. The WIA-ENE-018 standard provides a framework for systematic parameter selection, measurement, and interpretation based on specific water uses and regulatory requirements.

Modern sensor technologies enable continuous, real-time monitoring of many parameters, revolutionizing water quality management. However, laboratory analysis remains essential for parameters requiring sophisticated analytical techniques, particularly heavy metals and organic contaminants.

Rigorous quality assurance procedures ensure that water quality data is accurate, precise, and defensible—critical for protecting public health, complying with regulations, and supporting evidence-based decision-making.
