⚙️ Chapter 2: Robot Anatomy and Kinematics

"The beauty of a robot lies not in its appearance, but in the mathematics that govern its motion."

Understanding robot kinematics is essential for programming, troubleshooting, and optimizing industrial robot systems.

2.1 Mechanical Structure

An industrial robot's mechanical structure consists of interconnected rigid bodies (links) connected by joints that allow relative motion. This fundamental architecture enables the robot to position its end-effector (tool) anywhere within its workspace.

2.1.1 Links and Joints

Links are the rigid structural members of a robot. In a typical 6-axis articulated robot:

  • Link 0 (Base): Fixed to the ground or mounting surface
  • Link 1 (Lower Arm): Rotates about the base (J1)
  • Link 2 (Upper Arm): Connected to lower arm via shoulder joint (J2)
  • Link 3 (Forearm): Connected to upper arm via elbow joint (J3)
  • Links 4-6 (Wrist Assembly): Three intersecting axes providing tool orientation

Joints provide the degrees of freedom. Industrial robots primarily use two types:

Joint Type Symbol Motion Typical Range Applications
Revolute (R) θ Rotational -180° to +180° (typically) Most industrial robots
Prismatic (P) d Linear/Translational 0 to 2000mm (varies) Cartesian robots, SCARA Z-axis
Cylindrical (C) r, θ, z Combination Varies Specialized applications

2.1.2 Standard Robot Configurations

Articulated (RRR...R) - All Revolute Joints

The most common configuration, typically with 6 revolute joints (6R):

Configuration: 6R (RRRRRR)
Joints: J1 (base rotation), J2 (shoulder), J3 (elbow),
        J4 (wrist rotation), J5 (wrist bend), J6 (flange rotation)

Workspace: Near-spherical
Singularities: 3 (shoulder, elbow, wrist)
Payload Capacity: Highest (structural rigidity)

SCARA (RRPR) Configuration

Configuration: RRPR
Joints: J1 (base rotation), J2 (elbow rotation),
        J3 (vertical prismatic), J4 (wrist rotation)

Workspace: Cylindrical
Speed: Highest for pick-and-place
Rigidity: Excellent in vertical direction

Delta (Parallel Mechanism)

Delta robots use parallel kinematics where multiple kinematic chains connect the base to the end-effector. This provides high speed but limited workspace.

Configuration: 3 parallel RRR chains
Advantages:
  - Very high speed (200+ picks/min)
  - Low moving mass
  - High stiffness
Limitations:
  - Limited workspace (inverted cone)
  - Complex inverse kinematics

2.1.3 Coordinate Systems

Multiple coordinate systems are used in robot control:

  1. World Coordinate System (WCS): Fixed reference frame, typically aligned with the factory floor
  2. Robot Base Coordinate System (BCS): Origin at robot base, Z-axis vertical
  3. Tool Center Point (TCP): Origin at the functional point of the end-effector
  4. Workpiece Coordinate System: Defined relative to the part being manipulated
  5. Joint Space: Angles/positions of each joint (θ₁, θ₂, ..., θₙ)

2.2 Degrees of Freedom (DOF)

A robot's degrees of freedom determine its ability to position and orient its end-effector in 3D space.

2.2.1 Required DOF for General Tasks

In 3D space, an object has 6 degrees of freedom:

  • 3 Translational DOF: Position (X, Y, Z)
  • 3 Rotational DOF: Orientation (Roll, Pitch, Yaw) or (Rx, Ry, Rz)

Minimum DOF Requirements:

Task Type Required DOF Example Robot
Point-to-point movement (vertical approach) 3 (XYZ) Simple Cartesian
Planar manipulation 3 (X, Y, Rz) 3-axis SCARA
Full 3D positioning with simple orientation 4-5 4-axis SCARA or 5-axis articulated
Arbitrary position and orientation 6 6-axis articulated robot
Redundant (obstacle avoidance) 7+ 7-axis collaborative robots

2.2.2 Redundancy and Singularities

Kinematic Redundancy (DOF > 6):

Robots with more than 6 DOF are kinematically redundant, offering advantages:

  • Multiple joint configurations can achieve the same end-effector pose
  • Ability to avoid obstacles while maintaining end-effector position
  • Improved manipulability near singularities
  • Optimization of criteria like energy efficiency or joint limits

Example: KUKA LBR iiwa (7-axis cobot) can reach around obstacles that would block a 6-axis robot.

Singularities:

Singular configurations occur when a robot loses one or more degrees of freedom. At singularities:

  • Infinite joint velocities may be required for certain end-effector velocities
  • Control becomes unstable
  • The robot cannot move in certain directions

Common Singularities in 6-Axis Robots:

  1. Shoulder Singularity: Occurs when the wrist center lies on the J1 axis
  2. Elbow Singularity: Occurs when the arm is fully extended or retracted
  3. Wrist Singularity: Occurs when J4 and J6 axes align

Avoiding Singularities:

  • Path planning algorithms that detect and avoid singular regions
  • Joint limit monitoring to prevent approaching singular configurations
  • Redundant DOF allowing alternative configurations
  • Damped least-squares (DLS) methods in inverse kinematics

2.3 Forward Kinematics

Forward kinematics (FK) computes the position and orientation of the end-effector given the joint angles/positions. This is the "easy" problem with a unique solution.

2.3.1 Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) Parameters

The DH convention provides a systematic way to describe robot kinematics using 4 parameters per link:

Parameter Symbol Description
Link Length aᵢ Distance between Zᵢ and Zᵢ₊₁ along Xᵢ
Link Twist αᵢ Angle between Zᵢ and Zᵢ₊₁ about Xᵢ
Link Offset dᵢ Distance between Xᵢ₋₁ and Xᵢ along Zᵢ
Joint Angle θᵢ Angle between Xᵢ₋₁ and Xᵢ about Zᵢ

Transformation Matrix:

The transformation from frame i-1 to frame i is given by:

     ⎡ cos(θᵢ)  -sin(θᵢ)cos(αᵢ)   sin(θᵢ)sin(αᵢ)  aᵢcos(θᵢ) ⎤
Tᵢ = ⎢ sin(θᵢ)   cos(θᵢ)cos(αᵢ)  -cos(θᵢ)sin(αᵢ)  aᵢsin(θᵢ) ⎥
     ⎢    0          sin(αᵢ)           cos(αᵢ)          dᵢ     ⎥
     ⎣    0             0                  0             1      ⎦

The overall transformation from base to end-effector is:

T₀ⁿ = T₁ × T₂ × T₃ × ... × Tₙ

2.3.2 Example: 6-Axis Articulated Robot

Typical DH Parameters for ABB IRB 6700 Style Robot:

Joint i θᵢ (variable) dᵢ (mm) aᵢ (mm) αᵢ (degrees)
1 θ₁* 780 320 -90°
2 θ₂* 0 1280 0°
3 θ₃* 0 200 -90°
4 θ₄* 1142 0 90°
5 θ₅* 0 0 -90°
6 θ₆* 200 0 0°

*θᵢ are the joint variables that change during robot motion

2.4 Inverse Kinematics

Inverse kinematics (IK) computes the joint angles/positions required to achieve a desired end-effector pose. This is the "hard" problem that may have multiple solutions, no solution, or infinite solutions.

2.4.1 Analytical vs. Numerical Solutions

Analytical (Closed-Form) Solutions:

For robots with special geometric properties (e.g., 6R robots with spherical wrist), analytical solutions exist:

Advantages:

  • Fast computation (microseconds)
  • All solutions can be found
  • No convergence issues
  • Deterministic behavior

Disadvantages:

  • Only available for specific robot geometries
  • Complex mathematical derivation
  • Difficult to extend to redundant robots

Numerical (Iterative) Solutions:

Methods like Jacobian-based techniques or optimization:

Advantages:

  • Applicable to any robot geometry
  • Can handle redundancy
  • Can optimize additional criteria

Disadvantages:

  • Slower computation (milliseconds)
  • May not converge
  • May get stuck in local minima
  • Only finds one solution at a time

2.4.2 Multiple Solutions

A 6-axis robot typically has up to 8 possible IK solutions for a given end-effector pose. These correspond to different robot configurations:

Solution Shoulder Elbow Wrist Typical Use Case
1 Left Up Non-flip Default configuration
2 Left Up Flip Overhead work
3 Left Down Non-flip Reaching under obstacles
4 Left Down Flip Complex paths
5-8 Right Up/Down Flip/Non-flip Alternative approaches

2.4.3 Solution Selection Criteria

When multiple IK solutions exist, the controller must select one based on criteria such as:

  1. Minimum Joint Motion: Solution closest to current position (default for point-to-point moves)
  2. Obstacle Avoidance: Configuration that avoids collisions
  3. Joint Limit Margins: Solution farthest from joint limits
  4. Singularity Avoidance: Configuration with best manipulability
  5. Energy Efficiency: Minimize power consumption
  6. User Preference: Specified configuration (elbow-up, wrist-flip, etc.)

2.5 Workspace and Envelope

2.5.1 Reachable Workspace

The reachable workspace is the set of all points in 3D space that the robot's TCP can reach, considering only position (not orientation).

Factors Affecting Workspace:

  • Link lengths (longer links = larger workspace)
  • Joint ranges (limited by mechanical stops and software limits)
  • Robot mounting (floor, wall, ceiling, angle)
  • Obstacle geometry (safety barriers, fixtures)

Workspace Shapes by Robot Type:

Robot Type Workspace Shape Volume Formula
6-Axis Articulated Near-spherical with exclusion zone V ≈ (4/3)π(R³ - r³)
SCARA Cylindrical annulus V = πh(R² - r²)
Delta Inverted cone/sphere V ≈ (1/3)πr²h
Cartesian Rectangular prism V = L × W × H

Example: ABB IRB 6700-155/2.85

Reach: 2850 mm
Workspace Type: Spherical shell
Inner Dead Zone Radius: ~800 mm
Outer Radius: 2850 mm
Workspace Volume: ≈ 86 m³

Joint Ranges:
  J1: ±180° (full rotation)
  J2: -60° to +85°
  J3: -180° to +70°
  J4: ±300° (advanced version)
  J5: ±120°
  J6: ±360°

2.5.2 Dexterous Workspace

The dexterous workspace is the subset where the TCP can achieve any orientation. This is typically much smaller than the reachable workspace.

Applications Requiring Dexterous Workspace:

  • Complex assembly operations
  • Deburring with specific tool angles
  • Spray painting (maintaining angle to surface)
  • Arc welding (torch angle control)

2.6 Jacobian Matrix and Velocity Kinematics

The Jacobian matrix relates joint velocities to end-effector velocities, crucial for smooth motion and force control.

2.6.1 Definition

For a 6-DOF robot:

v = J(θ) × θ̇

where:
  v = [vₓ, vᵧ, vᵤ, ωₓ, ωᵧ, ωᵤ]ᵀ  (end-effector velocity: 3 linear + 3 angular)
  J(θ) = 6×6 Jacobian matrix (function of joint angles)
  θ̇ = [θ̇₁, θ̇₂, ..., θ̇₆]ᵀ  (joint velocities)

2.6.2 Applications

  1. Velocity Control: Convert desired Cartesian velocity to joint velocities
  2. Force Control: J^T relates joint torques to end-effector forces
  3. Singularity Detection: det(J) = 0 indicates singularity
  4. Manipulability Analysis: Measure of dexterity at a configuration

Manipulability Index:

w(θ) = √det(J(θ)J(θ)ᵀ)

High w: Robot is highly maneuverable
Low w: Near singularity, limited motion capability

2.7 Summary

This chapter covered the mathematical foundations of robot motion:

  • Robot anatomy consists of links connected by joints (revolute or prismatic)
  • DOF determines the robot's ability to position and orient its end-effector
  • Forward kinematics (FK) computes TCP pose from joint angles using DH parameters
  • Inverse kinematics (IK) computes joint angles from desired TCP pose - multiple solutions possible
  • Workspace defines the volume the robot can reach, affected by joint limits and geometry
  • Jacobian matrix relates joint and Cartesian velocities, enabling smooth motion control
  • WIA-ROB-003 defines standard formats for all kinematic computations

Key Takeaways:

  • Understanding kinematics is essential for robot programming and troubleshooting
  • Singularities must be avoided for stable control
  • IK solution selection significantly impacts cycle time and safety
  • Workspace analysis prevents collisions and optimizes robot placement
