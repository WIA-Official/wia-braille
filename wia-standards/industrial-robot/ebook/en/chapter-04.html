🛡️ Chapter 4: Safety Standards and Regulations

"Safety is not negotiable. Every robot installation must prioritize human wellbeing above productivity."

Understanding and implementing safety standards is a legal and ethical obligation for all industrial robot applications.

4.1 ISO 10218-1: Robots and Robotic Devices - Safety Requirements for Industrial Robots

ISO 10218-1:2011 specifies safety requirements for the design and construction of industrial robots.

4.1.1 Scope and Application

ISO 10218-1 applies to industrial robots used in manufacturing environments, covering:

  • Mechanical design and construction
  • Control systems and software
  • Protective devices and safety functions
  • Information for use (manuals, warnings, training)
  • Robot manufacturer responsibilities

Excluded:

  • Non-industrial robots (e.g., service robots, consumer products)
  • Robot application-specific hazards (covered by ISO 10218-2)
  • Transportation and handling of robots

4.1.2 Fundamental Safety Principles

Three-Step Method (ISO 12100):

  1. Inherent Safe Design: Eliminate hazards by design (e.g., limited force/speed)
  2. Safeguarding: Add protective measures (e.g., barriers, sensors)
  3. Information for Use: Warnings, training, PPE requirements

Safety-Related Control Performance:

Performance Level (PL) Category Probability of Dangerous Failure Typical Application
PLa B 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ /hour Low risk applications
PLb 1 3×10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁵ /hour Moderate risk
PLc 1,2 10⁻⁶ to 3×10⁻⁶ /hour Significant risk
PLd 2,3 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁶ /hour High risk - most industrial robots
PLe 3,4 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁷ /hour Very high risk

4.1.3 Mandatory Safety Functions

1. Emergency Stop (E-Stop)

Requirements per ISO 10218-1:

  • Availability: One E-stop on teach pendant, one on control cabinet minimum
  • Type: Category 0 (immediate power removal) or Category 1 (controlled stop then power removal)
  • Action: Mushroom-head button, red, yellow background
  • Reset: Manual reset required (button release alone insufficient)
  • Performance Level: PLd or higher
  • Response Time: <100 ms from actuation to motion stop

2. Safety-Rated Monitored Stop (STO - Safe Torque Off)

Removes power to motor drives in a safe, controlled manner:

  • Dual-channel monitoring
  • Diagnostic coverage >99%
  • Prevents unexpected startup
  • Used when maintenance personnel enter robot cell

3. Speed Monitoring

Safety-rated monitoring of robot TCP and joint speeds:

  • Automatic Mode: Maximum 250 mm/s TCP speed for collaborative operation
  • Manual Mode (T1): Maximum 250 mm/s
  • Manual High Speed (T2): Production speed, but only when cell is safeguarded

4. Position Monitoring

Safety-rated monitoring of robot position to prevent exceeding workspace limits:

  • Software limits (monitored by safety controller)
  • Hardware limits (mechanical stops as backup)
  • Axis-specific and Cartesian space limits

4.1.4 Operating Modes

Mode Speed Limit Access Purpose
Automatic Full speed No personnel in cell Production operation
Manual (T1) ≤250 mm/s Operator in cell with pendant Teaching, troubleshooting
Manual High Speed (T2) Full speed Operator in cell, safeguards active Program verification

Mode Selection Requirements:

  • Key switch or coded access
  • Clear mode indication (visual + audible)
  • Mode change requires deliberate action
  • Automatic mode requires safeguarding verification

4.1.5 Mechanical Design Requirements

Power and Force Limiting:

  • Maximum contact force for collaborative operation: 150 N (body), 65 N (head/face)
  • Clamping/crushing force limits in pinch points
  • Sharp edges and pinch points eliminated or guarded

Protective Stop Performance:

Parameter Requirement Verification Method
Stop Time (Category 0) ≤ specified value (robot-specific) Measurement at max speed
Stop Distance ≤ calculated safety distance High-speed camera measurement
Overrun ≤ 10% of stop distance Statistical analysis (10 tests min)

4.2 ISO 10218-2: Robot Systems and Integration

ISO 10218-2:2011 addresses safety requirements for robot system integration, covering the complete robotic cell including peripheral equipment.

4.2.1 Risk Assessment

Systematic Risk Assessment Process:

  1. Define Limits: Intended use, space limits, operator interaction
  2. Identify Hazards: Mechanical, electrical, thermal, noise, etc.
  3. Estimate Risk: Severity × probability for each hazard
  4. Evaluate Risk: Compare against acceptable risk threshold
  5. Reduce Risk: Implement safeguarding measures
  6. Verify: Confirm risk reduction effectiveness
  7. Document: Record all decisions and measures

Risk Estimation Formula (ISO 12100):

Risk = Severity × Probability × Frequency

Severity (S):
  S1 = Minor injury (first aid)
  S2 = Serious injury (medical treatment)
  S3 = Major injury (permanent disability)
  S4 = Fatal injury

Probability (P):
  P1 = Slight possibility
  P2 = Possible
  P3 = Probable
  P4 = Very likely

Frequency of Exposure (F):
  F1 = Rare (<1/month)
  F2 = Occasional (weekly)
  F3 = Frequent (daily)
  F4 = Continuous (hourly)

4.2.2 Safeguarding Methods

1. Physical Barriers (Safeguarding Devices)

Barrier Type Application Pros Cons
Fixed Guards Perimeter fencing Simple, reliable, low cost Limits access, maintenance difficulty
Interlocked Guards Access doors/gates Controlled access, automatic stop More complex, regular testing needed
Light Curtains Entry points, part loading Fast, no physical contact Requires clear line of sight, expensive
Laser Scanners Area protection, AGV paths Flexible zones, 2D/3D capable High cost, false triggers possible
Pressure Mats Floor-level detection Large area coverage, invisible Trip hazard, requires dry environment

2. Safety Distances

Minimum distance between hazard zone and safeguarding device:

S = K × T + C

where:
  S = minimum safety distance (mm)
  K = approach speed (typically 1600 mm/s for hand/arm, 2000 mm/s for whole body)
  T = total system stop time (s)
  C = intrusion distance (mm, depends on safeguarding device)

Example (Light Curtain):
  Stop time = 250 ms
  K = 1600 mm/s
  C = 850 mm (for 30mm resolution light curtain)
  S = 1600 × 0.250 + 850 = 1250 mm minimum

4.2.3 Collaborative Operation Modes

ISO 10218-2 defines four methods for safe human-robot collaboration:

1. Safety-Rated Monitored Stop (SMS)

  • Robot stops when operator enters collaborative workspace
  • Operator can work on part while robot is stopped
  • Robot resumes when operator exits (automatic or manual restart)
  • Typical Use: Part loading/unloading, inspection

2. Hand Guiding

  • Operator physically guides robot using hand-held device
  • Robot operates in reduced mode (≤250 mm/s)
  • Three-position enabling device required
  • Force/torque sensors detect operator input
  • Typical Use: Teaching, finishing operations, assembly

3. Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM)

  • Robot speed varies based on distance to operator
  • Maintains minimum protective separation distance
  • Robot slows or stops as operator approaches
  • 3D safety sensors (laser scanners, depth cameras) monitor workspace
  • Typical Use: Large parts, variable operator interaction

Protective Separation Distance Formula:

Sp = Sh + Sr + C + Zs + Zr

where:
  Sp = protective separation distance
  Sh = distance traveled by human during robot reaction time
  Sr = distance traveled by robot during stopping
  C = intrusion distance
  Zs = position measurement uncertainty
  Zr = robot position uncertainty

4. Power and Force Limiting (PFL)

  • Robot designed to limit force and power in case of contact
  • Contact detected via joint torque sensors or external sensors
  • Robot stops or retracts upon contact
  • Force limits: ≤150 N (body contact), ≤65 N (head/neck)
  • Typical Use: True collaborative work, close proximity tasks

Biomechanical Limits (ISO/TS 15066):

Body Region Transient Contact (N) Quasi-Static Contact (N)
Skull 130 65
Forehead 130 65
Face 65 35
Neck 150 65
Back/Shoulders 210 110
Chest 140 75
Abdomen 110 65
Upper Arms 150 80
Lower Arms 160 80
Hands/Fingers 140 60

4.3 ISO/TS 15066: Collaborative Robots

ISO/TS 15066:2016 provides specific guidance for collaborative robot applications, supplementing ISO 10218-1/2.

4.3.1 Collaborative Workspace

Definitions:

  • Collaborative Workspace: Safeguarded space where robot and human can perform tasks simultaneously
  • Collaborative Operation: State in which robot and operator work within collaborative workspace
  • Maximum Space: Volume swept by robot including tool and workpiece
  • Restricted Space: Portion of maximum space where collaborative operation occurs

4.3.2 Contact Force Measurement

Test Setup Requirements:

  • Biomechanical test objects simulating body parts
  • Force/pressure sensors meeting accuracy requirements
  • Worst-case scenarios (maximum speed, mass, geometry)
  • Multiple contact points and angles tested
  • Statistical validation (minimum 10 measurements per scenario)

Force Measurement Procedure:

  1. Identify all possible contact scenarios (clamping, impact, free contact)
  2. For each scenario, determine worst-case conditions
  3. Position appropriate test object
  4. Execute robot motion
  5. Record force/pressure measurements
  6. Verify measurements ≤ allowable limits (with safety margin)
  7. Document all results

4.3.3 Risk Reduction Strategies

Design Features for Collaborative Robots:

  • Rounded Edges: Radius ≥3 mm on all robot surfaces
  • Soft Surfaces: Compliant covers on links and joints
  • Low Inertia: Lightweight links reduce impact forces
  • Joint Torque Sensors: High-resolution monitoring (0.1 Nm resolution)
  • Redundant Safety: Dual-channel monitoring of critical functions
  • Visual Indicators: LED status lights showing robot mode/state
  • Acoustic Signals: Warning sounds before motion

4.5 Summary

This chapter covered industrial robot safety standards:

  • ISO 10218-1: Robot manufacturer requirements (design, safety functions)
  • ISO 10218-2: System integrator requirements (risk assessment, safeguarding)
  • ISO/TS 15066: Collaborative robot specific guidance (force limits, biomechanics)
  • Safety functions: E-stop, STO, speed/position monitoring, force limiting
  • Operating modes: Automatic, Manual (T1/T2)
  • Collaborative operation: SMS, hand guiding, SSM, PFL
  • WIA-ROB-003 safety data formats and compliance verification

Key Takeaways:

  • Safety is a legal requirement, not optional
  • Risk assessment must be performed for every robot installation
  • Multiple layers of safeguarding provide defense-in-depth
  • Collaborative robots require force/pressure testing and validation
  • Regular safety function testing and maintenance is mandatory
