👁️ Chapter 5: Sensors and Perception

"Sensors are the eyes, ears, and touch of robots. Without perception, automation cannot adapt to the real world."

Modern industrial robots rely on diverse sensor technologies to navigate complex environments, ensure quality, and enable flexible automation.

5.1 Position Encoders

Position encoders measure joint angles and linear positions with high precision, forming the foundation of robot control.

5.1.1 Absolute vs. Incremental Encoders

Type Operation Advantages Disadvantages Typical Use
Incremental Counts pulses from home position Low cost, high resolution, simple Requires homing, position lost on power-off Legacy robots, cost-sensitive applications
Absolute Unique code for each position No homing needed, position retained Higher cost, more complex Modern industrial robots, safety-critical

Encoder Technologies:

  • Optical Encoders: LED + photodetectors reading coded disk (most common)
    • Resolution: 10,000 to 4,000,000 counts per revolution
    • Accuracy: ±5 to ±20 arc-seconds
    • Typical brands: Heidenhain, Renishaw, Sick, Stegmann
  • Magnetic Encoders: Hall-effect sensors reading magnetic patterns
    • Resolution: 1,000 to 100,000 counts per revolution
    • Robust in harsh environments (dust, vibration)
    • Applications: Welding robots, dirty environments
  • Resolver: Electromagnetic analog position sensor
    • Extremely robust, high temperature capable
    • Infinite resolution (analog output)
    • Applications: Aerospace, military, high-reliability

5.1.2 Multi-Turn Encoders

For multi-revolution joints (e.g., J6 can rotate ±360° or more), multi-turn encoders track total rotations.

Technologies:

  • Mechanical Gearing: Multiple code disks geared together
  • Battery-Powered Counting: Electronics count turns, battery backup
  • Wiegand Sensor: Self-powered pulse generator, no battery
  • Energy Harvesting: Generates power from shaft rotation

WIA-ROB-003 Encoder Data Format:

{
  "sensor_type": "absolute_encoder",
  "joint_id": "J1",
  "position_raw": 2457638,  // encoder counts
  "position_degrees": 45.234,
  "resolution": 131072,  // 17-bit (counts per revolution)
  "accuracy_arcsec": 10,
  "multi_turn": true,
  "turns": 2,
  "total_position_degrees": 765.234,
  "temperature_c": 38.5,
  "status": "ok",
  "error_flags": []
}

5.2 Force-Torque Sensors

Force-torque (F/T) sensors measure forces and moments in multiple axes, enabling force control and contact detection.

5.2.1 Six-Axis F/T Sensors

Measurement Axes:

  • Fx, Fy, Fz: Forces in X, Y, Z directions
  • Tx, Ty, Tz: Torques about X, Y, Z axes

Key Specifications:

Parameter Typical Range Application Dependent
Force Range 100 N to 10,000 N Assembly: 100-500 N; Heavy industry: 1000-10000 N
Torque Range 10 Nm to 500 Nm Depends on tool size and operation
Resolution 0.01 N to 1 N Fine assembly needs high resolution
Accuracy ±0.5% to ±2% full scale Calibration and temperature compensation
Sampling Rate 1000 Hz to 7000 Hz High-speed operations need faster sampling
Protection Rating IP50 to IP67 Environment dependent

Sensing Technologies:

  • Strain Gauge: Most common, excellent accuracy, moderate cost
    • Wheatstone bridge configuration
    • Temperature compensation required
    • Examples: ATI Industrial Automation, Robotiq, OnRobot
  • Piezoelectric: High stiffness, fast response, limited low-frequency response
    • Best for dynamic force measurement
    • Not suitable for static force holding
  • Capacitive: High resolution, sensitive to electromagnetic interference
    • Used in research applications
    • Less common in industrial settings

5.2.2 Applications

1. Force-Controlled Assembly

Peg-in-Hole Insertion:
  - Measure contact forces during insertion
  - Adjust position based on force feedback
  - Detect successful insertion (force drop)
  - Typical force threshold: 20-50 N

Example WIA-ROB-003 Code:
{
  "command": "force_controlled_insertion",
  "target_position": [500, 200, 100, 0, 180, 0],
  "max_force_z": 50,  // N
  "max_force_xy": 10,  // N
  "compliance": {
    "x": 0.05,  // mm/N
    "y": 0.05,
    "z": 0.01
  },
  "insertion_depth": 50,  // mm
  "success_criteria": {
    "force_drop": 15,  // N
    "depth_achieved": 48  // mm
  }
}

2. Polishing and Deburring

  • Maintain constant contact force (typically 20-100 N)
  • Adapt to surface variations
  • Detect tool wear (force increase over time)
  • Typical control loop: 1-2 kHz

3. Collaborative Safety

  • Detect human contact (force spike above baseline)
  • Trigger protective stop (within 100 ms)
  • Differentiate intentional contact (hand guiding) from collision

5.2.3 Joint Torque Sensors

Integrated into robot joints for collision detection without external F/T sensor.

Advantages:

  • No wrist-mounted sensor (saves weight, space)
  • Can detect collisions anywhere on robot arm
  • Better for collaborative robots

Challenges:

  • Less accurate than dedicated F/T sensor
  • Affected by joint friction and dynamics
  • Requires careful calibration and modeling

5.3 Vision Systems

Vision sensors provide robots with visual perception for positioning, inspection, and guidance.

5.3.1 2D Vision Systems

Components:

  • Camera: Industrial area-scan or line-scan camera
    • Resolution: 0.3 MP (VGA) to 20+ MP
    • Frame rate: 30 fps to 1000+ fps
    • Sensor: CCD or CMOS
    • Interface: GigE Vision, USB3 Vision, Camera Link
  • Lens: Fixed focal length or telecentric
    • Focal length: 4 mm to 100 mm+
    • Working distance: 50 mm to 5000 mm+
    • Telecentric for dimensional measurement (eliminates perspective error)
  • Lighting: Critical for consistent imaging
    • Types: Ring light, backlight, dome, structured light
    • LED: Monochrome (red, blue, IR) or RGB
    • Strobe capability for motion freeze
  • Vision Processing: Dedicated vision computer or embedded
    • Software: Cognex VisionPro, Keyence CV-X, OpenCV, Halcon
    • Processing time: 10 ms to 1000 ms depending on complexity

Common Vision Tasks:

Task Algorithm Typical Accuracy Processing Time
Part Location Pattern matching, edge detection ±0.1 mm to ±0.5 mm 50-200 ms
Barcode Reading 1D/2D code decoding 100% decode rate (good contrast) 20-100 ms
Dimension Measurement Caliper, blob analysis ±0.01 mm (telecentric lens) 30-150 ms
Defect Detection Anomaly detection, classification 95-99.9% detection rate 100-500 ms
Color Verification RGB/HSV analysis ΔE < 3 (color difference) 20-80 ms

5.3.2 3D Vision Systems

Technologies:

1. Structured Light

  • Projects patterns onto object surface
  • Camera(s) capture distorted patterns
  • Triangulation computes 3D coordinates
  • Accuracy: 0.01 mm to 0.5 mm
  • Speed: 0.5 to 5 seconds per scan
  • Applications: Bin picking, volume measurement, weld seam finding

2. Stereovision

  • Two cameras at known separation (baseline)
  • Correspondence matching between images
  • Triangulation for depth calculation
  • Accuracy: 0.5 mm to 5 mm (distance-dependent)
  • Speed: 30-60 fps real-time
  • Applications: AGV navigation, obstacle avoidance, large part measurement

3. Time-of-Flight (ToF)

  • Measures time for light to return from object
  • Every pixel yields distance measurement
  • Accuracy: 1 mm to 10 mm
  • Speed: 30-60 fps
  • Range: 0.5 m to 10 m
  • Applications: Safety zones, volume scanning, bin picking

4. Laser Line Scanning

  • Projects laser line onto object
  • Camera views line profile at angle
  • Robot or object motion creates 3D scan
  • Accuracy: 0.05 mm to 0.5 mm
  • Speed: 1000-8000 profiles/second
  • Applications: Weld seam tracking, inspection, reverse engineering

5.4 Tactile and Proximity Sensors

5.4.1 Tactile Sensors

Tactile Array Sensors:

  • Grid of force-sensing elements (taxels)
  • Resolution: 10x10 to 64x64 taxels
  • Force range per taxel: 0.1 N to 100 N
  • Applications: Grasping control, surface exploration, delicate manipulation

Binary Contact Sensors:

  • Simple on/off detection of contact
  • Examples: Limit switches, photoelectric sensors at gripper
  • Applications: Part presence detection, gripper confirmation

5.4.2 Proximity Sensors

Technology Range Advantages Limitations
Inductive 0-40 mm Detects metal, rugged, low cost Short range, metal only
Capacitive 0-50 mm Detects any material, through barriers Affected by humidity, dust
Ultrasonic 30-8000 mm Long range, detects any material Slow response, affected by temperature/humidity
Laser Distance 20-10,000 mm Accurate, fast, long range Expensive, requires clean environment
Photoelectric 0-50,000 mm Very long range, multiple modes Requires line of sight, affected by reflectivity

5.4.3 Safety Sensors

Laser Scanners (Safety-Rated):

  • 2D/3D area monitoring
  • Multiple safety zones with different actions
  • Range: up to 70 meters
  • Response time: <30 ms
  • Safety rating: PLd or PLe (IEC 62061)
  • Examples: SICK S3000, S300, microScan3, Keyence SZ-V series

Safety Light Curtains:

  • Infrared transmitter/receiver pairs
  • Resolution: 14 mm to 40 mm
  • Height: 150 mm to 3000 mm
  • Range: up to 70 meters
  • Muting capability for material passage
  • Applications: Perimeter guarding, machine access points

5.5 Summary

This chapter covered industrial robot sensor systems:

  • Position encoders: Absolute/incremental, optical/magnetic, multi-turn
  • Force-torque sensors: Six-axis F/T sensors, joint torque sensors, force control applications
  • Vision systems: 2D cameras, 3D scanning, vision-guided robotics
  • Tactile and proximity sensors: Contact detection, non-contact ranging
  • Safety sensors: Laser scanners, light curtains for safeguarding
  • WIA-ROB-003 sensor data formats for standardized integration

Key Takeaways:

  • Sensor selection depends on application requirements (accuracy, speed, environment)
  • Vision systems enable flexible automation but require careful lighting and calibration
  • Force control enables robots to adapt to variations and perform complex assembly
  • Safety-rated sensors are mandatory for collaborative and human-proximate applications
