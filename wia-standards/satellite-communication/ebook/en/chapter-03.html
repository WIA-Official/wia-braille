📻 Frequency Bands and Allocation

3.1 Satellite Frequency Overview

Satellite communication uses radio waves primarily in microwave range (1-40 GHz). Frequency selection critically impacts antenna size, bandwidth, weather sensitivity, and system cost.

Tradeoff: Lower frequencies resist weather but require large antennas and offer narrow bandwidth. Higher frequencies enable small antennas and wide bandwidth but suffer rain fade.

3.2 C-band (4-8 GHz)

Traditional satellite band with "compromise" characteristics balancing stability and efficiency.

Advantages:

  • Weather-resistant: minimal rain/snow impact
  • High reliability: 99.9%+ availability
  • Wide coverage: broad beam patterns

Disadvantages:

  • Large antennas: 2.4-3.7m dishes required
  • Terrestrial interference: 5G overlap conflicts
  • Limited bandwidth vs. higher frequencies

Applications:

  • TV broadcast backhaul
  • Intercontinental telecommunications
  • Maritime, oil platform communications
C-band Crisis: USA and India reallocating 3.7-3.98 GHz to 5G mid-band, forcing satellite operators to migrate with billions in compensation.

3.3 Ku-band (12-18 GHz)

Most widely used band since 1980s, standard for DTH broadcasting and VSAT networks.

Advantages:

  • Small antennas: 45-60cm consumer, 1.2-1.8m enterprise
  • Wide bandwidth: 2x capacity vs. C-band
  • High satellite EIRP enables small receivers
  • Spot beams for frequency reuse

Disadvantages:

  • Rain fade: 10-20 dB attenuation in heavy rain
  • Snow accumulation blocks signals
  • Precise antenna alignment required

Applications:

  • DTH satellite TV (DirecTV, SKY)
  • VSAT networks (bank ATMs, retail POS)
  • In-flight WiFi services

3.4 Ka-band (27-40 GHz)

Next-generation HTS frequency enabling multi-Gbps satellite capacity. Used by Starlink, Viasat, HughesNet.

Advantages:

  • Massive bandwidth: multi-GHz spectrum available
  • Tiny antennas: 30-60cm for high-speed (Starlink Dishy)
  • Frequency reuse: narrow spot beams enable multi-use
  • High capacity: hundreds of Gbps per satellite
  • Low congestion: underutilized spectrum

Disadvantages:

  • Severe rain fade: 30+ dB attenuation in downpours
  • Atmospheric absorption by oxygen/water vapor
  • High power consumption to compensate losses
  • Complex technology: phased arrays required

Rain Fade Mitigation:

  • Adaptive Coding Modulation (ACM): dynamically adjust modulation
  • Site diversity: multiple ground stations avoid localized rain
  • Power control: increase transmission during rain
  • Frequency diversity: Ka + Ku dual-band auto-switching
Ka-band Future: Only band achieving Tbps-class throughput. Expected to carry 80%+ satellite internet traffic by 2030s. LEO satellite diversity helps overcome rain fade.

3.5 ITU Frequency Coordination

International Telecommunication Union (UN agency) manages global frequency and orbital resources, preventing interference.

ITU Roles:

  • Frequency Allocation Table: defines band usage worldwide
  • Orbital Slot Coordination: maintains 2-3° GEO satellite spacing
  • Interference Resolution: arbitrates cross-border disputes
  • WRC (World Radiocommunication Conference): policy meetings every 3-4 years

Service Classifications:

  • FSS (Fixed-Satellite Service): Point-to-point (VSAT, relay)
  • BSS (Broadcasting-Satellite Service): DTH TV broadcasting
  • MSS (Mobile-Satellite Service): Sat phones, aviation/maritime

Coordination Process:

  1. Advance Publication (7 years before launch)
  2. Coordination Request (5 years before)
  3. Notification (3 years before)
  4. Registration (after launch)
First Come First Served: GEO slots allocated by priority. However, "paper satellites" (reserved without actual launch) led to ITU's 7-year use-it-or-lose-it rule.

3.6 5G and C-band Sharing

Recent 5G deployment using C-band lower portion (3.7-3.98 GHz) created serious interference with satellite downlinks. US FCC reallocated spectrum, paying $12B compensation to satellite operators. Korea also allocated 3.7-3.8 GHz to 5G, requiring satellite receivers to migrate higher or implement advanced filtering.

3.7 Conclusion

Satellite frequencies span L, S, C, X, Ku, K, Ka, V bands, each with distinct propagation, antenna size, bandwidth, and weather characteristics. Traditional C-band is stable but faces 5G competition. Ku-band dominates DTH broadcasting despite rain sensitivity. Ka-band is critical for next-gen HTS, offering massive bandwidth but severe rain fade requiring advanced mitigation. ITU manages international allocation through first-come-first-served principles with use-it-or-lose-it enforcement. Next chapter examines ground infrastructure: VSAT, gateways, and antenna systems.
